Question 1

A newly certified MLS begins reporting high-complexity coagulation results on February 3. On September 15 of the same year, the laboratory replaces its clot-based analyzer with a new instrument that uses a different detection method. Which competency assessment schedule meets the CLIA minimum for this scientist?

  1. Semiannually in year one, then annually, plus once before reporting on the new analyzer
  2. Assess at six months and one year; training records cover the new analyzer
  3. Assess once at one year from February 3, covering the new analyzer at that visit
  4. Assess before the analyzer change, then annually from September 15

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. High-complexity testing personnel are assessed at least semiannually in their first year of testing, then annually, and must be reassessed before reporting patient results whenever the methodology or instrumentation changes.
  • B. Tempting because training on a new instrument is documented, but training is not competency assessment. CLIA requires the individual's performance to be reevaluated on the new method before patient results are reported.
  • C. Tempting because annual assessment is the usual rule, but the first year requires semiannual assessment, and a new method requires reassessment before results are reported, not months later.
  • D. Tempting because it resets the cycle at the method change, but the first-year semiannual schedule still runs from when the scientist began testing patient specimens, so one assessment before September is not enough.

Under 42 CFR 493.1451, the technical supervisor evaluates and documents the competency of each person doing high-complexity testing at least semiannually during the first year the person tests patient specimens, then at least annually. So a new scientist is assessed twice in the first year before moving to the annual cycle. A change in test methodology or instrumentation triggers a reevaluation before the person reports patient results on it. Each assessment uses the six CLIA elements: direct observation of routine testing, monitoring the recording and reporting of results, review of worksheets, QC, PT and maintenance records, direct observation of maintenance and function checks, testing previously analyzed or blind samples, and assessment of problem-solving skills.

Sources: 42 CFR Part 493 (CLIA regulations)

Question 2

A patient in septic shock has a mean arterial pressure of 55 mm Hg on vasopressors and cool, mottled hands. A fingerstick glucose on the unit's meter reads 64 mg/dL (3.6 mmol/L), and both levels of meter QC passed this morning. The meter's labeling states that results may be inaccurate in severely hypotensive patients or patients in shock. The nurse calls the laboratory for guidance. What should the laboratory scientist advise NEXT under a typical POCT policy?

  1. Accept the meter result, because QC passed today and the reading is above the critical limit
  2. Repeat the fingerstick on the other hand after warming it, then report the higher value
  3. Run a third level of meter QC and repeat the fingerstick if it is in range
  4. Confirm with a venous or arterial blood specimen sent for plasma glucose on the core laboratory analyzer

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because passing QC shows the meter and strips work, but QC cannot detect a patient-specific error such as poor capillary perfusion in shock.
  • B. Tempting because warming improves capillary flow, but the limitation is the patient's poor perfusion, which persists, and choosing the higher of two results is not a valid way to resolve a discrepancy.
  • C. Tempting as extra assurance, but the problem is not analytic. Additional QC cannot correct a specimen drawn from a poorly perfused capillary bed.
  • D. Correct. The patient falls in a population for which the meter's labeling warns of inaccurate results, so the result should be confirmed by the laboratory method on a venous or arterial specimen.

Bedside glucose meters were largely cleared on data from healthy subjects. FDA asks that their labeling warn that results may be inaccurate in severe hypotension, shock, dehydration, hypoxia and hyperglycemic hyperosmolar states. In shock, peripheral vasoconstriction slows capillary flow and can make fingerstick glucose differ from central blood. Quality control checks the meter and strips, not the patient's circulation, so a passing QC run does not validate this result. Most POCT policies send a venous or arterial specimen to the core laboratory when the patient falls outside the meter's labeled use or the result does not fit the clinical picture.

Sources: CDC MMWR: Good Laboratory Practices for Waived Testing Sites (2005)

Question 3

A hospital laboratory reviews its Levey-Jennings charts for serum magnesium at the end of the month. For the first 10 days, points on both control levels scattered evenly around the mean. A new reagent lot was placed in use on day 11. From day 11 to day 20, every point on both levels fell between +0.4 SD and +0.9 SD; none exceeded 1 SD. Which explanation BEST fits this pattern?

  1. A trend from a slowly failing photometer lamp
  2. Evaporation from the control vials over the month
  3. A shift caused by the new reagent lot
  4. Random error from imprecise sampling that started after day 10

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A failing lamp gives a trend: points drift steadily further from the mean over many days. This pattern jumped to a new level on one day and then stayed there.
  • B. Evaporation concentrates the control gradually and produces an upward drift in each vial, not a step change on the day a reagent lot changed.
  • C. Correct. Ten consecutive results on the same side of the mean on both levels, starting abruptly with the new reagent lot, is a 10x violation showing a systematic shift.
  • D. Random error widens the scatter on both sides of the mean and shows up as 1-3s or R-4s violations. Here the scatter stayed tight and simply moved upward.

On a Levey-Jennings chart a shift is an abrupt change to a new mean that then holds, while a trend is a gradual, progressive drift. Ten consecutive points on one side of the mean break the 10x rule even when every point is within 1 SD, and the rule is applied across runs and across levels. An abrupt shift that starts with a new reagent lot points to that lot or to the calibration done with it. CLIA calls for calibration verification when controls show an unusual shift, and lot-change evaluation before a new lot is used is meant to catch this before patient results are affected.

Sources: Westgard: "Westgard Rules" and Multirules; CLIA Standard: Calibration and Calibration Verification Procedures, 42 CFR 493.1255

Question 4

During a POCT audit on a medical-surgical unit, the coordinator observes several glucose testing practices. The unit's professional-grade meters are shared between patients. Which observation requires correction?

  1. Single-use, auto-disabling lancets are discarded into the sharps container at the bedside
  2. A reusable lancing pen is used for several patients, with a fresh lancet loaded each time
  3. Each meter is cleaned and disinfected after each patient with the wipe its manufacturer lists
  4. A patient on contact precautions has a meter assigned to the room for the whole stay

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting to flag as wasteful, but single-use auto-disabling devices prevent reuse, and immediate disposal at the point of use is the safe practice.
  • B. Correct. Fingerstick devices are not to be used on more than one person, even when they are reusable for one individual, because the pen body can carry blood between patients. Single-use, auto-disabling lancets are recommended for assisted monitoring.
  • C. Tempting to flag because disinfectant wipes can damage some meters, but cleaning and disinfecting after every use per the manufacturer's instructions is exactly the recommended practice for a shared meter.
  • D. Tempting to flag because it takes a meter out of circulation, but dedicating a meter to one patient whenever possible is recommended, with cleaning and disinfection before it is reassigned.

CDC warns that unsafe practices in assisted glucose monitoring, such as shared fingerstick devices and meters, have spread hepatitis B and C between patients, notably in long-term care facilities. CDC advises that fingerstick devices are used on one person only, that single-use auto-disabling lancets are used where staff perform the testing, that meters are dedicated to one patient when possible, and that any shared meter is a professional-grade device cleaned and disinfected after every use according to its manufacturer. If the manufacturer gives no cleaning method, the meter should not be shared. The POCT coordinator documents the finding and retrains the staff involved.

Sources: CDC Injection Safety: Considerations for Blood Glucose Monitoring and Insulin Administration (2024)

Question 5

In a reference laboratory, a 4 L bottle of concentrated formic acid breaks inside a walk-in reagent room, and a technologist cleans up the spill before leaving the room. The technologist reports no symptoms. Under the OSHA laboratory standard, what must the employer offer the technologist?

  1. A medical consultation at no cost, without loss of pay, to decide whether an examination is needed
  2. A consultation after the chemical hygiene officer measures air levels in the room
  3. An examination through the employee's own health insurance
  4. A medical examination later, if symptoms of exposure develop

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. After a spill, leak or similar event likely to cause a hazardous exposure, the affected employee is offered a medical consultation, at no cost and without loss of pay, to decide whether an examination is needed.
  • B. Tempting because exposure monitoring guides decisions, but the consultation follows the event itself; monitoring results above the action level are a separate trigger for medical surveillance.
  • C. Tempting because the examination is medical care, but the laboratory standard requires consultations and examinations at no cost to the employee and without loss of pay.
  • D. Tempting because symptoms do trigger an examination, but a spill likely to have caused a hazardous exposure triggers a medical consultation on its own, without waiting for symptoms.

29 CFR 1910.1450, the OSHA laboratory standard, requires a written chemical hygiene plan, reviewed at least annually, and gives employees access to medical attention in three situations: when they develop signs or symptoms of exposure, medical surveillance when exposure monitoring shows levels routinely above the action level (or the PEL) for a substance whose own OSHA standard requires it, and when a spill, leak, explosion or similar event is likely to have caused a hazardous exposure. In the last case the employee is offered a medical consultation to decide whether an examination is needed. The services are provided by or under a licensed physician, at no cost and without loss of pay.

Sources: OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1450 (laboratory standard)

Question 6

A reference laboratory's send-out bench has four packages to prepare for air transport today. Infection has been considered for each patient, and none of the materials has been inactivated. Which one can be shipped as UN 3373, Biological substance, Category B?

  1. Serum from a patient under investigation for Lassa fever
  2. A Coccidioides immitis mold culture on a slant, for confirmation
  3. EDTA blood from a patient with suspected dengue, for PCR
  4. A Shiga toxin-producing E. coli O157 isolate, for serotyping

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Serum looks like an ordinary patient specimen, but Lassa virus is listed as Category A in any form, not just in culture, so this goes as UN 2814.
  • B. A mold culture can look like routine reference work, but Coccidioides immitis is a listed Category A agent when shipped as a culture, so it travels as UN 2814.
  • C. Correct. Dengue virus appears on the indicative list as Category A in culture form; a patient specimen collected for diagnosis is not a culture and goes as Category B unless professional judgment of the case says otherwise.
  • D. Stool sent for testing for this organism would be Category B, which is why this tempts, but a verotoxigenic E. coli culture is on the Category A list.

Two questions decide the category: is the agent on the Category A indicative list, and is it listed in any form or as cultures only? Viral hemorrhagic fever agents such as Lassa, Ebola and Marburg are Category A in any form, including a patient's serum. Many bacteria, fungi and viruses, including verotoxigenic E. coli, Coccidioides immitis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, HIV, hepatitis B and dengue virus, are Category A only as cultures, meaning material in which the agent has been intentionally propagated. A diagnostic blood specimen from a patient with suspected dengue is a patient specimen, so it is shipped as UN 3373 in triple packaging with the Category B diamond, unless the history or local conditions lead the shipper to judge it Category A.

Sources: CDC/NIH BMBL, 6th ed. (2020); 49 CFR 173.199 (Category B infectious substances); WHO Laboratory Biosafety Manual, 4th ed. (2020)

Question 7

A reference laboratory's creatinine level 1 control has a mean of 1.00 mg/dL (88 µmol/L) and an SD of 0.04 mg/dL. The laboratory uses 1-3s, 2-2s, R-4s, 4-1s and 10x as rejection rules and checks every one of them on every run, without waiting for a 1-2s warning. Level 1 results, oldest to newest: Run 1: 0.98 mg/dL (87 µmol/L) Run 2: 1.07 mg/dL (95 µmol/L) Run 3: 1.05 mg/dL (93 µmol/L) Run 4: 1.06 mg/dL (94 µmol/L) Run 5 (today): 1.05 mg/dL (93 µmol/L) Level 2 has been within 1 SD in every run. What is the BEST action for today's run?

  1. Reject the run under the 4-1s rule and look for a systematic error
  2. Accept the run, because no level 1 result exceeds 2 SD
  3. Reject the run under 10x and recalibrate before the next run
  4. Accept the run and chart a trend for review at month end

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Runs 2 to 5 are +1.75, +1.25, +1.50 and +1.25 SD: four consecutive results beyond +1 SD on the same side. That breaks 4-1s, which detects systematic error.
  • B. The largest deviation is +1.75 SD, so no 2 SD limit is crossed, but 4-1s is a rejection rule of its own. With rules applied to every run, it does not need a 1-2s trigger first.
  • C. 10x needs ten consecutive results on one side of the mean. Run 1 is below the mean, so there are only four in a row above it.
  • D. Noting the pattern is reasonable, but four consecutive results beyond 1 SD on one side already meet a rejection rule, so the run cannot be accepted and deferred.

Each z-score is (result - 1.00) / 0.04: run 1 -0.5, run 2 +1.75, run 3 +1.25, run 4 +1.50, run 5 +1.25. The last four are all beyond +1 SD on the same side, which is the 4-1s rule. Westgard notes that in current practice 4-1s and 10x can be applied without a preceding 1-2s warning, and that 4-1s may look back over earlier runs or across levels. A persistent small shift like this points to systematic error, such as calibration drift, a reagent problem or a change in the control lot. The laboratory then corrects the cause and evaluates patient results since the last acceptable run.

Sources: Westgard: "Westgard Rules" and Multirules; 42 CFR Part 493 (CLIA regulations)

Question 8

Night shift in a hospital microbiology laboratory that serves as a sentinel laboratory. A blood culture from a patient with fever after drinking unpasteurized goat milk grows tiny gram-negative coccobacilli after 3 days. The isolate is oxidase positive and urease positive within 2 hours, and the sentinel rule-out protocol cannot exclude Brucella. The isolate, on a sealed agar slant, is to be sent by courier to the state public health (reference) laboratory. Which UN number and proper shipping name apply to this package?

  1. UN 2814, Infectious substance, affecting humans
  2. UN 2900, Infectious substance, affecting animals
  3. UN 3373, Biological substance, Category B
  4. UN 3291, Regulated medical waste, not otherwise specified

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Brucella species are on the indicative list of Category A agents when they are cultures, and a culture suspected of being Category A is shipped as UN 2814 in UN-specification triple packaging with a Division 6.2 label.
  • B. Brucellosis is a zoonosis, which makes this tempting, but UN 2900 is for agents that cause disease in animals only. An agent that infects humans, or humans and animals, takes UN 2814.
  • C. Tempting because most clinical material leaves the laboratory as Category B, and the original blood specimen could have. The isolate is now a propagated culture of a listed agent, which moves it into Category A.
  • D. UN 3291 covers medical or clinical waste being sent for disposal. A live isolate sent for confirmatory identification is an infectious substance for testing, not waste.

Classification turns on the form of the material as well as the organism. The WHO indicative list of Category A agents names Brucella abortus, B. melitensis and B. suis as Category A when shipped as cultures; a patient's blood or serum sent for Brucella testing would usually travel as Category B. Once a sentinel laboratory has grown an isolate and cannot rule Brucella out, it refers the culture to the reference laboratory of the Laboratory Response Network, and when there is doubt the guidance is to assign Category A. The package goes as UN 2814, Infectious substance, affecting humans, in UN-specification triple packaging that passes drop, water-spray and 95 kPa tests, with a Division 6.2 label, a list of contents near the secondary packaging, a shipping paper with emergency response information, and the technical name, here suspected Brucella, in parentheses on the transport document.

Sources: CDC/NIH BMBL, 6th ed. (2020); 49 CFR 173.199 (Category B infectious substances); CDC Laboratory Response Network

Question 9

Specimen processing receives a lavender-top tube for a CBC from a medical floor. The tube label reads the patient's name and medical record number, both matching the electronic order. The date of birth printed on the tube label differs from the date of birth on the order and in the patient's record. The nurse who drew the specimen is on the unit and offers to come down and correct the label. What is the BEST action?

  1. Reject the specimen, document the reason, and request a correctly labeled recollection
  2. Test the specimen and report the CBC because the medical record number matches
  3. Correct the date of birth in the laboratory information system to match the label
  4. Let the nurse correct the date of birth on the label and then test the specimen

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Two identifiers must agree between the label and the order. A mismatch makes a replaceable specimen unacceptable, so it is rejected, the reason is documented and the unit is asked to recollect.
  • B. A matching medical record number is tempting, but a second identifier that disagrees means the identity of the specimen is in doubt. A CBC on a living inpatient can be recollected.
  • C. Changing the patient record to fit a label reverses the logic of identification and could attach this blood to the wrong person's chart.
  • D. Relabeling after the fact cannot prove which patient the blood came from; the mismatch may mean a label from another patient's record was used. Most laboratories allow relabeling only for irreplaceable specimens, under a documented procedure.

Specimen identification depends on at least two person-specific identifiers that match between the patient, the label and the order; room numbers do not count. The Joint Commission requires two identifiers and labeling in the presence of the patient, and CLIA requires the laboratory to have written criteria for specimen labeling and for accepting or rejecting specimens. When a replaceable specimen has a discrepant identifier, the safe action is rejection with documentation and a recollection. Wrong-blood-in-tube errors are dangerous because results look plausible and can follow the patient into transfusion or treatment decisions.

Sources: 42 CFR Part 493 (CLIA regulations); Joint Commission Performance Goals (2026)

Question 10

An outpatient clinic laboratory performs a CLIA-waived visual urine hCG cassette test. Each cassette has an internal control line. A new shipment arrives with a new lot number, and a medical assistant who has completed training starts on the testing bench the same day. Which practice BEST follows good laboratory practice for waived testing before patient results are reported?

  1. Run positive and negative external controls on the new lot, performed by the newly trained operator
  2. Test two patient urines on both the old and new lots and compare the lines
  3. Rely on each cassette's internal control line, because it checks the device with each test
  4. Run external controls at the next monthly QC date already on the clinic schedule

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. CDC recommends external controls at least with each new shipment, each new lot number and each new operator before patient testing, in addition to the internal controls the insert requires.
  • B. Tempting as a lot-to-lot check, but patient urines of unknown hCG status cannot show that the new lot detects a positive. External controls of known reactivity are the recommended check.
  • C. Tempting because the internal control is built in, but it does not check the antibody reagents against known positive and negative samples. External controls are recommended with each new lot and shipment and by each new operator.
  • D. Tempting because a schedule exists, but waiting a month would release results on an unchecked lot and from an operator whose performance has not been checked with known material.

The only CLIA requirement for a certificate-of-waiver site is to follow the current manufacturer's instructions, but surveys found many waived sites skipping QC, using expired kits and keeping poor records. CDC's good laboratory practices recommend running internal controls as often as the insert specifies and external controls at least with each new shipment, each new lot number and each new operator, before patient results are reported. Results, lot numbers, expiration dates and corrective actions are documented, and patient testing stops when controls fail until the problem is resolved.

Sources: CDC MMWR: Good Laboratory Practices for Waived Testing Sites (2005)
