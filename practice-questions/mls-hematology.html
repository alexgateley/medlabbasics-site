Question 1

During the night shift, a slightly cloudy CSF is diluted 1:10 with a diluent that lyses red cells and stains white cell nuclei. Both sides of an improved Neubauer hemocytometer are charged, and all nine large (1 mm²) squares are counted on each side, at a chamber depth of 0.1 mm. Side 1 has 120 white cells and side 2 has 132 white cells. What is the CSF white cell count?

  1. 140/µL (140 × 10⁶/L)
  2. 1,260/µL (1,260 × 10⁶/L)
  3. 12,600/µL (12.6 × 10⁹/L)
  4. 1,400/µL (1.4 × 10⁹/L)

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. This leaves out the 1:10 dilution: 126 ÷ 0.9 µL = 140/µL is the count in the diluted fluid, not in the patient's CSF.
  • B. This applies the dilution (126 × 10) but leaves out the volume; nine 1 mm² squares at 0.1 mm depth hold 0.9 µL, not 1 µL, so the count must still be divided by 0.9.
  • C. This divides by the depth alone (126 × 10 ÷ 0.1), as if one square had been counted; the nine squares counted make the area 9 mm², so the volume is 0.9 µL.
  • D. Correct. Average cells = (120 + 132) ÷ 2 = 126; volume counted = 9 mm² × 0.1 mm = 0.9 µL; count = 126 × 10 ÷ 0.9 = 1,400/µL.

Every hemocytometer count uses the same relation: cells per µL = cells counted × dilution factor ÷ volume counted (µL), where volume = area counted (mm²) × depth (0.1 mm). Counting both sides and averaging checks the charge; a large difference between sides means the chamber is refilled. Here the average is 126 cells in 0.9 µL of a 1:10 dilution, so the CSF holds 1,400 white cells per µL against a reference of 0 to 5/µL. A count this far above normal is reported promptly, called if the laboratory's critical list includes it, and followed by a cytospin differential.

Sources: ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026); Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.

Question 2

An outpatient clinic laboratory reports a platelet count of 38 × 10³/µL (38 × 10⁹/L) on an EDTA sample from a patient with no history of thrombocytopenia. The analyzer flags platelet clumps, and the smear shows large platelet aggregates at the feathered edge. A second sample is collected in a 3.2% sodium citrate tube (9 parts blood to 1 part anticoagulant) and gives a platelet count of 186 × 10³/µL (186 × 10⁹/L) with no clumps on its smear. Which platelet count should be reported?

  1. 186 × 10³/µL, the measured citrate count
  2. 205 × 10³/µL (205 × 10⁹/L)
  3. 169 × 10³/µL, after dividing by 1.1
  4. 112 × 10³/µL, the mean of the EDTA and citrate counts

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. This is the measured citrate count, but it is about 10% low because one part liquid anticoagulant dilutes nine parts blood; reporting it without correction understates the platelets.
  • B. Correct. The citrate count is multiplied by 1.1 to correct for the 9:1 dilution by liquid anticoagulant: 186 × 1.1 = 204.6, reported as 205 × 10³/µL, with a comment that the count is from citrated blood.
  • C. This divides by 1.1 instead of multiplying; the liquid citrate dilutes the blood, so the measured count is lower than the true count and the correction must raise it.
  • D. Averaging mixes a falsely low, clumped EDTA count with a valid one; once clumping is confirmed, the EDTA platelet count and platelet indices are not reported at all.

EDTA-dependent pseudothrombocytopenia is an in vitro artifact: antibodies, mostly IgG, bind cryptic epitopes on glycoprotein IIb/IIIa that are exposed when EDTA chelates calcium, and the platelets clump. Impedance counters read the clumps as large platelets or even small lymphocytes, so the platelet count falls and the WBC can rise. The fix is smear confirmation, then a count by fluorescence or in another anticoagulant. Sodium citrate is liquid and dilutes the sample nine to ten, so the count is multiplied by 1.1. Citrate results are reliable for a few hours, and if citrate also clumps, magnesium sulfate or a manual count is used.

Sources: Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.; Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 3

A 58-year-old patient has fatigue and bruising. Results are: WBC 2.8 × 10³/µL (2.8 × 10⁹/L) Neutrophils 0.9 × 10³/µL (0.9 × 10⁹/L) Hgb 8.9 g/dL (89 g/L) Platelets 64 × 10³/µL (64 × 10⁹/L) Bone marrow: myeloblasts 14% of nucleated cells, some with cup-shaped nuclear indentations; mild dysplasia in the erythroid line Karyotype 46,XY[20] NPM1 mutation detected (variant allele frequency 38%) FLT3-ITD not detected Under the WHO 5th edition classification, which diagnosis is BEST?

  1. AML with maturation, defined by differentiation
  2. AML with NPM1 mutation
  3. AML with myelodysplasia-related changes
  4. Myelodysplastic neoplasm with increased blasts-2 (MDS-IB2)

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Differentiation-based types apply only when no defining genetic abnormality is found and need at least 20% blasts; neither holds here.
  • B. Correct. WHO 5th edition diagnoses AML with NPM1 mutation regardless of blast count, so 14% marrow blasts with the mutation is AML.
  • C. Dysplasia is present, but this type needs MDS-related cytogenetics or gene mutations and at least 20% blasts; an NPM1 mutation takes precedence over dysplasia.
  • D. A marrow blast count of 10 to 19% fits MDS-IB2 on numbers alone, but a defining NPM1 mutation overrides the blast threshold and makes this AML.

WHO 5th edition (2022) removed the 20% blast requirement for AML with defining genetic abnormalities, except for AML with BCR::ABL1 and AML with CEBPA mutation, which still need at least 20%. NPM1-mutated AML can therefore be diagnosed at any blast count, which is why molecular results must be read together with the marrow count before a case is signed out as MDS. Cup-shaped nuclei are a common NPM1 morphology. With FLT3-ITD absent, NPM1-mutated AML is a favorable-risk group, so the distinction from MDS-IB2 changes the patient's treatment and prognosis. The International Consensus Classification sets a 10% blast floor for NPM1-mutated AML, and 14% meets that too.

Sources: WHO Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumours, 5th ed. (Khoury 2022)

Question 4

A reference lab receives a smear and CBC from a 27-year-old patient whose platelet count has stayed near 52 × 10³/µL (52 × 10⁹/L) through a year of treatment for presumed immune thrombocytopenia. Hgb and WBC are normal. The smear shows many platelets as large as or larger than red cells. Neutrophils, eosinophils and monocytes contain pale blue, spindle-shaped cytoplasmic inclusions; there is no toxic granulation, no vacuolation and no large dark granules. A sibling has hearing loss and a low platelet count. Which disorder do these findings MOST suggest?

  1. Infection with Döhle bodies and consumption of platelets
  2. Chédiak-Higashi syndrome with giant lysosomal granules
  3. Alder-Reilly anomaly with mucopolysaccharide granules in leukocytes
  4. MYH9-related disorder (May-Hegglin anomaly) with macrothrombocytopenia

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Döhle bodies in infection come with toxic granulation and vacuoles and usually affect neutrophils. A well patient with a year of stable thrombocytopenia, giant platelets and no toxic change points to an inherited cause.
  • B. Chédiak-Higashi produces giant granules in leukocytes, with partial albinism and severe childhood infections. A well adult with spindle-shaped pale inclusions and giant platelets does not fit.
  • C. Alder-Reilly cells carry large, dark, metachromatic granules in granulocytes and sometimes lymphocytes and monocytes. The inclusions here are pale blue and spindle-shaped, and the anomaly does not cause giant platelets or thrombocytopenia.
  • D. Correct. Macrothrombocytopenia with pale blue Döhle-like inclusions in several leukocyte types, and a relative with hearing loss, fits an autosomal dominant MYH9 disorder.

MYH9-related disease results from heterozygous mutations in the gene for non-muscle myosin heavy chain IIA. Aggregates of the abnormal protein form pale blue, oval or spindle-shaped inclusions in neutrophils, eosinophils and monocytes (May-Hegglin inclusions), and platelets are large and reduced in number. It is often mistaken for immune thrombocytopenia until the smear is reviewed. Some families develop kidney disease, hearing loss or cataracts, depending on where the mutation lies. Recognizing giant platelets also matters because impedance counters may class them as red cells and understate the platelet count.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 5

A patient with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis has these results: Hgb 9.8 g/dL (98 g/L) MCV 75 fL RDW 13.9% (0.139) Serum iron 32 µg/dL (5.7 µmol/L; reference 60-150 µg/dL, 10.7-26.9 µmol/L) TIBC 210 µg/dL (37.6 µmol/L; reference 250-450 µg/dL, 44.8-80.6 µmol/L) Transferrin saturation 15% Ferritin 340 ng/mL (340 µg/L; reference 30-300) C-reactive protein raised Which mechanism BEST explains these results?

  1. Hepcidin-driven iron sequestration in macrophages and enterocytes
  2. Reduced globin synthesis from a gene defect
  3. Iron stores depleted by slow gut blood loss
  4. A heme synthesis block trapping mitochondrial iron

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Inflammatory cytokines raise hepcidin, which blocks iron absorption and release from macrophages, so serum iron and TIBC fall while ferritin, an acute-phase reactant and storage marker, stays normal or high.
  • B. Thalassemia trait usually has normal iron studies, a higher red cell count and often a lower MCV; it does not lower serum iron and TIBC.
  • C. Tempting because serum iron and saturation are low, but depleted stores lower ferritin and raise TIBC; here ferritin is high and TIBC is low.
  • D. Sideroblastic anemia raises serum iron and transferrin saturation because iron is plentiful but cannot enter heme; serum iron here is low.

Anemia of chronic inflammation develops when cytokines such as interleukin-6 raise hepatic hepcidin. Hepcidin degrades the iron exporter ferroportin, so iron is held in macrophages and enterocytes and little reaches transferrin. The typical panel is low serum iron, low or low-normal TIBC (transferrin is a negative acute-phase protein), low to moderately reduced saturation (32 ÷ 210 = 15%) and normal to high ferritin. Inflammation also blunts erythropoietin and marrow response. The anemia starts normocytic and may become mildly microcytic, as here. A ferritin below about 100 ng/mL in an inflamed patient, or a raised soluble transferrin receptor, suggests superimposed iron deficiency.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 6

A patient in the intensive care unit with gram-negative sepsis begins oozing from venipuncture sites. Results are: Platelets 41 × 10³/µL (41 × 10⁹/L) PT 22.4 s (reference 11.0-13.5 s) aPTT 54 s (reference 25-35 s) Fibrinogen 88 mg/dL (0.88 g/L; reference 200-400 mg/dL, 2.0-4.0 g/L) D-dimer 9,800 ng/mL FEU (9.8 mg/L FEU; reference less than 500 ng/mL FEU) The smear has occasional red cell fragments with sharp angles and points. Which condition do these results MOST support?

  1. Vitamin K deficiency from antibiotics and poor intake
  2. Severe liver disease with impaired factor synthesis
  3. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
  4. Acute disseminated intravascular coagulation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Vitamin K deficiency prolongs the PT and aPTT but leaves fibrinogen, the platelet count, factor VIII and the D-dimer normal.
  • B. Liver disease also prolongs the PT and aPTT and can lower fibrinogen and platelets, but it does not explain an acute onset in sepsis with schistocytes and a D-dimer this high; factor VIII, if measured, would be normal or high.
  • C. TTP causes thrombocytopenia and schistocytes, but it does not consume clotting factors, so PT, aPTT and fibrinogen stay normal.
  • D. Correct. Thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT and aPTT, low fibrinogen, a very high D-dimer and schistocytes in a septic patient show consumption of platelets and factors with secondary fibrinolysis.

In acute DIC, a trigger such as sepsis exposes tissue factor and generates thrombin throughout the circulation. Platelets and clotting factors, including fibrinogen and factor VIII, are consumed, so the platelet count falls and the PT and aPTT lengthen, while plasmin lyses the cross-linked fibrin and the D-dimer climbs. Fibrin strands in small vessels shear red cells into schistocytes. Severe liver disease can produce a similar screen, but factor VIII is normal or raised there and the D-dimer is usually lower, so a low factor VIII favors DIC. Results are serial: a falling fibrinogen and platelet count track progression.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 7

A bone marrow aspirate from a patient with pancytopenia and an MCV of 121 fL shows erythroid precursors that are larger than normal, with finely dispersed, open nuclear chromatin even in cells whose cytoplasm is already well hemoglobinized. Giant metamyelocytes and giant bands are present. Which defect accounts for this appearance?

  1. Defective heme synthesis in erythroid mitochondria
  2. Slowed nuclear DNA synthesis while the cytoplasm matures normally
  3. Reduced erythropoietin output from damaged kidneys
  4. Precipitation of excess unpaired globin chains

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Faulty heme synthesis gives sideroblastic anemia with ringed sideroblasts on an iron stain and often hypochromic cells, not large precursors with immature nuclei.
  • B. Correct. When DNA synthesis is slowed (B12 or folate deficiency), the nucleus lags while the cytoplasm matures normally; this nuclear-cytoplasmic asynchrony defines megaloblasts and giant myeloid forms.
  • C. Low erythropoietin gives a hypoproliferative normocytic anemia with a hypocellular erythroid line, not enlarged precursors with asynchronous maturation in all lines.
  • D. Unpaired globin chains damage erythroblasts in thalassemia, producing microcytic, hypochromic cells, not megaloblasts or giant metamyelocytes.

Megaloblastic hematopoiesis is a DNA problem. Each division needs thymidine, and B12 and folate are needed to make it; when they are short, nuclear maturation slows while RNA and hemoglobin synthesis continue. The result is large erythroid precursors whose nuclei look younger than their cytoplasm, giant metamyelocytes and bands in the myeloid line, and intramedullary death of the defective cells (ineffective erythropoiesis). That intramedullary destruction raises LDH and indirect bilirubin and explains why the blood shows pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 8

A preoperative screen on an adult patient gives: PT 12.1 s (reference 11.0-13.5 s) aPTT 92 s (reference 25-35 s) 1:1 mix with normal pooled plasma, immediate aPTT 31 s aPTT repeated after a 10-minute activator preincubation 90 s The patient had a tonsillectomy and two dental extractions with no unusual bleeding, takes no medications, and the specimen was drawn by venipuncture. Which deficiency do these results MOST support?

  1. Mild hemophilia A
  2. Lupus anticoagulant directed at phospholipid
  3. Factor XI deficiency
  4. Factor XII deficiency

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Mild hemophilia A gives a correcting aPTT, but a defect long enough to raise the aPTT to 92 s would be expected to cause bleeding after a tonsillectomy and extractions.
  • B. A lupus anticoagulant typically fails to correct on a 1:1 mix; this aPTT corrected fully.
  • C. Factor XI deficiency also prolongs the aPTT and corrects on mixing, but it typically causes bleeding after surgery at sites with high fibrinolytic activity such as tonsils and tooth sockets, which this patient did not have.
  • D. Correct. Factor XII deficiency gives a long aPTT that corrects with normal plasma but causes no bleeding, because in vivo coagulation does not need factor XII.

The aPTT uses a contact activator, so in the test tube it depends on factor XII, prekallikrein and high-molecular-weight kininogen. In the body, tissue factor with factor VIIa starts coagulation and thrombin can activate factor XI directly, so deficiencies of the contact factors prolong the aPTT without any bleeding; they are found incidentally on preoperative screens. A full correction on mixing points to a deficiency rather than an inhibitor. Prekallikrein deficiency looks the same, but lengthening the activator preincubation lets factor XII autoactivate and shortens the aPTT; here it stayed long, leaving factor XII.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 9

A 71-year-old patient with chronic lymphocytic leukemia has new anemia. A reference laboratory reports: WBC 48.6 × 10³/µL (48.6 × 10⁹/L), 88% lymphocytes Hgb 7.9 g/dL (79 g/L) MCV 97 fL MCHC 37.1 g/dL (371 g/L) Platelets 142 × 10³/µL (142 × 10⁹/L) Reticulocytes 9.4% (0.094) Haptoglobin less than 10 mg/dL (0.1 g/L) The smear shows many small, dense, round red cells lacking central pallor, polychromasia and many smudge cells. Which result would MOST directly distinguish the cause of these spherocytes from hereditary spherocytosis?

  1. A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) with anti-IgG
  2. A raised LD with an undetectable haptoglobin on the same sample
  3. An MCHC above the reference range on the automated analyzer
  4. An incubated osmotic fragility curve shifted toward lysis at higher salt concentrations

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. IgG coating the red cells shows an immune cause, warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia, which is a known complication of chronic lymphocytic leukemia. The DAT is negative in hereditary spherocytosis.
  • B. These confirm hemolysis but occur in both disorders, so they do not distinguish an immune cause from a membrane defect.
  • C. A raised MCHC is a clue to spherocytes of any cause; both hereditary spherocytosis and immune hemolysis produce it.
  • D. Increased osmotic fragility reflects the spherocyte's low surface-to-volume ratio, which is the same in immune and hereditary spherocytes, so it does not separate them.

Spherocytes form when red cells lose membrane, either because of an inherited defect in cytoskeletal proteins or because macrophages in the spleen nibble away membrane coated with IgG. Both kinds show increased osmotic fragility, a raised MCHC, reticulocytosis and the usual hemolysis markers. The direct antiglobulin test separates them: positive in autoimmune hemolytic anemia, negative in hereditary spherocytosis. In warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia the DAT is usually positive for IgG with or without C3. Chronic lymphocytic leukemia is a recognized secondary cause, so a new hemolytic anemia in this patient calls for a DAT first.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 10

A reference lab's molecular section reports serial quantitative RT-PCR results for BCR::ABL1 on the International Scale (IS) for a 55-year-old patient with chronic-phase CML receiving a tyrosine kinase inhibitor: At diagnosis: 84% 3 months: 7.6% 6 months: 0.82% 12 months: 0.074% Which interpretation of the 12-month result is correct?

  1. No major molecular response, since transcripts are still detectable
  2. Not yet a major response, which requires a level at or below 0.01% IS
  3. Major molecular response (MMR), because the level is at or below 0.1% IS
  4. Uninterpretable until confirmed by FISH for the fusion

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Major molecular response is defined by a level at or below 0.1% IS, not by absent transcripts. Undetectable or very low levels describe deeper responses.
  • B. A level of 0.01% IS corresponds to a deeper (4-log) response. The threshold for a major molecular response is 0.1%, which this result is below.
  • C. Correct. A BCR::ABL1 level at or below 0.1% on the International Scale, a 3-log reduction from the standardized baseline, is a major molecular response.
  • D. FISH is far less sensitive than quantitative PCR at low disease levels and is not used to confirm a molecular response. A standardized IS value is interpreted directly.

Quantitative RT-PCR measures BCR::ABL1 transcripts against a control gene, and conversion to the International Scale lets results from different laboratories be compared, with 100% IS representing the standardized untreated baseline. A major molecular response is a level of 0.1% IS or lower, one-thousandth of that baseline. Monitoring is usually done at 3, 6 and 12 months and then periodically, so the trend matters as well as a single value. Here the level fell steadily, crossing below 0.1% by 12 months. A rising level on treatment prompts repeat testing and often a kinase domain mutation study.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version
