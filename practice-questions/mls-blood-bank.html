Question 1

A patient with anti-D is carrying a fetus that typed D positive by cell-free fetal DNA testing. Her anti-D titers by tube indirect antiglobulin test, each tested in parallel with the previous frozen sample: 16 weeks: 4 20 weeks: 8 24 weeks: 32 The laboratory's critical titer is 16. What does the 24-week result mean for the pregnancy?

  1. The titer remains the guide and should be repeated at 2- to 4-week intervals
  2. The rise reflects passive anti-D from routine Rh immune globulin
  3. The fetus is severely anemic and needs an intrauterine transfusion now
  4. The fetus is at risk of anemia, so MCA Doppler surveillance now replaces titers

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Repeating titers at 2- to 4-week intervals is the plan while the titer is under the critical value. At 32, it has passed the laboratory's critical titer of 16.
  • B. Routine antenatal Rh immune globulin is given at 28 weeks, after this sample, and it is not given to a patient already immunized to D. The rising titer is her own active response.
  • C. A titer shows the risk of disease, not its severity. Intrauterine transfusion follows evidence of fetal anemia from Doppler and fetal blood sampling, not a titer alone.
  • D. Correct. Once the titer reaches the critical value, further titers add little, and the fetus is followed with middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity every 1 to 2 weeks to detect anemia.

Maternal antibody titers screen for risk. Each laboratory sets a critical titer, typically 1:8 to 1:32, at which the fetus may be at risk of significant hemolysis. Under that value, titers are repeated every 2 to 4 weeks after 20 weeks; at or over it, fetal assessment moves to middle cerebral artery Doppler, because a high peak systolic velocity for gestational age signals the high-output state of fetal anemia. An elevated velocity then leads to fetal blood sampling and intravascular intrauterine transfusion when needed. Testing each sample in parallel with the frozen previous one keeps the series comparable, so a rise of two dilutions here is a true change.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Neonate (2024)

Question 2

In an outpatient infusion center, a patient develops stridor, wheezing and a blood pressure of 72/40 mm Hg within minutes of starting a unit of plasma; temperature is unchanged. The reaction workup shows a correct clerical check, no hemolysis and a negative DAT. A pretransfusion sample has: IgA < 1 mg/dL (reference 70–400 mg/dL [0.7–4.0 g/L]) Anti-IgA: detected The patient will need plasma again next month. Which product should the blood bank arrange?

  1. Plasma collected from IgA-deficient donors
  2. Irradiated plasma
  3. Leukocyte-reduced plasma
  4. Pooled solvent/detergent-treated plasma

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. With IgA deficiency and anti-IgA, plasma itself carries the antigen, and it cannot be washed. Plasma from IgA-deficient donors, sourced through the blood supplier, removes the target.
  • B. Irradiation stops donor T lymphocytes from proliferating to prevent transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease. It has no effect on IgA, and plasma does not need it.
  • C. Leukocyte reduction lowers febrile reactions, HLA alloimmunization and CMV transmission. It removes white cells, not plasma proteins such as IgA.
  • D. Solvent/detergent treatment inactivates enveloped viruses, but the pooled plasma still contains normal amounts of IgA from many donors, so the antigen remains.

Most allergic transfusion reactions are mild urticaria from donor plasma allergens, but anaphylaxis (hypotension, bronchospasm, laryngeal edema) without fever within minutes of starting the transfusion is a different event. A recognized cause is severe IgA deficiency with anti-IgA antibodies, although most anaphylactic reactions have no identified cause. For such a patient, cellular components can be washed to remove plasma, while plasma must come from IgA-deficient donors. Because these units are rare, the transfusion service plans ahead with the blood supplier as soon as the need is known.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Complications of Transfusion (2026); Circular of Information for Blood and Blood Components (2024)

Question 3

On the evening shift, a hospital blood bank that performs antiglobulin crossmatches for every patient tests 3 units for a group O, RhD-positive patient with no antibody history. Antibody screen (3 cells, IAT): negative Patient DAT: 0; autocontrol 0 Units 1 and 2: IS 0 | IAT 0 Unit 3: IS 0 | IAT 2+ What is the BEST next step?

  1. Perform a DAT on a segment from unit 3
  2. Retype unit 3 for ABO from a segment
  3. Run an 11-cell antibody identification panel on the patient's plasma
  4. Repeat the crossmatch with DTT-treated unit 3 cells

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Donor cells already coated with IgG react in any antiglobulin test, whatever the recipient's plasma contains. A positive DAT on the segment explains the result and removes the unit from use.
  • B. ABO incompatibility shows at immediate spin, as strong agglutination of a mislabeled unit would; this unit is compatible at IS and reacts only at the antiglobulin phase.
  • C. A panel looks for an alloantibody, but the screen is negative, and an antibody to a low-prevalence antigen on unit 3 would react with a panel cell only by chance. It is a later step if the donor DAT is negative.
  • D. DTT-treated cells remove interference from anti-CD38 drugs, which would also make units 1 and 2 and the screen react; a reaction confined to one unit does not fit.

An antiglobulin crossmatch tests the recipient's plasma against the actual donor cells, so it can detect what screening cells miss. When one unit is incompatible at the antiglobulin phase while the screen, autocontrol and other units are negative, the two main explanations are a donor unit with a positive DAT and a recipient antibody to a low-prevalence antigen carried by that donor but absent from the screening cells. A DAT on the donor segment separates them quickly. DAT-positive donors are rare (about 0.01% in one large series); the unit is not issued and the supplier is notified. If the donor DAT is negative, the low-prevalence antibody is investigated.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 606 (blood and components cGMP); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 4

A 45-year-old patient with common variable immunodeficiency is bleeding before an urgent procedure and needs red cells and plasma before the ABO discrepancy can be resolved. Results: Anti-A 4+ | Anti-B 0 | Anti-D 3+ A1 cells 0 | B cells 0 Autocontrol 0 | Antibody screen negative IgG 180 mg/dL (1.8 g/L); reference 700–1600 mg/dL (7.0–16.0 g/L) Which components should be issued until the discrepancy is resolved?

  1. Group O red cells and group AB plasma, compatible with any ABO type
  2. Group O red cells and group O plasma, as universal donor components
  3. Group A red cells and group A plasma, matching the forward grouping
  4. Group AB red cells and group AB plasma, since no isoagglutinins are present

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Group O red cells lack A and B, and group AB plasma lacks anti-A and anti-B, so both are compatible whatever the patient's group proves to be.
  • B. Group O red cells are safe, but group O plasma carries anti-A and anti-B, which would react with the patient's strongly A-positive red cells.
  • C. The forward grouping suggests group A, but with the reverse grouping missing its expected anti-B the group is unresolved, and the transfusion service does not issue group-specific components.
  • D. Absent isoagglutinins reflect low antibody production, not a true AB type; the forward grouping shows no B antigen, and AB red cells carry B for any anti-B below detection.

This is a group I ABO discrepancy: the reverse grouping is weaker than the forward grouping predicts because the patient makes little antibody. Causes include hypogammaglobulinemia and agammaglobulinemia, immunosuppression, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, the elderly, and infants younger than about 4 months. Workup includes incubating the reverse grouping at room temperature or 4 °C with group O cells and an autocontrol as controls. While the ABO group is unresolved, red cells must be compatible with any ABO antibody the patient may have and plasma must lack antibodies to any antigen the patient's cells may carry, so group O red cells and group AB plasma are issued, and the discrepancy is documented.

Sources: Circular of Information for Blood and Blood Components (2024); ISBT Red Cell Immunogenetics and Blood Group Terminology

Question 5

A 59-year-old patient in an orthopedic unit has been receiving intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam for 9 days for osteomyelitis. Results: Hgb 7.4 g/dL (74 g/L), down from 11.8 g/dL (118 g/L) 4 days ago Haptoglobin less than 10 mg/dL (less than 0.1 g/L) DAT: anti-IgG 3+, anti-C3 2+ Antibody screen: negative Acid eluate tested with screening cells: nonreactive The patient has not been transfused. Which test should the reference laboratory perform NEXT?

  1. Test the plasma with piperacillin-treated red cells and with the drug in solution
  2. Repeat the elution with a heat method and test the eluate with an 11-cell panel
  3. Perform an autologous adsorption at 37 °C to look for alloantibodies
  4. Perform a Donath-Landsteiner test on a freshly drawn clotted specimen

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Drug-dependent antibodies react only when the drug is present, either bound to the cells or added to the test system, so this testing confirms piperacillin as the cause.
  • B. A repeat eluate is tempting when the DAT is strong, but a nonreactive eluate with a strong DAT in a patient on a beta-lactam already points to a drug-dependent antibody, which reference cells without the drug will not detect.
  • C. Autoadsorption removes warm autoantibody so alloantibodies can be detected, but the antibody screen is already negative and there is no panreactive autoantibody to remove.
  • D. PCH gives a complement-only DAT in a child after a viral illness; this patient has IgG on the cells and a drug exposure instead.

Drug-induced immune hemolytic anemia is suspected when a patient develops hemolysis with a positive DAT and a nonreactive (or weak) eluate while taking a drug. Penicillins and cephalosporins, now piperacillin most often, can form a stable hapten on the red cell membrane or act as drug-dependent antibodies that react only in the drug's presence. Reference laboratories test the patient's plasma and eluate against drug-treated red cells and against untreated cells with the drug added in solution. A warm autoantibody, by contrast, gives a reactive eluate that reacts with every panel cell and persists after the drug is stopped.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Merck Manual Professional: Complications of Transfusion (2026)

Question 6

A blood center's quality control laboratory tests four units of Red Blood Cells Leukocytes Reduced prepared by prestorage filtration: Unit A: residual WBCs 1.2 × 10⁶, red cell recovery 89% Unit B: residual WBCs 4.1 × 10⁶, red cell recovery 86% Unit C: residual WBCs 7.5 × 10⁵, red cell recovery 81% Unit D: residual WBCs 2.6 × 10⁶, red cell recovery 91% Which unit fails quality control?

  1. Unit D, for its red cell recovery above 90%
  2. Unit B, for its residual leukocyte count
  3. Unit C, for its red cell recovery below the 85% minimum
  4. Unit A, for its residual leukocyte count

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. There is no upper limit on red cell recovery; 91% simply means the filter retained little red cell mass, which is desirable.
  • B. Unit B's 4.1 × 10⁶ leukocytes is the highest count but still below the limit of 5.0 × 10⁶, and its 86% recovery meets the 85% requirement.
  • C. Correct. Unit C has very few leukocytes, but it retained only 81% of the original red cells; a leukocyte-reduced red cell unit must keep at least 85%.
  • D. Unit A has 1.2 × 10⁶ residual leukocytes, well under 5.0 × 10⁶, and an 89% recovery; it passes both criteria.

Red Blood Cells Leukocytes Reduced must contain fewer than 5.0 × 10⁶ residual leukocytes and must retain at least 85% of the original red cell content. Each unit has to meet both criteria. Unit C has the lowest leukocyte count (7.5 × 10⁵ is 0.75 × 10⁶), so it is easy to pass it on the count alone, but it lost too much red cell mass in the filter and fails. Leukocyte reduction lowers the risk of febrile nonhemolytic reactions, HLA alloimmunization and cytomegalovirus transmission; a failed unit triggers investigation of the filter lot, the filtration temperature and the procedure.

Sources: Circular of Information for Blood and Blood Components (2024)

Question 7

A hospital transfusion service follows 21 CFR 606.151: pretransfusion specimens must be less than 3 days old when the patient has been pregnant or transfused in the previous 3 months. For patients with neither event in the previous 3 months, the laboratory's validated policy allows a preoperative specimen to be used for up to 14 days. A day-shift processor reviews four crossmatch requests. Which request requires a NEW specimen?

  1. A 34-year-old woman who delivered 7 weeks ago, specimen collected 4 days ago
  2. A 58-year-old man with no transfusions ever, specimen collected 9 days ago
  3. A 70-year-old woman transfused 5 months ago, specimen collected 6 days ago
  4. A 46-year-old man transfused 3 weeks ago, specimen collected yesterday

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Pregnancy within the previous 3 months can stimulate new alloantibodies, so the specimen must be less than 3 days old; at 4 days it is too old.
  • B. With no transfusion or pregnancy in the previous 3 months, the laboratory's validated 14-day preoperative policy applies, so this specimen can still be used.
  • C. The transfusion was more than 3 months ago, so the 3-day restriction does not apply and the 14-day policy still covers the specimen.
  • D. Recent transfusion triggers the 3-day rule, but a specimen drawn yesterday is still within it, so a new draw is not needed.

Transfusion or pregnancy can stimulate a primary or anamnestic antibody response within days. An antibody that was undetectable when the specimen was drawn can rise to a clinically significant level, so a specimen older than 3 days may miss it. That is why 21 CFR 606.151(b) requires fresh specimens, less than 3 days old, for any patient pregnant or transfused in the previous 3 months. Patients with neither exposure are unlikely to form new antibodies quickly, and many services validate a longer hold for preadmission testing, often with a patient-history declaration.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 606 (blood and components cGMP)

Question 8

A 29-year-old pregnant patient at 12 weeks has these results at an obstetric clinic laboratory: Monoclonal IgM anti-D, tube, immediate spin: 1+ (D-positive controls give 3+ to 4+) Antibody screen: negative Reference laboratory RHD genotyping: weak D type 2 According to the AABB and CAP work group recommendations, how should this patient be managed?

  1. As Rh(D) positive, with no Rh immune globulin and D-positive red cells acceptable
  2. As a partial D, receiving D-negative red cells and Rh immune globulin
  3. As Rh(D) negative, with antenatal and postpartum Rh immune globulin
  4. As Rh(D) positive for RhIG, but given D-negative red cells

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Weak D types 1, 2 and 3 carry all D epitopes at reduced density and are managed as D positive for both RhIG and red cell selection.
  • B. Weak D type 2 is a weak D, not a partial D; partial D types such as DVI lack epitopes and are managed as D negative, but that does not apply here.
  • C. This conservative approach was common before genotyping, but people with weak D types 1, 2 and 3 have not been shown to make alloanti-D, so the RhIG would be unnecessary.
  • D. The recommendation handles RhIG and transfusion the same way for weak D types 1 to 3; there is no reason to restrict this patient to D-negative red cells.

A serologic weak D phenotype (weaker than expected anti-D reactions) can reflect weak D or partial D, which serology cannot reliably tell apart. The 2015 AABB and CAP work group recommended RHD genotyping for pregnant patients and transfusion candidates with this phenotype. Weak D types 1, 2 and 3, the most common in people of European ancestry, encode all D epitopes at lower density, and alloanti-D has not been reported in them, so they are managed as D positive: no RhIG and D-positive red cells acceptable. Other weak D types and partial D types are managed as D negative. Genotyping saves RhIG doses and D-negative units.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Neonate (2024); ISBT Red Cell Immunogenetics and Blood Group Terminology

Question 9

A 44-year-old patient received 2 units of red cells 9 days ago during hip fracture repair; the pretransfusion antibody screen was negative. Hgb on day 1: 10.4 g/dL (104 g/L) Hgb today: 8.1 g/dL (81 g/L) Total bilirubin today: 2.6 mg/dL (44 µmol/L) Antibody screen today: 1+ with one of three cells DAT: IgG 1+ mixed-field; C3 0 Acid eluate: 2+ with all Jk(a+) panel cells, 0 with all Jk(a-) cells Which interpretation is BEST?

  1. Anti-Jka coating transfused donor cells in a delayed reaction
  2. Drug-dependent antibody against the perioperative antibiotic
  3. Passive anti-Jka transferred in the residual plasma of a donor unit
  4. Warm autoantibody with mimicking Jka specificity, to be confirmed by autoadsorption

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A new alloantibody, IgG on a minority of circulating cells and an eluate with clear Jka specificity 1 to 2 weeks after transfusion describe an anamnestic delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction against Jk(a+) donor cells.
  • B. Drug-dependent antibodies usually give a nonreactive eluate with untreated panel cells; this eluate reacts strongly with drug-free cells and shows a single blood group specificity.
  • C. Passive antibody from a donor is possible but small in volume from red cell units, and it would not explain an antibody that was absent before transfusion and now appears with falling hemoglobin and rising bilirubin.
  • D. Autoantibodies can mimic a specificity, but they coat the patient's own cells, so the DAT is not mixed-field, and recently transfused patients are poor candidates for autoadsorption because donor cells are still circulating.

Elution frees antibody bound to red cells so its specificity can be tested on a panel. After a recent transfusion the circulation holds two populations, the patient's own antigen-negative cells and donor cells, so IgG on the donor cells alone gives a mixed-field DAT. An anamnestic response 1 to 4 weeks after exposure raises an antibody that was below detectable levels at screening, a classic behavior of Kidd antibodies. The eluate's Jka specificity, the weak new plasma antibody, the hemoglobin drop and the bilirubin rise together define a delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction. The laboratory notifies the transfusion service physician and supplies Jk(a-) units from now on.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Complications of Transfusion (2026)

Question 10

A hospital blood bank performs routine dose mapping of its X-ray blood irradiator with dosimeters placed throughout a canister filled with a water-equivalent phantom. Results: Central dose 2,600 cGy Minimum dose at any point 1,320 cGy Maximum dose at any point 3,900 cGy What should the technologist conclude?

  1. It passes, because the central dose meets the 2,500 cGy target
  2. It fails, because the maximum dose is over 3,000 cGy
  3. It fails, because the minimum dose is under 1,500 cGy
  4. It passes, because each point received more than 1,000 cGy

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. The central dose does meet the target, but a passing map also requires every point in the canister to receive at least 1,500 cGy.
  • B. A maximum of 3,900 cGy is within the usual upper limit of 5,000 cGy, so the maximum is not the problem.
  • C. Correct. Some part of a component in this position would receive too little radiation to stop T lymphocyte proliferation reliably.
  • D. 1,000 cGy is not the required minimum; the required minimum anywhere in the component is 1,500 cGy.

The Circular of Information states that the standard irradiation dose is 2,500 cGy targeted to the central portion of the container, with a minimum of 1,500 cGy delivered to any part of the component. Dose mapping checks the whole field, because a component in a cold spot could receive too little dose to inactivate lymphocytes and the recipient would stay at risk of TA-GVHD. A minimum of 1,320 cGy fails even though the center passes. The laboratory should remove the irradiator from service, have it evaluated, review units irradiated since the last acceptable map, and repeat the mapping before use.

Sources: Circular of Information for Blood and Blood Components (2024); FDA Blood Guidances
