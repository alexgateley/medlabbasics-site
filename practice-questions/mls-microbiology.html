Question 1

Sputum from a patient with a chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation shows many neutrophils and gram-negative diplococci with flattened adjacent sides. Gray-white, nonhemolytic colonies grow on sheep blood and chocolate agar; a colony slides intact across the agar when pushed with a loop. Results: Oxidase: positive CTA glucose, maltose, sucrose and lactose: no acid Nitrate reduction: positive DNase: positive Tributyrin hydrolysis: positive Which organism is MOST likely?

  1. Neisseria cinerea
  2. Neisseria gonorrhoeae
  3. Moraxella catarrhalis
  4. Neisseria meningitidis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. N. cinerea is an asaccharolytic diplococcus that can be confused with other Neisseria, but it does not reduce nitrate, is DNase negative and does not hydrolyze tributyrin.
  • B. Gonococci produce acid from glucose, grow poorly on sheep blood agar and are an unusual cause of a COPD exacerbation.
  • C. Correct. An oxidase-positive diplococcus that uses no carbohydrates and is DNase, nitrate and tributyrin positive, with colonies that slide on the agar, fits M. catarrhalis.
  • D. Meningococci produce acid from glucose and maltose in CTA media, which this isolate does not.

Moraxella catarrhalis is a gram-negative diplococcus that causes otitis media, sinusitis and exacerbations of chronic lung disease. It is oxidase positive but produces no acid from glucose, maltose, sucrose, lactose or fructose. Identification is best confirmed by positive results in at least three of DNase production, nitrate and nitrite reduction, and tributyrin hydrolysis (butyrate esterase). Its colonies remain intact and slide across the agar like a hockey puck. Over 90% of isolates produce beta-lactamase, so a beta-lactamase test is often reported with the identification.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 2

During a procedure audit in an outpatient clinic laboratory, a reviewer reads how prenatal group B streptococcus screening is performed: Specimen: vaginal-rectal swab at 36 weeks in transport medium Processing: swab streaked directly onto sheep blood agar Incubation: 35 °C in 5% CO2, read at 24 hours Report: negative if no beta-hemolytic colonies are present Which change is MOST needed to meet current screening recommendations?

  1. Extend blood agar incubation to 72 hours before reporting
  2. Replace blood agar with MacConkey agar to suppress vaginal flora
  3. Collect a separate cervical swab instead of a vaginal-rectal swab
  4. Incubate the swab in selective enrichment broth, such as Lim broth, before subculture

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Longer incubation of a direct plate adds little; the main loss of sensitivity comes from too few organisms being plated, which selective enrichment corrects.
  • B. MacConkey agar inhibits gram-positive organisms, so it would suppress group B streptococci along with other gram-positive flora.
  • C. A combined lower vaginal and rectal swab is the recommended specimen; cervical swabs yield fewer carriers and are not used for screening.
  • D. Correct. Overnight incubation in a Todd-Hewitt broth with antibiotics such as colistin and nalidixic acid raises GBS numbers and suppresses fecal flora before subculture.

Direct plating misses many group B streptococcus carriers, so the CDC procedure places the vaginal-rectal swab in a selective enrichment broth (Todd-Hewitt with colistin and nalidixic acid, or gentamicin and nalidixic acid) and incubates it 18 to 24 hours at 35-37 °C before subculture to blood agar or a chromogenic medium, or before a validated NAAT. The audit also exposes a second gap: about 4% of isolates are nonhemolytic, so a report based on beta-hemolysis alone misses them; suspicious colonies are confirmed by CAMP test or latex agglutination. Refrigerated specimens processed within 24 hours give the best recovery.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 3

An outpatient clinic stool from a patient with bloody diarrhea after a backyard barbecue is plated on Campy-CVA agar and incubated microaerobically at 42 °C. At 48 hours there are flat, gray, spreading colonies. Results: Gram stain: curved, comma- and S-shaped gram-negative rods, some in gull-wing pairs Oxidase: positive Catalase: positive Hippurate hydrolysis: positive Which organism do these results identify?

  1. Campylobacter jejuni
  2. Campylobacter fetus, a cause of bacteremia
  3. Helicobacter pylori, a strong urease producer
  4. Campylobacter coli

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A curved, oxidase- and catalase-positive rod recovered at 42 °C in a microaerobic atmosphere that hydrolyzes hippurate is C. jejuni.
  • B. C. fetus is usually recovered from blood in older or immunocompromised patients, grows best at 25 to 37 °C rather than 42 °C, and is hippurate negative.
  • C. H. pylori is a curved, oxidase- and catalase-positive rod, but it is a gastric organism detected by stool antigen, breath test or biopsy, not by stool culture at 42 °C.
  • D. C. coli grows under the same conditions with the same morphology and is the closest near-miss, but it does not hydrolyze hippurate.

Campylobacter jejuni grows best at 42 °C in a microaerobic atmosphere (about 5% oxygen and 10% carbon dioxide), so selective media such as Campy-CVA are incubated hot to suppress fecal flora. Colonies are flat, gray and watery, and the Gram stain shows comma, S or gull-wing forms. Oxidase and catalase are positive, and hippurate hydrolysis confirms C. jejuni; a hippurate-negative isolate is C. coli or another species and needs further testing. Shedding is usually heavy enough that enrichment is not needed. Undercooked poultry is a common source, and C. jejuni infection is a recognized trigger of Guillain-Barre syndrome.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 4

Two of two blood culture sets from a 68-year-old patient with suspected endocarditis grow gram-positive cocci in chains. Results: Colonies: small, gray, nonhemolytic on sheep blood agar Catalase: negative Bile esculin: positive (blackening) Growth in 6.5% NaCl broth: none PYR: negative Lancefield group D antigen: positive Which identification fits, and which association makes it important to report accurately?

  1. Streptococcus mitis group; dental disease and recent dental work
  2. Streptococcus gallolyticus group; colorectal adenoma or carcinoma
  3. Enterococcus faecalis; a urinary or biliary source
  4. Streptococcus agalactiae; a diabetic soft-tissue source

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Viridans streptococci are common agents of endocarditis after dental procedures, but they grow poorly on bile, are rarely bile esculin positive and lack group D antigen.
  • B. Correct. A group D, bile esculin positive, salt-negative, PYR-negative streptococcus is the S. bovis/S. gallolyticus group, and bacteremia or endocarditis with it is linked to colonic adenoma and carcinoma.
  • C. Enterococci also blacken bile esculin and carry group D antigen, which is why this is tempting, but they grow in 6.5% NaCl and are PYR positive; this isolate fails both.
  • D. Group B streptococci cause bacteremia in older adults with diabetes, but they carry group B antigen, are usually beta-hemolytic and do not hydrolyze bile esculin.

Bile esculin separates enterococci and group D streptococci from other streptococci, because only they grow in 40% bile and hydrolyze esculin to blacken the medium. The 6.5% NaCl broth and PYR then split the pair: enterococci grow in salt and are PYR positive, whereas the S. bovis group (now S. gallolyticus and relatives) fails in salt and is PYR negative. Getting this right matters beyond therapy: S. gallolyticus bacteremia and endocarditis are associated with colonic adenomas and carcinoma, so the report prompts a colon evaluation, while a false report can lead to unnecessary investigations. Bile esculin positive isolates from blood deserve full identification rather than a presumptive call.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 5

Specimen processing receives three swabs from the same genital ulcer, collected at an urgent care clinic and each labeled for herpes simplex virus NAAT: Swab 1: calcium alginate tip on a wooden shaft, in viral transport medium Swab 2: rayon tip on a plastic shaft, in a gel-containing bacterial transport tube Swab 3: flocked nylon tip on a plastic shaft, in viral transport medium Which swabs are acceptable for testing?

  1. Swabs 2 and 3
  2. Swab 3
  3. Swabs 1, 2 and 3
  4. Swabs 1 and 3

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Rayon on plastic is an acceptable swab, but gel-containing bacterial transport swabs are excluded for HSV testing, so swab 2 fails on the transport device.
  • B. Correct. A flocked synthetic swab on a plastic shaft in viral transport medium meets both requirements: no inhibitory swab material and no gel.
  • C. Accepting every swab ignores the device rules; a false-negative HSV result from an inhibited reaction could delay a diagnosis that changes care.
  • D. Viral transport medium is right for swab 1, but calcium alginate tips and wooden shafts release substances that inhibit amplification, so the device itself is unacceptable.

Molecular and viral tests depend on the collection device as much as on the site. The IDSA/ASM guide says not to use calcium alginate-tipped swabs, wooden-shaft swabs or gel-containing transport swabs for HSV, because alginate and wood release substances that inhibit nucleic acid amplification and harm viral recovery, and gel media are not validated for viral testing. Flocked or other synthetic swabs on plastic shafts, placed in viral (M4 or M5 type) transport medium, are preferred, and flocked swabs release more of the sample. Unacceptable devices are rejected and recollected while the lesion is still present.

Sources: CDC STI Treatment Guidelines (2021)

Question 6

An outpatient clinic sends serum from an unvaccinated patient who has fever, cough and a rash that began yesterday, 12 days after contact with a confirmed measles case. Results: Measles IgM: negative Measles IgG: negative No other specimen was collected The clinician still suspects measles. What should the laboratory recommend NEXT?

  1. Convalescent IgG in 4 weeks, with no further testing now
  2. A urine specimen as the single confirmatory test
  3. A nasopharyngeal or throat swab for measles RT-PCR
  4. Reporting measles excluded, since both antibody classes are negative

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A later rise in IgG supports the diagnosis in retrospect, but waiting a month gives up the chance to confirm the case while isolation and contact tracing decisions are being made.
  • B. Urine can yield measles RNA and is sometimes collected in addition, but nasopharyngeal and throat swabs are preferred, so urine alone is the weaker choice.
  • C. Correct. Viral RNA detection is most successful on the first days of rash, and these swabs are the preferred specimens, so the negative early serology does not end the workup.
  • D. IgM is undetectable in some cases until about 3 days after symptoms begin, so serum drawn this early can be negative in a patient who does have measles.

Measles is confirmed by collecting a nasopharyngeal swab, throat swab or urine specimen for RT-PCR along with a blood specimen for serology, and nasopharyngeal or throat swabs are preferred over urine. Detection of measles RNA succeeds best when the specimen is taken on the first day of rash, although it may still be found 10 to 14 days after rash onset. The IgM response is not detectable in some cases until about 3 days after symptoms begin, so an acute serum drawn 3 days or less after rash onset that is negative, with a negative RT-PCR or none done, is followed by a second serum collected 3 to 10 days after onset. Suspected cases are reported to public health immediately, because confirmation drives isolation and contact investigation.

Sources: CDC: Clinical Overview of Measles

Question 7

Salmonella enterica serotype Enteritidis grows from the blood cultures of an immunocompromised inpatient. The automated panel reports every agent as susceptible: Ampicillin Cefazolin Cefuroxime Ceftriaxone Ciprofloxacin Gentamicin Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole The laboratory's procedure, based on CLSI M100, states: for Salmonella and Shigella, never report first- or second-generation cephalosporins, cephamycins or aminoglycosides as susceptible; for fecal isolates report ampicillin, a fluoroquinolone and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; for extraintestinal Salmonella also report a third-generation cephalosporin. Which panel results should the laboratory suppress instead of releasing them as susceptible?

  1. Cefazolin, cefuroxime and gentamicin
  2. Gentamicin, releasing each cephalosporin
  3. Cefazolin and gentamicin, keeping cefuroxime
  4. The three cephalosporins and gentamicin

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Cefazolin (first generation), cefuroxime (second generation) and gentamicin (an aminoglycoside) are suppressed, while ampicillin, ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and, because the isolate is from blood, ceftriaxone are released.
  • B. Gentamicin is suppressed as an aminoglycoside, but cefazolin and cefuroxime can look active in vitro against Salmonella without working clinically, so their susceptible results must be suppressed too.
  • C. Tempting because cefuroxime is a cephalosporin with useful gram-negative activity, but it is a second-generation agent, so the procedure bars it as well; only ceftriaxone qualifies as the third-generation cephalosporin.
  • D. This treats every cephalosporin alike. The procedure bars only first- and second-generation agents and, for an extraintestinal isolate, calls for a third-generation cephalosporin, so ceftriaxone must be released.

Salmonella and Shigella survive inside host cells, and some drugs that inhibit them in vitro do not reach effective levels there, so first- and second-generation cephalosporins, cephamycins and aminoglycosides are not reported as susceptible even when the MIC is low. The specimen source then decides the list: a stool isolate gets ampicillin, a fluoroquinolone and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, while a blood isolate also gets a third-generation cephalosporin such as ceftriaxone. Applying the rule means knowing the generation of each cephalosporin: cefazolin is first generation, cefuroxime second and ceftriaxone third. Expert rules in the instrument or LIS should suppress the misleading results before release.

Sources: CLSI M100, 35th ed. (2025)

Question 8

A patient with advanced HIV infection has fever, weight loss, diarrhea and anemia. CD4 count: 30 cells/µL (0.03 × 10⁹/L) A mycobacterial blood culture becomes positive on day 12. The AFB smear of the broth shows acid-fast bacilli. On subculture, smooth, nonpigmented colonies appear after 2 weeks, with no color change after light exposure. An MPT64 antigen test on the growth is negative. Which organism is MOST likely?

  1. Mycobacterium kansasii
  2. Mycobacterium avium complex
  3. Mycobacterium fortuitum
  4. Mycobacterium tuberculosis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. M. kansasii can disseminate, but it is a photochromogen whose colonies turn yellow after light exposure, and it mainly causes pulmonary disease.
  • B. Correct. MAC causes disseminated infection with fever, diarrhea and anemia when the CD4 count falls below about 50 cells/µL; it is a slow-growing nonchromogen with smooth colonies and is recovered from blood.
  • C. M. fortuitum is a rapid grower that forms colonies within 7 days on subculture; 2 weeks to growth places this isolate among the slow growers.
  • D. Disseminated tuberculosis also occurs in advanced HIV, but M. tuberculosis forms rough, buff colonies, and the negative MPT64 antigen test argues against M. tuberculosis complex.

Disseminated Mycobacterium avium complex infection develops late in HIV infection, typically with a CD4 count below 50 cells/µL, and causes fever, weight loss, diarrhea, abdominal pain, anemia and thrombocytopenia. It is confirmed by mycobacterial blood or bone marrow culture; MAC in stool or sputum may be colonization. MAC is a slow grower (more than 7 days to visible colonies) and a nonchromogen with smooth colonies. A negative MPT64 antigen test on the isolate excludes M. tuberculosis complex, and a DNA probe, MALDI-TOF or sequencing confirms the species.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 9

A mycobacteriology laboratory reviews a Lowenstein-Jensen slant that grew 3 colonies at 6 weeks, identified as M. tuberculosis complex by DNA probe. Findings for that patient: Three sputum fluorochrome smears: no acid-fast bacilli M. tuberculosis complex nucleic acid amplification test on the same specimen: not detected, internal control valid Clinical note: no cough, no weight loss, imaging without infiltrate or cavity Liquid culture on the same specimen: no growth at 6 weeks Processing log: digested and decontaminated in the same batch as a 4+ smear-positive sputum from another patient Which explanation is MOST likely?

  1. A nontuberculous mycobacterium that the DNA probe called M. tuberculosis complex
  2. Inhibitors in the specimen that caused a false-negative amplification test
  3. Paucibacillary tuberculosis that the smears and the amplification test both missed
  4. Laboratory cross-contamination from another specimen during batch processing

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Commercial probes for the complex are highly specific, and a nontuberculous isolate would also have to explain the negative amplification test and the sterile liquid culture.
  • B. Inhibition is a real cause of false-negative amplification results, but the internal control was valid, and inhibition would not account for three negative smears or the absence of disease.
  • C. Low-burden disease does give negative smears and can give a negative amplification test, but the liquid culture stayed sterile and the clinical picture does not fit, so this explains the data less well.
  • D. Correct. A single slant with few colonies, negative smears, a negative amplification test and no clinical findings, in a batch that held a heavily positive specimen, fits transfer of bacilli during processing.

False-positive M. tuberculosis cultures arise from cross-contamination between specimens or cultures, mislabeling, specimen mix-ups and nonsterile reagents, and the error may be pre-analytic, analytic or post-analytic. The classic laboratory pattern is one culture out of a series positive with few colonies, growth on solid medium while the liquid culture stays sterile, negative smears and a negative amplification test, and a heavily smear-positive specimen processed in the same batch. Because a false positive drives isolation and months of therapy, the laboratory investigates with the tuberculosis control program, reviews processing records and genotyping, and may have to issue a corrected report. Prevention includes processing high-burden specimens separately, limiting batch size and using aerosol-free technique.

Sources: CDC Guidelines for Preventing TB Transmission in Health-Care Settings (2005)

Question 10

Over three weeks, tracheal aspirates from three ventilated infants in the neonatal intensive care unit grow the same oxidase-negative gram-negative rod. The colonies are not pigmented, are non-lactose fermenting on MacConkey agar at 24 hours and do not swarm on sheep blood agar. Results: DNase: positive Gelatin hydrolysis: positive Lysine decarboxylase: positive Ornithine decarboxylase: positive L-arabinose: not fermented Which organism is MOST likely?

  1. Serratia marcescens
  2. Enterobacter cloacae complex
  3. Proteus mirabilis
  4. Serratia liquefaciens

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A non-lactose-fermenting member of the Enterobacterales that is DNase, gelatinase, lysine and ornithine positive and does not ferment L-arabinose fits S. marcescens, even without its red pigment.
  • B. Enterobacter is a common cause of NICU infection and is ornithine positive, but it does not produce DNase or gelatinase and is lysine negative, so this pattern excludes it.
  • C. P. mirabilis also hydrolyzes gelatin and is ornithine positive, but it swarms across blood agar, usually produces H2S and is lysine decarboxylase negative.
  • D. S. liquefaciens shares DNase and gelatinase production, which makes it the closest near-miss, but it ferments L-arabinose, and this isolate does not.

Serratia marcescens is one of the few Enterobacterales that produces DNase, lipase and gelatinase. It is lysine and ornithine decarboxylase positive, usually does not ferment lactose and does not ferment L-arabinose, which separates it from S. liquefaciens. Its red pigment, prodigiosin, is often absent, may form only at room temperature and can be lost on subculture, so the biochemical pattern decides. The species carries a chromosomal AmpC beta-lactamase and causes outbreaks in neonatal units, so a cluster of the same isolate from three infants is reported to infection prevention according to policy.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)
