Question 1

In a hospital core laboratory, a knee aspirate from a patient with a history of gout is opaque and yellow-green with low viscosity. WBC 88,000/µL (88,000 × 10⁶/L) Neutrophils 93% Polarized microscopy: intracellular needle-shaped crystals, strongly negatively birefringent Gram stain: no organisms seen Which interpretation is BEST?

  1. Acute gout explains the findings, so culture can be canceled
  2. The crystals are likely an anticoagulant artifact; repeat the tap
  3. The negative Gram stain rules out a bacterial infection
  4. Infection is not excluded; culture results are still needed, as gout and infection can coexist

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Gout flares can reach very high counts, which makes this tempting, but crystal arthritis and infection coexist; canceling the culture could miss a septic joint.
  • B. Anticoagulant artifacts (such as calcium oxalate or lithium heparin crystals) are extracellular and do not mimic strongly negatively birefringent needles engulfed by neutrophils.
  • C. The Gram stain detects organisms in only about 60 to 80% of nongonococcal septic joints and fewer gonococcal ones, so a negative smear cannot exclude infection.
  • D. Correct. Crystals do not exclude coexisting septic arthritis, the count and appearance are in the septic range, and a negative Gram stain misses many infections.

Opaque yellow-green fluid with very low viscosity, a WBC count above 50,000/µL and more than 90% neutrophils is the septic pattern, even though gout can produce counts up to about 100,000/µL. The monosodium urate crystals confirm crystal disease but do not exclude coexisting infection, and the Gram stain is negative in a sizable share of septic joints, while culture is positive in more than 90% of nongonococcal infections. The laboratory reports the crystals and the count, keeps the culture going and follows the procedure for calling results that suggest septic arthritis.

Sources: AFP: Septic Arthritis (2021); Merck Manual: Acute Infectious Arthritis (2026)

Question 2

A post-vasectomy semen specimen collected 12 weeks after the procedure is examined fresh. The uncentrifuged wet preparation shows a few sperm across several high-power fields, some of them motile. Which report is MOST appropriate?

  1. Vasectomy successful; sperm under 1 × 10⁶/mL
  2. Centrifuge at 3,000 × g, then report motility from the pellet
  3. Azoospermia, because sperm are too few to count
  4. Sperm present, some motile; repeat testing advised

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A low concentration does not establish success when motile sperm are present; motile sperm indicate the vas may still be passing sperm.
  • B. High-speed centrifugation damages motility, so it is avoided when motile sperm are the question; motility is judged on the fresh, uncentrifuged specimen.
  • C. Azoospermia means no sperm at all; a count too low for an accurate concentration is reported as sperm seen, not as their absence.
  • D. Correct. Motile sperm 12 weeks after vasectomy mean sterility is not yet established; the report states their presence and motility so that repeat testing is arranged and other contraception continues.

Post-vasectomy testing, usually 8 to 16 weeks after the procedure, asks whether sperm, and especially motile sperm, are still in the ejaculate. Finding some sperm in early specimens is not unusual, so the laboratory reports exactly what it sees, including motility, and the patient keeps using other contraception until a specimen is free of sperm. Motility is assessed on the fresh uncentrifuged specimen because high-speed centrifugation lowers it; centrifugation is used to search the pellet for sperm when the wet preparation shows none.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 3

A reference laboratory receives a random urine from a 52-year-old patient with long-standing back and knee arthritis and a bluish-gray discoloration of the ear cartilage. The urine was pale yellow when voided but turned dark brown to black after several hours on the bench, and an aliquot made alkaline darkens within minutes. Results are: Reagent strip pH 7.0 Reagent strip glucose (glucose oxidase) negative Reagent strip blood negative, bilirubin negative Copper reduction tablet test positive Ferric chloride test: transient blue-green color Which substance MOST likely darkened the urine?

  1. Methemoglobin formed from hemoglobin in acid urine
  2. Melanin from oxidized melanogen in metastatic melanoma
  3. Porphobilin from oxidized porphobilinogen
  4. Homogentisic acid from alkaptonuria

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Hemoglobin standing in acid urine can oxidize to brown methemoglobin, but the blood pad is negative and the pH is 7.0, so there is no heme to oxidize.
  • B. Melanogen also darkens to black on standing, which makes it the near-miss, but melanin forms a gray-black precipitate with ferric chloride rather than a transient blue-green color, and the arthritis and dark ear cartilage point to ochronosis, not melanoma.
  • C. Porphobilinogen is colorless when voided and oxidizes on standing, but to a red to port-wine porphobilin rather than black, and it is detected with Ehrlich's reagent, not by copper reduction or ferric chloride.
  • D. Correct. Homogentisic acid oxidizes to a brown-black pigment on standing, faster in alkaline urine; it reduces copper while the glucose oxidase pad stays negative, gives a transient blue-green ferric chloride reaction, and its deposits in cartilage (ochronosis) explain the arthritis and dark ears.

Several substances are pale when voided and darken on standing, so the color change alone does not identify them. Homogentisic acid accumulates in alkaptonuria, an inherited deficiency of homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase in the tyrosine pathway, and oxidizes to a brown-black pigment, faster when the urine is alkaline. It is a reducing substance, so the copper reduction test is positive while the specific glucose oxidase pad stays negative, and it gives a transient blue-green color with ferric chloride. The oxidized pigment also deposits in cartilage and connective tissue (ochronosis), causing arthritis and dark ear cartilage. Melanogen darkens too but gives a gray-black ferric chloride precipitate. The laboratory reports the observation so quantitative homogentisic acid testing can confirm it.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.

Question 4

A urine refrigerated overnight in specimen processing is pink and turbid. Strip results: pH 5.5, specific gravity 1.028, otherwise negative. On microscopy, a dense layer of fine yellow-brown granules with no distinct crystal shape covers most of each field and hides any cells or casts. What is the BEST next step?

  1. Reject it and request a freshly voided specimen
  2. Warm an aliquot and examine it again
  3. Add dilute acetic acid to dissolve the precipitate
  4. Report moderate uric acid crystals and finish

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because the field is unreadable, but refrigeration is the recommended way to hold urine and this artifact can be cleared in the laboratory, so recollection only delays the result.
  • B. Correct. Amorphous urates precipitate in cold, acidic, concentrated urine and redissolve on warming, which clears the field so cells and casts can be counted.
  • C. Acid dissolves amorphous phosphates, which form in alkaline urine; acid makes urates less soluble and can convert them to uric acid crystals.
  • D. Uric acid crystals have distinct rhombic or rosette shapes; amorphous granules are a storage artifact, and reporting them misses whatever they hide.

Refrigeration preserves urine but lowers the solubility of urates and phosphates. In acidic, concentrated urine, amorphous urates settle as yellow-brown granules, and the pigment uroerythrin often colors the sediment pink (brick dust). Warming an aliquot redissolves them; amorphous phosphates in alkaline urine do not dissolve with heat but do dissolve in dilute acid. Many laboratories keep a separate unrefrigerated aliquot for crystal identification so that storage precipitates are not mistaken for clinically meaningful crystals.

Sources: AFP: Urinalysis Review (2005); Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.

Question 5

A hospital core laboratory receives paired pleural fluid and serum from a patient with an effusion of unknown cause. The laboratory's upper reference limit for serum LDH is 225 U/L. Pleural fluid total protein 2.7 g/dL (27 g/L) Serum total protein 6.2 g/dL (62 g/L) Pleural fluid LDH 168 U/L Serum LDH 214 U/L Using Light's criteria, which classification is BEST supported?

  1. Indeterminate until the protein and LDH criteria agree with each other
  2. Transudate, because the protein ratio is under 0.5
  3. Exudate, because both LDH criteria are met
  4. Transudate, because fluid protein is below 3.0 g/dL (30 g/L)

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Discordant criteria are common and expected. Light's criteria are designed so that any single positive criterion indicates an exudate; agreement between them is not required.
  • B. The protein ratio is 2.7 ÷ 6.2 = 0.44, below 0.5, but Light's criteria need only one criterion to call an exudate; a normal protein ratio does not override the LDH results.
  • C. Correct. The fluid-to-serum LDH ratio is 168 ÷ 214 = 0.79 (at least 0.6), and the fluid LDH of 168 U/L exceeds two thirds of the upper reference limit (150 U/L). Meeting any one criterion classifies the fluid as an exudate.
  • D. A fluid protein of at least 3 g/dL (30 g/L) suggests an exudate, but a value below it does not establish a transudate when the paired LDH criteria are met.

Light's criteria call a pleural effusion an exudate when at least one of three is met: fluid-to-serum total protein ratio of 0.5 or more, fluid-to-serum LDH ratio of 0.6 or more, or fluid LDH above two thirds of the upper limit of normal for serum. Here the protein ratio is 0.44, but the LDH ratio is 0.79 and the fluid LDH (168 U/L) is above 150 U/L, so two criteria are met. The criteria are very sensitive for exudates (about 98%) but misclassify roughly a fifth of transudates, which is why a serum-minus-pleural protein gradient above 3.1 g/dL can be used when a transudate is clinically suspected and results sit just over the cutoffs.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Pleural Effusion (2025)

Question 6

An outpatient clinic laboratory receives serum and ascitic fluid drawn at the same visit from a patient with new ascites. Serum albumin 3.1 g/dL (31 g/L) Ascitic fluid albumin 1.5 g/dL (15 g/L) Ascitic fluid total protein 2.0 g/dL (20 g/L) Which interpretation is MOST appropriate?

  1. SAAG 4.6 g/dL (46 g/L), consistent with right heart failure
  2. SAAG 1.6 g/dL (16 g/L), consistent with portal hypertension
  3. SAAG 0.5 g/dL (5 g/L), consistent with nephrotic syndrome
  4. SAAG 1.6 g/dL (16 g/L), consistent with peritoneal carcinomatosis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. This adds the two albumins instead of subtracting the fluid value from the serum value; the gradient is a difference.
  • B. Correct. 3.1 − 1.5 = 1.6 g/dL, which is at least 1.1 g/dL and points to portal hypertension (cirrhosis, massive liver metastases or right heart failure).
  • C. This subtracts fluid albumin from fluid total protein. The gradient uses serum albumin minus ascitic albumin drawn at the same time.
  • D. The arithmetic is right, but peritoneal carcinomatosis causes ascites by peritoneal leak and gives a gradient below 1.1 g/dL, not a high one.

The serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG) is serum albumin minus ascitic fluid albumin, from specimens collected the same day: 3.1 − 1.5 = 1.6 g/dL (16 g/L). A gradient of 1.1 g/dL or more indicates portal hypertension, as in cirrhosis, massive hepatic metastases or right heart failure; a gradient below 1.1 g/dL points to causes that do not involve portal hypertension, such as peritoneal carcinomatosis, tuberculous peritonitis, pancreatic ascites or nephrotic syndrome. The SAAG replaced the transudate and exudate terms for ascites because it classifies the fluid more accurately.

Sources: Khan & Linganna, Ascites, SBP and HRS (CCJM 2023)

Question 7

A semen specimen for fertility testing arrives in specimen processing 2 hours after collection. The patient says it was kept in a car in freezing weather on the way in. Which action is MOST appropriate?

  1. Reject it and request a new collection
  2. Report motility with a comment on delay
  3. Analyze it and correct motility for the 2-hour delay with a decay factor
  4. Warm it to 37 °C, then assess motility

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The specimen exceeded the 1-hour delivery window and was exposed to cold, both of which lower motility and vitality, so the results would not reflect the patient.
  • B. A disclaimer does not make an unreliable motility usable for a fertility decision; the laboratory's rejection criteria apply.
  • C. No validated correction factor exists for motility lost to delay and cold; results from a compromised specimen cannot be adjusted to true values.
  • D. Rewarming does not reverse cold shock or the loss of motility over 2 hours; the result would still underestimate motility.

Motility, the most time- and temperature-sensitive semen parameter, should be assessed promptly after liquefaction and within about 60 minutes of ejaculation. Specimens are kept near room or body temperature during transport, and exposure to extreme temperature is a rejection criterion. A specimen that is both late and chilled gives falsely low motility and vitality, which could misclassify the patient. The correct step is to reject it per procedure, document the reason, and give the patient collection instructions: 2 to 7 days of abstinence, a sterile container, the whole ejaculate, no lubricant, and delivery within 1 hour.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 8

Pericardial fluid from a patient with a persistent effusion and suspected tuberculous pericarditis arrives in specimen processing. Which test on the fluid can confirm this diagnosis?

  1. Fluid-to-serum protein and LDH ratios
  2. Gram stain and routine aerobic culture
  3. Culture for acid-fast bacilli
  4. Cytology for malignant cells

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. These ratios can show that the fluid is an exudate, but many causes produce an exudate, so they cannot name tuberculosis as the cause.
  • B. Routine bacterial methods detect purulent pericarditis; mycobacteria stain poorly with the Gram stain and need special media and long incubation.
  • C. Correct. Growth of Mycobacterium tuberculosis in culture of the pericardial fluid (or tissue) confirms tuberculous pericarditis; a negative culture does not fully exclude it because yield is limited.
  • D. Cytology is sent on pericardial fluid to look for malignancy, a major cause of persistent effusions, but it cannot identify a mycobacterial infection.

Pericardial fluid is usually sent for cell count, chemistry, cytology and cultures, but apart from culture and cytology most results are nonspecific. When tuberculosis is possible, acid-fast stains and mycobacterial cultures are essential, because growth of the organism in culture of the fluid establishes tuberculous pericarditis. The specimen should go to the microbiology laboratory for mycobacterial processing under the laboratory's biosafety procedure, since Mycobacterium tuberculosis cultures are handled in a biosafety level 3 setting.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Pericarditis (2026)

Question 9

An emergency department patient with fever, headache and neck stiffness received an antibiotic at an urgent care clinic 18 hours before lumbar puncture. CSF results are: Appearance: cloudy WBC 2,400/µL (2,400 × 10⁶/L), 90% neutrophils Glucose 22 mg/dL (1.2 mmol/L); simultaneous serum glucose 110 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L) Protein 280 mg/dL (2,800 mg/L) Gram stain of the cytocentrifuged sediment: no organisms seen Which interpretation is BEST?

  1. Bacterial meningitis remains likely despite the negative Gram stain
  2. Viral meningitis, because no organisms were seen
  3. Cryptococcal meningitis; an India ink preparation is the next step
  4. A traumatic tap explains the cell count and protein

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Thousands of neutrophils, a CSF/serum glucose ratio of 0.2 and protein near 300 mg/dL are a bacterial pattern; prior antibiotics lower the yield of both the Gram stain and culture.
  • B. Viral meningitis usually has a few hundred cells that are mostly lymphocytes after the first day or two, a normal glucose and a milder protein rise.
  • C. Cryptococcus lowers glucose, but it usually brings a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis, not 2,400 cells that are 90% neutrophils.
  • D. Blood from a traumatic tap adds white cells and protein in proportion to red cells, but it does not drop the CSF glucose to a fifth of the serum value.

Normal CSF has 0 to 5 cells/µL, glucose 50 to 80 mg/dL (about two thirds of serum) and protein 15 to 45 mg/dL. In bacterial meningitis the count is often in the thousands with neutrophils predominating, glucose falls below half of serum (a ratio under 0.23 strongly suggests bacteria), and protein is commonly 100 to 500 mg/dL. The Gram stain needs about 10⁵ organisms/mL and loses sensitivity after antibiotics, so a negative stain does not exclude bacteria. Culture is still set up, and antigen tests or multiplex PCR may help.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Acute Bacterial Meningitis (2026)

Question 10

A reference laboratory receives a urine specimen from an outpatient clinic 7 hours after collection. It was not refrigerated and contains no preservative. Results are: Appearance cloudy, strong ammonia odor pH 8.5 Nitrite positive, leukocyte esterase negative Glucose negative, bilirubin negative Microscopic: many bacteria, WBC 0-2/HPF with several cell ghosts, many amorphous phosphates Which action should the laboratory scientist take?

  1. Report the results with a comment that they suggest a urinary tract infection
  2. Reject the specimen and request a fresh, properly stored one
  3. Refrigerate the specimen and repeat the strip test the next morning
  4. Add a boric acid preservative now and test the specimen within the hour

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The nitrite and bacteria look like infection, but bacteria multiply and convert nitrate to nitrite in a specimen left warm for hours, so these results describe the container rather than the patient.
  • B. Correct. The specimen exceeded the time limit for unrefrigerated, unpreserved urine, and its pattern (alkaline pH, ammonia odor, bacterial overgrowth, lysed cells, precipitated phosphates) shows the changes of delay, so the results cannot be reported reliably.
  • C. Cooling slows further change but does not undo it, and a longer delay adds more phosphate and urate precipitation to an already compromised specimen.
  • D. A preservative must be added at collection; adding it after 7 hours cannot reverse bacterial growth, pH change or cell lysis that have already happened.

Urine should be examined within about 2 hours of collection, or be refrigerated or preserved. At room temperature, bacteria multiply and split urea to ammonia, raising the pH and producing the ammonia odor; alkaline, dilute urine lyses white cells and casts; phosphates precipitate and cloud the specimen; and nitrate-reducing bacteria turn a negative nitrite positive. Glucose can fall as bacteria consume it, and bilirubin and urobilinogen degrade in light. Because the result pattern reflects the delay, the correct step is to reject the specimen under the laboratory's rejection criteria and request a properly handled recollection, documenting the reason.

Sources: AFP: Urinalysis Review (2005); 42 CFR Part 493 (CLIA regulations)
