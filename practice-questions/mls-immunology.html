Question 1

An emergency department patient with fever, rash and pharyngitis 3 weeks after a new sexual partner is tested on the night shift with the laboratory HIV algorithm: HIV-1/2 antigen/antibody (4th-generation) immunoassay: reactive HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation immunoassay: HIV-1 negative, HIV-2 negative HIV-1 RNA (NAT): detected, 1,800,000 copies/mL Which interpretation is CORRECT?

  1. Established HIV-1 infection with a weak antibody response
  2. HIV-2 infection missed by the differentiation assay
  3. Acute HIV-1 infection, before HIV-1 antibody is detectable
  4. False-reactive screening result; HIV infection is excluded

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Established infection gives a positive HIV-1 differentiation result. Negative antibody with a positive RNA places this patient in the window before seroconversion.
  • B. The HIV-1 RNA test detects HIV-1, not HIV-2, so a positive NAT supports HIV-1. HIV-2 would be suspected with a reactive screen, negative HIV-1 RNA and an HIV-2 reactive or indeterminate differentiation result.
  • C. Correct. The 4th-generation screen detected p24 antigen before antibody formed, the differentiation assay is still negative, and NAT confirms HIV-1 with the high viral load typical of acute infection.
  • D. A negative differentiation assay alone could suggest a false-reactive screen, but a detectable HIV-1 RNA with a very high load confirms real infection.

The recommended laboratory algorithm screens with a 4th-generation antigen/antibody immunoassay, which detects HIV-1 p24 antigen as well as HIV-1 and HIV-2 antibodies. A reactive screen goes to an HIV-1/HIV-2 differentiation immunoassay. When that is negative or indeterminate, an HIV-1 NAT resolves it: detected RNA means acute HIV-1 infection, while undetected RNA suggests a false-reactive screen. After exposure, RNA appears first (about 10 days), then p24 antigen a few days later, then antibody several weeks after exposure, which explains this pattern in a febrile patient 3 weeks out.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 2

An outpatient rheumatology clinic sends serum from a patient with Raynaud phenomenon and tight, thickened skin of the fingers. On HEp-2 indirect immunofluorescence at 1:1,280, each interphase nucleus contains about 50 discrete, coarse, evenly sized speckles scattered through the nucleus. In metaphase cells the speckles line up within the condensed chromosome mass. Which disease is this pattern MOST closely associated with?

  1. Limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis
  2. Mixed connective tissue disease with high-titer anti-U1 RNP
  3. Drug-induced lupus with antihistone antibodies
  4. Primary Sjögren disease with anti-Ro60 and anti-La antibodies

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. About 40 to 80 discrete speckles in interphase that align with the chromosomes in mitosis is the centromere pattern, which is most strongly associated with limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis.
  • B. Anti-U1 RNP gives a large, coarse speckled pattern with many irregular speckles and an unstained metaphase plate, not a countable number of discrete dots.
  • C. Antihistone antibodies produce a homogeneous pattern that stains the whole nucleus and the metaphase chromatin evenly, not discrete speckles.
  • D. Anti-Ro60 and anti-La give fine, tiny speckles throughout the nucleoplasm with an unstained mitotic chromatin mass, unlike these countable dots.

The International Consensus on ANA Patterns calls this AC-3, the centromere pattern: roughly 40 to 80 discrete coarse speckles per interphase nucleus that line up within the chromatin mass of mitotic cells, because the target antigens (CENP-A, CENP-B and CENP-C) sit on each chromosome's centromere. It is strongly associated with limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis (the CREST spectrum), and in a patient with Raynaud phenomenon it predicts later development of that disease. It is also seen in a subset of primary biliary cholangitis. A low-titer centromere pattern should be confirmed with an antigen-specific CENP-B immunoassay.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 3

A reference laboratory measures IgM and IgG antibody to the same protein antigen in two patients on days 0, 7 and 14 after a documented exposure. Patient A: IgM negative, rising by day 7, highest on day 14; IgG negative on day 0 and 7, low positive on day 14 Patient B: IgM negative throughout or barely detectable; IgG low positive on day 0, rising steeply by day 7 to a titer far above patient A's day 14 value Which explanation BEST accounts for patient B's pattern?

  1. A primary response in which this antigen drives IgG synthesis before IgM
  2. A T-cell-independent response to a repeating polysaccharide on the antigen
  3. Antibody from immune globulin given on the day of exposure
  4. Reactivated memory B cells that had already switched to IgG

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. In a primary response IgM comes first and IgG follows after class switching, which is what patient A shows; an IgG-first primary response is not the expected pattern.
  • B. T-independent antigens drive mainly IgM with weak class switching and little memory, the opposite of a large, fast IgG response.
  • C. Passive immune globulin raises IgG at once and then the level falls with the half-life of IgG; it does not produce a steep rise between day 0 and day 7.
  • D. Correct. A secondary response from memory B cells has a short lag, a much higher peak and is dominated by IgG, which fits a rising IgG with little IgM.

Patient A shows a primary response: a lag of several days, IgM produced first, then a smaller and later IgG response after helper T cells drive class switching. Patient B shows a secondary (anamnestic) response: a low baseline IgG from earlier exposure or vaccination, a short lag, a much higher peak titer, and IgG as the main antibody, because memory B cells that had already switched isotype and matured in affinity are reactivated. Paired sera interpreted this way help separate a new infection (IgM first) from re-exposure or a booster, where IgM may stay low or undetectable.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 4

On night shift, the core laboratory runs a one-step sandwich immunoassay for serum hCG on a patient with heavy vaginal bleeding and a uterus much larger than expected for 11 weeks of pregnancy. The analytic measuring range is 2 to 10,000 mIU/mL (IU/L). Because the undiluted value looked low for the clinical picture, the specimen was run in a dilution series. Readings before the dilution factor is applied: Undiluted: 640 mIU/mL 1:10: 3,900 mIU/mL 1:100: 2,450 mIU/mL 1:1,000: 246 mIU/mL Which hCG result should the laboratory report?

  1. 39,000 mIU/mL, from the lowest dilution inside the measuring range
  2. About 245,000 mIU/mL, where the dilutions agree
  3. No value until heterophile antibody blocking tubes are tested
  4. 640 mIU/mL, since the undiluted reading is inside the measuring range

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because labs often report the lowest in-range dilution, but the 1:100 dilution gives a far higher corrected value, so the 1:10 tube was still in the hook zone and underestimates the hCG.
  • B. Correct. Corrected values are 640, 39,000, 245,000 and 246,000 mIU/mL; the 1:100 and 1:1,000 results agree, so the antigen excess has been diluted out and about 245,000 mIU/mL is the true concentration.
  • C. Heterophile antibodies usually raise a sandwich result falsely, and that value falls out of proportion on dilution; here the corrected results rise and then agree, which is the hook effect, not interference.
  • D. An in-range reading is not proof of accuracy in a one-step sandwich assay; corrected results that rise on dilution show the undiluted value is falsely low from antigen excess.

In a one-step sandwich immunoassay the capture and labeled antibodies meet the specimen together. When hCG is in great excess, free hCG fills the capture sites and separately occupies the labeled antibody, so fewer complete sandwiches reach the solid phase and the signal falls: the high-dose hook effect. Multiplying each reading by its dilution factor gives 640, 39,000, 245,000 and 246,000 mIU/mL. The corrected value keeps rising until the 1:100 tube and then holds steady at 1:1,000, so the true result is about 245,000 mIU/mL, and the 1:10 tube was still hooked. A value this high fits gestational trophoblastic disease; the laboratory reports it with the dilution documented.

Sources: ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 5

A public health laboratory tests serum from a dairy farm worker with 2 weeks of fever and sweats. The Brucella standard tube agglutination test is run in parallel with and without 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME) in the diluent: Standard agglutination titer 1:640 2-ME agglutination titer 1:20 (laboratory interpretation: 1:20 or lower is negative) Which interpretation is BEST supported?

  1. The agglutinating antibody is mostly IgM, typical of early infection
  2. Incomplete blocking antibodies are present and need an antiglobulin method to be detected
  3. Most of the agglutinating antibody is IgG, consistent with long-standing infection
  4. Antibody excess caused a prozone in the 2-ME tubes

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. 2-ME breaks the disulfide bonds that hold the IgM pentamer together, so a titer that disappears with 2-ME was IgM, typical of the first weeks of infection.
  • B. Blocking antibodies cause a falsely low standard titer, mainly in chronic disease; here the standard titer is high.
  • C. This reverses the test: 2-ME spares IgG, so an IgG-dominant serum would keep its titer after treatment.
  • D. Prozone causes missing agglutination at low dilutions with a positive reaction at higher ones; here the 2-ME series is negative beyond 1:20, not only at the start.

2-Mercaptoethanol and dithiothreitol reduce disulfide bonds and destroy the agglutinating activity of IgM, while IgG stays active, so the titer left after treatment reflects IgG. A high standard titer that falls to negative with 2-ME means the response is still mostly IgM, as in an early primary response. Because IgG appears later, the 2-ME test becomes positive later and is used to follow therapy, and a standard titer of 1:160 or higher supports the diagnosis when it fits the clinical picture.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 6

At 13:10 a patient in the emergency department develops hives, wheezing and hypotension 10 minutes after a wasp sting. The emergency physician calls the laboratory at 13:30 asking which specimen will BEST document mast-cell activation for this episode. Which specimen should the laboratory recommend?

  1. Serum tryptase collected within about 2 hours of onset
  2. Total serum IgE collected the next morning
  3. Venom-specific IgE drawn now to confirm the reaction
  4. CBC with an absolute eosinophil count collected today

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Tryptase released from mast-cell granules peaks in the first hours of anaphylaxis, so a specimen drawn within about 2 hours documents mast-cell activation.
  • B. Total IgE reflects atopy in general and is raised by many conditions; it does not show that mast cells degranulated during this reaction.
  • C. Venom-specific IgE identifies sensitization and is useful later, but it does not document that this episode involved mast-cell release, and results shortly after a reaction can be falsely low.
  • D. Eosinophilia is more typical of chronic allergic disease and parasitic infection; an acute sting reaction does not reliably change the count.

A type I (immediate) hypersensitivity reaction occurs when allergen cross-links IgE bound to mast cells and basophils, releasing histamine, tryptase and other mediators within minutes. Histamine clears too quickly to be practical, but tryptase stays elevated for several hours and is most useful when drawn within about 2 hours of symptom onset. Comparing it with a baseline tryptase after recovery strengthens the interpretation, and a persistently high baseline suggests a mast cell disorder. A normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis, especially with food triggers. Venom-specific IgE and skin testing identify the allergen later but do not document the acute event.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Anaphylaxis (2026); WAO Anaphylaxis Guidance (2020); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 7

A reference laboratory measures IgG subclasses by rate nephelometry for a patient with painless swelling of both submandibular glands and autoimmune pancreatitis on imaging. Results: Total IgG 3,150 mg/dL (31.5 g/L); reference 700 to 1,600 mg/dL IgG4 98 mg/dL (0.98 g/L); upper reference limit 135 mg/dL (1.35 g/L) The instrument ran the IgG4 at its standard single dilution. What should the laboratory scientist do NEXT?

  1. Report the IgG4 as within the reference interval
  2. Repeat the IgG4 at higher dilutions until the corrected result stops changing
  3. Repeat the undiluted sample with a longer reading time to allow more complexes to form
  4. Recommend serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation to look for an IgG monoclonal protein before reporting

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Reporting is premature: a nephelometric result can read falsely low when the antigen far exceeds the reagent antibody, and the clinical picture suggests very high IgG4.
  • B. Correct. Diluting moves the reaction back to the antibody-excess side of the precipitin curve; a result that rises on dilution and then stabilizes is the true value.
  • C. More time does not fix antigen excess; past the zone of equivalence the complexes stay small and soluble and scatter less light.
  • D. A high total IgG can prompt an electrophoresis, but that does not resolve whether the IgG4 value itself is falsely low from antigen excess.

Nephelometry measures light scattered by antigen-antibody complexes. Along the precipitin curve, scatter rises with antigen while antibody is in excess, peaks at equivalence, then falls in antigen excess as small soluble complexes form, so a very high analyte can read as normal or low. IgG4-related disease, suggested here by salivary gland swelling, autoimmune pancreatitis and a high total IgG, is a known setting for falsely low nephelometric IgG4. Serial dilution until the corrected value stabilizes gives the true concentration; many analyzers also run an antigen-excess check.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 8

A 2-year-old boy has had four episodes of otitis media and one pneumonia since his maternal antibody waned. The immunology laboratory reports (age-matched reference in parentheses): IgG 90 mg/dL (0.9 g/L) (400 to 1,300 mg/dL) IgA less than 7 mg/dL (less than 0.07 g/L) (20 to 100 mg/dL) IgM less than 5 mg/dL (less than 0.05 g/L) (40 to 150 mg/dL) Flow cytometry: CD3+ T cells 78% (53 to 75%), CD19+ B cells 0.5% (15 to 30%), CD16+/CD56+ NK cells 14% (5 to 20%) Which condition do these results MOST strongly suggest?

  1. Severe combined immunodeficiency with absent T and B cells
  2. X-linked (Bruton) agammaglobulinemia from a Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) defect
  3. Selective IgA deficiency
  4. Transient hypogammaglobulinemia of infancy

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. SCID would show low or absent T cells and usually presents earlier with opportunistic infections; here T cells are present at a normal to high percentage.
  • B. Correct. A boy with pyogenic infections after 6 months, all immunoglobulin classes low and B cells under 2% fits a BTK defect that blocks B-cell maturation.
  • C. Selective IgA deficiency has low IgA with normal IgG, IgM and B-cell numbers; here every class is low and B cells are nearly absent.
  • D. Tempting because of the age and low IgG, but in this condition B-cell numbers are normal and IgA and IgM are usually not absent.

X-linked agammaglobulinemia is caused by mutations in Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK), which pre-B cells need to mature, so circulating CD19+ B cells are absent or below about 2% and IgG, IgA and IgM are all very low. Boys are protected by maternal IgG for the first months, then develop recurrent otitis, sinopulmonary infections and pneumonia with encapsulated bacteria. T cells and NK cells are normal, and the T-cell percentage can look high simply because B cells are missing. Confirmation is by intracellular BTK flow cytometry or genetic testing.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 9

A patient with known systemic lupus erythematosus has new proteinuria. The serum was separated within 1 hour and frozen. Results (laboratory reference in parentheses): C3 52 mg/dL (0.52 g/L) (90 to 180 mg/dL) C4 4 mg/dL (0.04 g/L) (10 to 40 mg/dL) CH50 low but measurable Anti-dsDNA titer increased from the last visit Which process BEST explains the complement results?

  1. Classical pathway consumption by circulating DNA-anti-DNA immune complexes
  2. Hereditary deficiency of functional C1 esterase inhibitor
  3. Loss of hemolytic activity from complement decay during storage at room temperature
  4. Alternative pathway activation driven by C3 nephritic factor

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Immune complexes of IgG and IgM bind C1 and consume C4, C2 and C3, so C3, C4 and CH50 all fall during active lupus nephritis.
  • B. C1 inhibitor deficiency lowers C4 but usually leaves C3 normal, and it does not explain rising anti-dsDNA.
  • C. Storage loss lowers functional CH50 but not the measured C3 and C4 protein, and this serum was frozen promptly.
  • D. C3 nephritic factor consumes C3 through the alternative pathway but spares C4, which is markedly low here.

The classical pathway starts when C1q binds antigen-antibody complexes, and C1s then cleaves C4 and C2 to form the C3 convertase C4b2a. In active SLE, circulating DNA-anti-DNA immune complexes drive this pathway continuously, so C4 and C3 are consumed faster than the liver can replace them and the CH50 falls. A low C4 with a low C3 points to classical (or lectin) activation, while a low C3 with normal C4 points to the alternative pathway. Falling complement with a rising anti-dsDNA titer is a marker of lupus activity, often renal.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 10

A patient who has taken hydralazine for 18 months develops arthralgia and pleuritic chest pain. Results: HEp-2 ANA 1:320, smooth even staining of the whole interphase nucleus, with the metaphase chromatin also stained Anti-dsDNA negative Anti-Sm negative C3 and C4 within reference intervals Urinalysis negative for protein and blood Which antibody is MOST likely responsible for the ANA result?

  1. Anti-Sm antibody below the detection limit of the immunoassay
  2. Antihistone antibody against chromatin proteins
  3. Anti-centromere antibody
  4. Anti-Scl-70 (topoisomerase I) antibody

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Anti-Sm produces a coarse speckled pattern, not a homogeneous one, and it is a marker of idiopathic SLE rather than drug-induced disease.
  • B. Correct. Antihistone antibodies give a homogeneous pattern and are found in most patients with drug-induced lupus from hydralazine or procainamide.
  • C. Anti-centromere antibody produces 40 to 80 discrete speckles per nucleus and is linked to limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis, not to a drug reaction.
  • D. Anti-Scl-70 marks diffuse systemic sclerosis and produces a fine granular nuclear pattern with nucleolar staining, which does not match this case.

Drug-induced lupus follows exposure to drugs such as hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid and TNF inhibitors and usually causes arthralgia and serositis with little kidney or central nervous system disease. Antihistone antibodies, which bind the histone proteins of chromatin, produce a homogeneous HEp-2 pattern that also stains the condensed metaphase chromatin. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm, the SLE-specific markers, are usually negative, and complement is typically normal because immune-complex nephritis is uncommon. The disease generally resolves after the drug is stopped, which is why identifying the pattern matters for the patient.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.
