Question 1 · Blood Banking

At 20 weeks' gestation, an outpatient prenatal sample from a group A, D-positive patient (phenotype D+ C+ E0 c0 e+) has anti-c with a titer of 64; her partner types c positive. The obstetric clinic notes that she is D positive and asks whether the antibody matters for the pregnancy. Which reply from the blood bank is MOST accurate?

  1. It affects her transfusion choices but not the pregnancy, since she is D positive
  2. Anti-c can cause severe HDFN and is followed like anti-D
  3. Rh immune globulin at 28 weeks will suppress the anti-c
  4. Anti-c is usually IgM, so it rarely reaches the fetus

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. D status matters only for anti-D. Anti-c targets a different Rh antigen, and a c-positive fetus is at risk whatever the mother's D type.
  • B. Correct. Anti-c is an IgG antibody that crosses the placenta and can cause severe hemolytic disease. Being D positive does not protect the fetus from antibodies to other Rh antigens.
  • C. Rh immune globulin is anti-D. It does not prevent or suppress antibodies to c or any other antigen, and it is contraindicated in a D-positive patient.
  • D. Rh antibodies, including anti-c, are typically IgG, the class that crosses the placenta. IgM antibodies such as most anti-Lewis antibodies are the ones that do not cause HDFN.

Besides D, many antigens can cause HDFN, including c, C, E and e in the Rh system and Kell, Duffy and Kidd. Anti-c is among the most important after anti-D and anti-K. A c-negative mother can make anti-c whatever her D type, so a D-positive phenotype offers no reassurance. The laboratory identifies the antibody, titers it serially, and reports when the titer reaches the laboratory's critical value (typically 1:8 to 1:32), after which the fetus is followed with middle cerebral artery Doppler. Testing the partner for c helps predict whether the fetus is at risk. Rh immune globulin prevents only anti-D.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Neonate (2024); RhoGAM Ultra-Filtered PLUS label (2025)

Question 2 · Blood Banking

A 71-year-old patient with plasma cell myeloma (total protein 11.4 g/dL [114 g/L]; reference range 6.0 to 8.3 g/dL [60 to 83 g/L]) needs crossmatched red cells. ABO results at immediate spin: Anti-A 4+ Anti-B 0 A1 cells 2+ B cells 3+ Group O screening cells 2+ Autocontrol 2+ Under the microscope, the red cells in the reverse tubes are stacked like coins. What is the BEST next step to resolve the reverse grouping?

  1. Incubate the reverse tubes at 4 °C for 15 minutes
  2. Repeat the reverse grouping with prewarmed plasma and cells
  3. Repeat the reverse grouping using saline replacement
  4. Perform an adsorption-elution with anti-A on the patient's cells

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Cold incubation strengthens weak or missing ABO antibodies, a problem in infants and immunodeficient patients. It would not disperse rouleaux and could add cold autoantibody reactions.
  • B. Prewarming removes reactivity from cold autoantibodies, which can also make O cells and the autocontrol react. The coin-stack appearance and very high protein point to rouleaux instead.
  • C. Correct. Rouleaux disperses when the patient's plasma is removed and replaced with saline, while true agglutination stays, so the real anti-B can be read.
  • D. Adsorption-elution detects weak A or B antigens on red cells. The forward grouping here is clear-cut; the problem lies in the plasma.

High concentrations of abnormal protein, as in myeloma or after high-molecular-weight plasma expanders, make red cells stick together in stacks that look like coins. In the reverse grouping, the patient's own plasma is tested, so every cell, including A1, O and autologous cells, appears agglutinated. Saline replacement removes the plasma after centrifugation, adds saline, and remixes: rouleaux disperses and true agglutination remains. Forward grouping is usually unaffected when the patient's cells are washed before testing. With rouleaux resolved, this patient is expected to show anti-B only, consistent with group A.

Sources: ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026); Circular of Information for Blood and Blood Components (2024)

Question 3 · Blood Banking

Cord blood from a pale, jaundiced newborn arrives in a hospital blood bank. The mother is group O, D negative, with anti-D at a titer of 1,024. Cord results: Anti-A 4+, anti-B 0 Anti-D (monoclonal IgM), immediate spin: 0 Rh control, immediate spin: 0 DAT anti-IgG 4+, anti-C3d 0 Eluate: anti-D What BEST explains the infant's D typing?

  1. Wharton's jelly in the cord sample masking agglutination
  2. Maternal D-negative red cells diluting the cord sample
  3. The infant is D negative and the DAT reflects maternal anti-A
  4. Maternal anti-D occupying the infant's D sites

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Wharton's jelly causes false-positive aggregation in unwashed cord samples, not false-negative typing. Washing the cells removes it.
  • B. A cord sample is fetal blood. Maternal cells entering the fetus are few, and they would produce a mixed-field reaction, not a negative type with a 4+ DAT.
  • C. Passive anti-A can make the DAT positive in a group A infant of a group O mother, but the eluate here is anti-D. Anti-D cannot be eluted from cells that lack D.
  • D. Correct. Heavy coating with maternal IgG anti-D can leave no free D sites for the typing reagent, so D-positive cells type D negative. The anti-D eluate proves the infant's cells carry D.

Blocked D is a rare false-negative D type in a newborn with severe anti-D HDFN: maternal IgG anti-D saturates the infant's D antigen sites, so the IgM typing reagent finds nothing to bind. The clue is the conflict between a D-negative type and a strongly positive IgG DAT whose eluate is anti-D. The laboratory resolves it by removing the bound antibody, with gentle heat elution or chloroquine treatment, and retyping, or by RHD genotyping, and it reports the infant as D positive. The mother, already immunized, is not a candidate for postpartum Rh immune globulin.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Neonate (2024)

Question 4 · Urinalysis and Body Fluids

A patient is brought to the emergency department after being found on the floor at home about 18 hours after a fall. The urine is red-brown and clear. Results are: Reagent strip blood 3+ Reagent strip protein trace Microscopic RBC 0-2/HPF Heparinized plasma: straw colored, not pink Haptoglobin 150 mg/dL (1.5 g/L) CK 48,000 U/L (800 µkat/L; reference 30-200 U/L, 0.5-3.3 µkat/L) Which pigment MOST likely accounts for the urine color?

  1. Free hemoglobin filtered after intravascular hemolysis
  2. Porphobilinogen oxidized to porphobilin on standing
  3. Myoglobin released from injured skeletal muscle fibers (rhabdomyolysis)
  4. Conjugated bilirubin from an obstructed bile duct

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because hemoglobin also gives a positive heme pad with no intact red cells, but hemolysis large enough to discolor urine leaves the plasma pink and consumes haptoglobin; here the plasma is straw colored and haptoglobin is normal.
  • B. Porphyrin precursors can darken urine to red or brown, but they have no peroxidase-like activity, so they would not explain a 3+ blood reaction or the CK of 48,000 U/L.
  • C. Correct. The heme pad reacts with myoglobin, red cells are absent, the plasma is clear, haptoglobin is normal and the CK is markedly raised, which together point to rhabdomyolysis from prolonged immobilization.
  • D. Bilirubin gives an amber to brown urine with yellow foam, but it does not react on the blood pad and nothing in this result set points to liver or biliary disease.

The reagent strip blood pad detects the peroxidase-like activity of heme, so intact red cells, free hemoglobin and myoglobin all react. Microscopy separates true hematuria (red cells present) from pigmenturia (heme pad positive, red cells absent). The plasma then separates the two pigments: hemoglobin binds haptoglobin and, once haptoglobin is saturated, stains the plasma pink, whereas myoglobin is small, binds little protein and is cleared quickly into the urine, leaving the plasma clear. A CK more than five times the upper limit after a long lie confirms muscle injury, which the laboratory should report promptly because myoglobin is toxic to the tubules.

Sources: AFP: Urinalysis Review (2005); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 5 · Chemistry

An emergency department sends specimens on an intoxicated patient found beside an empty bottle of windshield washer fluid. Results: Sodium 138 mmol/L Chloride 100 mmol/L Total CO2 24 mmol/L Glucose 90 mg/dL (5.0 mmol/L) BUN 14 mg/dL (5.0 mmol/L) Ethanol 230 mg/dL (49.9 mmol/L) Measured osmolality (freezing point depression) 366 mOsm/kg Using calculated osmolality = 2 × Na + glucose/18 + BUN/2.8 + ethanol/4.6 (all in mg/dL except sodium) and an anion gap reference of 8–16 mmol/L, which interpretation is BEST?

  1. Osmolal gap about 80, which the ethanol fully explains
  2. Osmolal gap about 30 after the ethanol term, which is expected at this ethanol level
  3. Osmolal gap about 30 after the ethanol term, so a co-ingested toxic alcohol should be excluded
  4. No toxic alcohol is present, because the anion gap is normal

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. This omits the ethanol term: 366 − 286 = 80. Ethanol must be added to the calculated value, and once it is, a gap of about 30 is still left unexplained.
  • B. Ethanol's osmoles are already inside the calculated value, so a residual gap cannot be blamed on the ethanol; it means another osmole is present.
  • C. Correct. Calculated osmolality is 276 + 5 + 5 + 50 = 336, so the gap is 366 − 336 = 30, well above 10; an unmeasured osmole such as methanol remains and needs specific testing.
  • D. The anion gap of 14 is normal, which is tempting, but ethanol occupies alcohol dehydrogenase and delays conversion of methanol to formic acid, so the acidosis can be absent early.

Calculated osmolality is 2 × 138 + 90/18 + 14/2.8 + 230/4.6 = 276 + 5 + 5 + 50 = 336 mOsm/kg, and the gap is 366 − 336 = 30 mOsm/kg; a gap above about 10 suggests an unmeasured osmole. The anion gap is 138 − (100 + 24) = 14 mmol/L, normal. Ethanol has a higher affinity for alcohol dehydrogenase than methanol or ethylene glycol, so while ethanol is present the parent toxic alcohol stays unmetabolized: the osmolal gap is high but the acidic metabolites, and the anion gap, have not yet appeared. The laboratory should report the gap and support specific methanol and ethylene glycol testing, usually by gas chromatography. Some references weight ethanol more heavily (1.25 × ethanol in mmol/L, about ethanol/3.7), which still leaves a gap of about 18.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 6 · Chemistry

An outpatient's hemoglobin A1c, measured by an NGSP-certified method, is 8.4% (68 mmol/mol). The clinic asks the laboratory to add the estimated average glucose (eAG) to the report, calculated from the ADAG regression eAG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × A1c − 46.7. Which eAG should be reported?

  1. 288 mg/dL (16.0 mmol/L)
  2. 183 mg/dL (10.2 mmol/L)
  3. 241 mg/dL (13.4 mmol/L)
  4. 194 mg/dL (10.8 mmol/L)

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. This adds the intercept instead of subtracting it, which gives a value far above the patient's mean glucose.
  • B. This is the eAG for an A1c of 8.0%; rounding the A1c to a whole number before calculating loses the 0.4% that matters here.
  • C. This is the product 28.7 × 8.4 without subtracting the intercept of 46.7.
  • D. Correct. 28.7 × 8.4 = 241.1, and 241.1 − 46.7 = 194.4, reported as 194 mg/dL or 10.8 mmol/L.

The A1C-Derived Average Glucose (ADAG) study related A1c to the mean of frequent glucose measurements over about 3 months and derived the linear regression eAG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × A1c − 46.7, or eAG (mmol/L) = 1.5944 × A1c − 2.594. Reference points worth knowing are about 126 mg/dL at 6%, 154 mg/dL at 7%, 183 mg/dL at 8% and 212 mg/dL at 9%, so each 1% step in A1c adds about 29 mg/dL. Reporting eAG puts A1c in the same units patients see on their meters, but the eAG is an estimate with a wide range around it for any individual, and conditions that alter red cell survival distort it just as they distort the A1c.

Sources: ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2025)

Question 7 · Chemistry

An ICU patient on day 6 of septic shock, receiving hydrocortisone, has thyroid tests ordered. Results are: TSH 0.3 mIU/L (reference 0.4-4.2 mIU/L) Free T4 0.9 ng/dL (12 pmol/L; reference 0.8-1.8 ng/dL, 10-23 pmol/L) Total T3 45 ng/dL (0.69 nmol/L; reference 80-200 ng/dL, 1.2-3.1 nmol/L) Reverse T3 62 ng/dL (0.95 nmol/L; reference 10-24 ng/dL, 0.15-0.37 nmol/L) Which interpretation is MOST likely?

  1. Subclinical hyperthyroidism
  2. Central hypothyroidism from pituitary failure
  3. Primary hypothyroidism
  4. Nonthyroidal illness

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. TSH is mildly low, but subclinical hyperthyroidism has normal or high-normal T3; here T3 is very low.
  • B. A low TSH with a low-normal free T4 can suggest central disease, which tempts, but the free T4 is in range, and a high reverse T3 points to altered peripheral deiodination; central hypothyroidism lowers reverse T3 along with T4.
  • C. Primary thyroid failure raises TSH as the pituitary responds to low hormone; this TSH is low.
  • D. Correct. Severe illness reduces conversion of T4 to T3 and slows clearance of reverse T3, giving a low T3, a high reverse T3 and a normal or low-normal free T4; glucocorticoids and illness can also lower TSH.

Nonthyroidal illness (euthyroid sick syndrome) is common in critical illness, starvation and after surgery. Cytokines and stress hormones reduce 5'-deiodinase activity, so less T4 becomes active T3 and more becomes inactive reverse T3, which is also cleared more slowly. The earliest and most frequent change is a low T3 with a high reverse T3; in longer or more severe illness, T4 and TSH can fall too, and glucocorticoids or dopamine further lower TSH. Thyroid testing in the ICU is therefore hard to interpret, and abnormal results are best repeated after recovery unless thyroid disease is strongly suspected.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 8 · Hematology

A patient on an unfractionated heparin infusion started warfarin 5 days ago. The sample was centrifuged within 1 hour of collection. Results are: aPTT 128 s (laboratory heparin therapeutic range 60-100 s) Anti-Xa, UFH-calibrated chromogenic, 0.42 IU/mL (therapeutic range 0.3-0.7 IU/mL) PT 24.6 s (reference 11.0-13.5 s), INR 2.5 Total bilirubin 0.7 mg/dL (12 µmol/L) Plasma not hemolyzed or lipemic aPTT before heparin was started 29 s (reference 25-35 s) Which explanation BEST accounts for the disagreement between the aPTT and the anti-Xa?

  1. Bilirubin interference falsely lowering the chromogenic anti-Xa
  2. Warfarin depletion of vitamin K-dependent factors lengthening the aPTT
  3. A heparin overdose that the anti-Xa method fails to detect
  4. Platelet factor 4 released during delayed centrifugation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Bilirubin can interfere with the optical anti-Xa assay, but the bilirubin is normal and the plasma is clear.
  • B. Correct. Falling factors II, IX and X lengthen the clot-based aPTT on top of the heparin effect, while the anti-Xa measures heparin activity alone.
  • C. The anti-Xa assay is the more specific measure of heparin; an overdose would raise it above 0.7 IU/mL rather than leave it mid-range.
  • D. PF4 neutralizes heparin, so release from delayed processing would lower both the aPTT and the anti-Xa; this sample was also spun within 1 hour.

The aPTT is a global clotting test: it lengthens with heparin but also with anything else that lowers intrinsic or common pathway factors, including warfarin or vitamin K deficiency, factor deficiencies and lupus anticoagulants. A chromogenic anti-Xa assay measures the heparin-antithrombin complex's inhibition of added factor Xa and is not affected by the patient's own factor levels. During warfarin overlap, the falling vitamin K-dependent factors and the rising INR prolong the aPTT, so the aPTT overstates the heparin effect and the anti-Xa is the better guide. A normal pre-heparin aPTT argues against a lupus anticoagulant.

Sources: Li et al., aPTT vs Anti-Xa Discordance in MCS (2024)

Question 9 · Hematology

A hospital core laboratory reports these results on a patient with anemia: RBC 3.00 × 10⁶/µL (3.00 × 10¹²/L) Hgb 9.0 g/dL (90 g/L) Hct 27% (0.27 L/L) Reticulocytes 6.0% (0.060) The laboratory uses a normal hematocrit of 45% and a reticulocyte maturation factor of 2.0 for a hematocrit of 25% to 34%. What is the reticulocyte production index, and what does it indicate?

  1. 0.9, marrow failure that needs an aspirate
  2. 1.8, an inadequate marrow response for this anemia
  3. 3.6, an appropriate marrow response
  4. 2.4, an adequate marrow response for this anemia

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. This divides by the maturation factor twice; the index is calculated once, and even a low index does not on its own establish marrow failure.
  • B. Correct. 6.0 × (27/45) = 3.6, and 3.6 ÷ 2.0 = 1.8; an index below 2 in a patient with anemia shows the marrow is not producing enough red cells.
  • C. This is the hematocrit-corrected count (6.0 × 27/45); it leaves out the maturation factor, so it overstates daily production because shift reticulocytes circulate longer in anemia.
  • D. This divides by 1.5, the maturation time for a hematocrit of 35% to 39%; at 27% the stated factor is 2.0, so the index is overstated and wrongly crosses the cutoff of 2.

The reticulocyte percentage is inflated in anemia for two reasons: fewer red cells sit in the denominator, and erythropoietin releases immature shift reticulocytes that circulate for up to two days instead of one. The production index corrects both: multiply the percentage by the patient hematocrit over a normal hematocrit (6.0 × 27/45 = 3.6), then divide by the maturation time for that hematocrit (3.6 ÷ 2.0 = 1.8). An index of 2 or more indicates an adequate response to blood loss or hemolysis (many sources expect 3 or more after acute loss); below 2 points to a hypoproliferative cause. The absolute count here (180 × 10³/µL) is above the reference range, which tempts a reading of adequate response, but it is not adequate for this degree of anemia.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 10 · Hematology

A 44-year-old patient arrives at the emergency department with fever and confusion. Results are: Hgb 8.1 g/dL (81 g/L) Platelets 13 × 10³/µL (13 × 10⁹/L) Reticulocytes 5.6% (0.056) LD 1,120 U/L (18.7 µkat/L; reference 140-280 U/L, 2.3-4.7 µkat/L) Haptoglobin less than 10 mg/dL (less than 0.1 g/L) DAT negative PT 12.6 s (reference 11.0-13.5 s) aPTT 30 s (reference 25-35 s) Fibrinogen 320 mg/dL (3.2 g/L; reference 200-400 mg/dL, 2.0-4.0 g/L) Creatinine 1.3 mg/dL (115 µmol/L) The smear has many helmet cells and small triangular red cell fragments. Which disorder do these results MOST support?

  1. Shiga toxin hemolytic uremic syndrome after bloody diarrhea
  2. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura from severe ADAMTS13 deficiency
  3. Evans syndrome with warm autoimmune hemolysis
  4. Disseminated intravascular coagulation from sepsis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Shiga toxin HUS also causes microangiopathic hemolysis, but it is dominated by acute kidney injury; a creatinine of 1.3 mg/dL with confusion and no diarrhea history fits TTP better.
  • B. Correct. Microangiopathic hemolysis with severe thrombocytopenia, a negative DAT, normal coagulation tests and neurologic signs fits TTP from severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.
  • C. Evans syndrome pairs immune thrombocytopenia with autoimmune hemolysis; the DAT would be positive and the smear would show spherocytes rather than many fragments.
  • D. DIC also causes schistocytes and thrombocytopenia, but it consumes clotting factors, so the PT and aPTT lengthen and fibrinogen falls; all three are normal here.

Schistocytes with thrombocytopenia define a thrombotic microangiopathy. In TTP, loss of ADAMTS13 activity (usually below 10%, most often from an autoantibody) leaves ultralarge von Willebrand factor multimers that build platelet-rich microthrombi; red cells are sheared passing them. The coagulation cascade is not consumed, so PT, aPTT and fibrinogen stay normal, which separates TTP from DIC. The DAT is negative, LD is high and haptoglobin absent. A platelet count of 13 × 10³/µL is a critical value, and schistocytes in this setting should be called promptly because TTP is treated urgently, before the ADAMTS13 result returns.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 11 · Immunology

A hospital immunology laboratory performs direct immunofluorescence on a biopsy of normal-appearing skin next to a lesion from a patient with painful oral erosions and flaccid skin blisters that rupture easily. With FITC-labeled anti-human IgG and anti-C3, fluorescence outlines the surfaces of the keratinocytes throughout the lower epidermis, giving a net-like pattern between the cells. The basement membrane zone and the dermal papillae do not stain. Which disease do these findings MOST likely indicate?

  1. Cutaneous lupus with immune deposits at the dermal-epidermal junction
  2. Bullous pemphigoid
  3. Dermatitis herpetiformis with IgA to transglutaminase
  4. Pemphigus vulgaris, with antibody to desmoglein 3

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Lupus deposits lie at the dermal-epidermal junction, not on keratinocyte surfaces inside the epidermis, and they are not the cause of flaccid blisters with oral erosions.
  • B. Bullous pemphigoid gives linear IgG and C3 along the basement membrane zone, from antibodies to hemidesmosome antigens BP180 and BP230, with tense subepidermal blisters rather than staining between keratinocytes.
  • C. Dermatitis herpetiformis shows granular IgA in the tips of the dermal papillae, and the conjugate here detected IgG and C3, with the papillae unstained.
  • D. Correct. IgG autoantibodies to desmoglein 3, a keratinocyte adhesion cadherin, bind the cell surfaces within the epidermis and produce the intercellular net pattern, with fragile intraepidermal blisters and oral erosions.

Direct immunofluorescence finds antibody and complement already bound in the patient's tissue, and the location of the fluorescence names the disease. In pemphigus vulgaris, IgG autoantibodies against desmoglein 3, and sometimes desmoglein 1, bind the keratinocyte cell surface, so the stain outlines cells within the epidermis; loss of cell-to-cell adhesion makes flaccid intraepidermal blisters. Bullous pemphigoid instead shows linear IgG and C3 along the basement membrane zone, and dermatitis herpetiformis shows granular IgA in the dermal papillae. Serum antibodies to desmogleins can also be measured by indirect immunofluorescence or by ELISA, which is the more sensitive test.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 12 · Microbiology

A watery stool that takes the shape of its container arrives with an order for Clostridioides difficile NAAT. The laboratory information system shows: C. difficile NAAT 3 days ago: negative Laxatives in the last 48 hours: none documented Requisition note: diarrhea continues, please repeat The laboratory's policy follows the IDSA/ASM guidance on repeat testing. What should the laboratory scientist do?

  1. Send the stool for anaerobic culture of C. difficile
  2. Run the NAAT again since the stool is unformed
  3. Decline the repeat test within 7 days and contact the provider
  4. Run a toxin immunoassay instead of a second NAAT

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Toxigenic culture is slow and is used mainly for reference or epidemiologic work; it does not answer the clinical question any faster or better than the negative NAAT already did.
  • B. An unformed specimen is necessary but not sufficient; a repeat NAAT within days of a negative one adds false positives from colonization far more often than it finds new disease.
  • C. Correct. Repeat NAAT after a negative result is not recommended for at least 6 days, so the laboratory follows its policy, declines the duplicate, and explains this to the provider.
  • D. Switching methods sounds like a way around the repeat rule, but the guidance against early retesting concerns the patient and interval, and toxin EIA alone is less sensitive than NAAT.

C. difficile testing is limited to unformed stools from patients with new, unexplained diarrhea (at least 3 unformed stools in 24 hours) who are not receiving laxatives, because colonization is common and a positive NAAT in a colonized patient misleads treatment. Formed stools are rejected unless the provider documents ileus. Once a NAAT is negative, repeating it within 6 days rarely yields a true new positive, and testing a positive patient again as a test of cure is not appropriate either. Here the specimen is acceptable, but the repeat interval is not, so the laboratory applies its repeat-testing policy and talks with the ordering provider.

Sources: CDC: C. diff Facts for Clinicians (2026); Merck Manual Professional: Clostridioides difficile Infection (2025)

Question 13 · Microbiology

At a hospital laboratory in southern Arizona, sputum from a patient with community-acquired pneumonia, fatigue, a rash and joint pain is plated on routine bacterial media. On day 4 a white, cottony mold covers part of the sheep blood agar plate. A technologist on the bench is about to lift the lid to make a tape preparation. What should be done FIRST?

  1. Subculture the mold to a 37 °C plate to look for a yeast phase
  2. Set up a slide culture so the conidia can be studied intact
  3. Tape the plate shut and open it only inside a class II biological safety cabinet
  4. Make the tape preparation at the bench while wearing an N95 respirator

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Coccidioides does not form budding yeasts at 37 °C; it forms spherules in tissue and under special conditions. Subculturing a sporulating mold on the bench also multiplies the exposure.
  • B. Slide cultures look attractive because they preserve conidial arrangement, but BMBL states they should not be prepared from unknown hyaline molds, precisely because the mold could be Coccidioides.
  • C. Correct. In an endemic area an unknown white mold from a respiratory specimen must be treated as possible Coccidioides. Its arthroconidia become airborne when the lid is lifted, and most laboratory exposures happen with cultures handled on the open bench.
  • D. A respirator protects one person but not others nearby, and opening a sporulating plate outside a cabinet releases arthroconidia into the room. Containment in a cabinet comes before personal protection.

Coccidioides immitis and C. posadasii are endemic to the Sonoran Desert, including southern Arizona and California, and cause community-acquired pneumonia with fatigue, rash and joint pain (desert rheumatism). In culture they grow as molds producing arthroconidia 3 to 5 µm across, small enough to reach the alveoli. Laboratory-acquired coccidioidomycosis is a documented hazard, and most exposures come from handling cultures of unknown status on the open bench. BMBL recommends handling clinical specimens and identifying isolates under BSL-2 practices in a cabinet, BSL-3 for propagating cultures already identified as Coccidioides, and no slide cultures from unknown hyaline molds.

Sources: CDC/NIH BMBL, 6th ed. (2020)

Question 14 · Microbiology

A stool culture is set up for a child with bloody diarrhea after a farm visit. The laboratory plans a selective charcoal-based plate for an organism that appears on Gram stain as thin, curved gram-negative rods in S and gull-wing shapes and is oxidase positive. Which incubation conditions are BEST for this plate?

  1. 35 °C in a candle extinction jar
  2. 42 °C in about 5% oxygen and 10% CO2
  3. 37 °C in an anaerobic jar with a palladium catalyst
  4. 25 °C in ambient air after cold enrichment

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A candle jar raises CO2 to a few percent but leaves most of the oxygen, so it is too oxygen-rich for Campylobacter; it suits Neisseria and Haemophilus.
  • B. Correct. Campylobacter jejuni and C. coli are microaerophilic and thermotolerant; 42 °C in a reduced-oxygen, CO2-enriched atmosphere favors them and suppresses much of the stool flora.
  • C. Campylobacter needs a small amount of oxygen; strict anaerobic conditions support it poorly and favor anaerobic gut flora instead.
  • D. Low-temperature methods are used for Yersinia enterocolitica, another farm-associated cause of diarrhea, not for Campylobacter, which does not grow at 25 °C.

Campylobacter is a leading bacterial cause of diarrhea, often bloody, linked to poultry, raw milk and farm animals. Its curved, seagull-shaped gram-negative rods and positive oxidase are recognizable, but it is easily missed in culture unless the right conditions are used. Selective blood-free charcoal or blood-based media with antibiotics are incubated at 42 °C in a microaerobic atmosphere (about 5% O2, 10% CO2, balance nitrogen) and held 48 to 72 hours. Higher temperature also suppresses many enteric organisms that compete on the plate.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Overview of Gastroenteritis (2025); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 15 · Laboratory Operations

During an analyzer outage, the core laboratory runs a manual glucose oxidase method read at 500 nm. Results: Reagent blank absorbance 0.020 Standard, glucose 150 mg/dL (8.3 mmol/L), absorbance 0.320 Patient absorbance 0.520 The method follows Beer's law across this range. What is the patient's glucose?

  1. 234 mg/dL (13.0 mmol/L)
  2. 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L)
  3. 90 mg/dL (5.0 mmol/L)
  4. 244 mg/dL (13.5 mmol/L)

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. This subtracts the blank from the patient but not from the standard (0.500 / 0.320 x 150), so the ratio is off.
  • B. Correct. Blank-corrected absorbances are 0.500 for the patient and 0.300 for the standard; (0.500 / 0.300) x 150 mg/dL = 250 mg/dL.
  • C. This inverts the ratio (0.300 / 0.500 x 150), giving a result lower than the standard although the patient absorbs more light.
  • D. This uses the raw absorbances (0.520 / 0.320 x 150), ignoring the color the reagent itself contributes.

Beer's law states that absorbance is proportional to concentration when path length and molar absorptivity are fixed, so an unknown can be read from a single standard: concentration of unknown = (absorbance of unknown / absorbance of standard) x concentration of standard. The reagent blank measures absorbance that does not come from the analyte, so it is subtracted from every reading first: 0.520 - 0.020 = 0.500 and 0.320 - 0.020 = 0.300. Then 0.500 / 0.300 x 150 = 250 mg/dL, or 13.9 mmol/L. A single-point calculation is valid only inside the linear range of the method, which the stem confirms here.
