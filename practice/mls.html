Question 1 · Blood Banking

In the hospital blood bank, a heel-stick sample from a newborn jaundiced at 20 hours of age types group A, D-positive. The mother is group O, D-positive, with a negative antibody screen. Newborn DAT: IgG 1+ Acid eluate against group O screening cells: nonreactive at IAT Last-wash control: nonreactive What is the NEXT step?

  1. Repeat the maternal antibody screen with ficin-treated cells
  2. Test the eluate against A1 and B reagent red cells
  3. Report a false-positive DAT caused by Wharton jelly contamination
  4. Request a Kleihauer-Betke test on a maternal sample

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Enzymes can enhance some antibodies, but the problem here is that the screening cells lack the suspected A antigen, not that a maternal antibody was too weak to see.
  • B. Correct. Group O screening cells lack A and B, so they cannot detect an eluted maternal anti-A; testing against A1 cells, with B cells as the comparison, shows whether anti-A explains the positive DAT.
  • C. Wharton jelly causes false agglutination only in poorly washed cord blood samples, and this is a heel-stick sample; besides, the IgG coating with a clean last-wash control points to a real antibody, and the ABO mismatch offers an explanation.
  • D. Fetal-maternal hemorrhage quantitation guides Rh immune globulin dosing for D-negative mothers; it does not identify the antibody coating this group A infant's cells.

A group A or B infant of a group O mother is the typical setting for ABO hemolytic disease of the newborn, because group O plasma contains IgG anti-A,B that crosses the placenta. ABO antigens are weak on fetal cells, so ABO incompatibility does not cause significant fetal disease; it shows up after birth as early jaundice. The DAT is often only weakly positive, and the maternal screen is negative because screening cells are group O. An eluate from the infant's cells is likewise nonreactive with group O cells, so it must be tested against A1 and B cells; reactivity with A1 cells alone confirms anti-A. The last-wash control shows the eluate reflects antibody bound to the cells, not unbound plasma antibody.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Merck Manual Professional: Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Neonate (2024)

Question 2 · Blood Banking

A blood center is reviewing old records before archiving. Processing records for a red cell unit were completed in March 2016, and the unit carried an expiration date in April 2016. Under 21 CFR 606.160(d), what is the earliest these individual product records may be discarded?

  1. March 2026, ten years after processing was completed
  2. Indefinitely, because the unit was issued for transfusion
  3. October 2016, six months after the expiration date
  4. April 2021, five years after the expiration date

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Ten years after the records of processing were completed is later than six months after expiration, so it sets the earliest discard date.
  • B. Indefinite retention applies when a product has no expiration date; this unit had one.
  • C. Six months after expiration is one arm of the rule, but the regulation requires the later of the two dates, and 10 years after processing is later.
  • D. Five years is a retention period found in other laboratory rules, not the federal blood product record rule.

21 CFR 606.160(d) requires individual product records to be kept no less than 10 years after the records of processing are completed, or 6 months after the latest expiration date for the product, whichever is later; records for products without an expiration date are kept indefinitely. Processing ended in March 2016, so the 10-year date is March 2026, while 6 months after the April 2016 expiration is October 2016. The later date governs. Long retention supports lookback, because a donor's later positive infectious disease test may require tracing earlier donations and their recipients.

Sources: 21 CFR Part 606 (blood and components cGMP)

Question 3 · Blood Banking

A patient typed D-negative at her first prenatal visit. Her postpartum specimen in a hospital blood bank gives: Anti-D, immediate spin: 0 Weak D test (antiglobulin phase): 1+ mixed field Rh control: 0 DAT: negative Newborn: D-positive What is the MOST likely cause of the weak D result?

  1. Weak D type 1 in the mother, reacting at the antiglobulin phase
  2. A partial D phenotype in the mother with missing D epitopes
  3. Antepartum Rh immune globulin coating the mother's own cells
  4. Fetal D-positive cells from a large fetomaternal bleed

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Weak D type 1 would give a uniform antiglobulin reaction and would have been detected at the first prenatal visit if her laboratory performed the weak D test.
  • B. A partial D phenotype gives a uniform reaction with the patient's own cells, not a mixed-field population, and she typed D-negative earlier in pregnancy.
  • C. Passive anti-D binds D antigen, which her D-negative cells lack, and her negative DAT confirms that her cells are not coated.
  • D. Correct. A mixed-field reaction means two red cell populations, here the mother's D-negative cells and a sizable number of D-positive fetal cells, so the bleed should be quantified and dosed.

Mixed-field agglutination shows two red cell populations: agglutinates among many free cells. In a D-negative mother after delivery of a D-positive infant, the minority population is fetal red cells, and a large fetomaternal hemorrhage can make the weak D test look positive because anti-D agglutinates only the fetal cells. Calling her weak D positive would wrongly deny her Rh immune globulin and risk anti-D formation. The correct response is to report her as D-negative, quantify the bleed with a Kleihauer-Betke stain or flow cytometry, and calculate enough vials to cover it.

Sources: RhoGAM Ultra-Filtered PLUS label (2025); Merck Manual Professional: Hemolytic Disease of the Fetus and Neonate (2024)

Question 4 · Urinalysis and Body Fluids

An automated urine sediment analyzer reports RBC 40/HPF on a specimen from a patient with poorly controlled diabetes. Reagent strip results: glucose 4+, blood negative, leukocyte esterase 1+, specific gravity 1.025. On manual review, the particles counted as red cells are colorless, smooth-walled ovals of varying size; several carry a smaller oval attached at one end, and a few form branching elongated chains. They persist after 2% acetic acid is added to a portion of the sediment. Which interpretation is BEST?

  1. Free fat droplets from lipiduria
  2. Budding yeast with pseudohyphae, not red cells
  3. Calcium oxalate monohydrate ovoids
  4. Crenated red cells in concentrated urine

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Lipid droplets vary in size and can be confused with red cells or yeast, but they are highly refractile spheres that do not bud, and heavy proteinuria would be expected with lipiduria.
  • B. Correct. Smooth ovals of variable size with buds and branching chains that resist acetic acid are yeast, most often Candida; glycosuria favors their growth, and the negative blood pad shows that red cells are not present.
  • C. Monohydrate ovals can mimic red cells and resist acetic acid, which makes them tempting, but they are birefringent and do not bud or form branching chains.
  • D. Red cells do crenate in hypertonic urine, but they would react on the blood pad, they would not bud or form chains, and they lyse in 2% acetic acid.

Yeast is a classic false red cell on both manual and automated sediment review, because both are small, colorless and round to oval. Three clues separate them: yeast varies more in size and shows buds or pseudohyphae; yeast is unaffected by dilute acetic acid, which lyses red cells; and the reagent strip blood pad stays negative when the particles are not red cells. Diabetes with glycosuria is a common setting for candiduria, and pseudohyphae can also grow if the specimen stands on the bench. The scientist corrects the analyzer result, reports yeast with pseudohyphae and no red cells, and notes any delay in testing.

Sources: AFP: Urinalysis Review (2005); Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.

Question 5 · Chemistry

On the night shift, an emergency department sends specimens from a 19-year-old patient with type 1 diabetes in ketoacidosis. Results are: Glucose 540 mg/dL (30.0 mmol/L) Beta-hydroxybutyrate 7.8 mmol/L Serum nitroprusside ketone test strongly positive BUN 19 mg/dL (6.8 mmol/L) Creatinine, kinetic alkaline picrate (Jaffe) method, 2.6 mg/dL (230 µmol/L) Creatinine 6 months ago 0.7 mg/dL (62 µmol/L) The same specimen run by an enzymatic creatinine method gives 1.0 mg/dL (88 µmol/L). What BEST explains the discrepancy?

  1. Acetoacetate reacting with alkaline picrate
  2. Hemoconcentration from the osmotic diuresis
  3. Acute kidney injury that the enzymatic method underestimates
  4. Beta-hydroxybutyrate reacting with alkaline picrate

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Acetoacetate forms a colored product with alkaline picrate that the analyzer reads as creatinine, and the positive error tracks the acetoacetate concentration; enzymatic methods lack this interference.
  • B. Volume depletion can raise true creatinine, but it would raise it equally by both methods and would usually raise BUN too; here the two methods disagree on the same specimen and BUN is normal.
  • C. Enzymatic creatinine methods agree closely with reference HPLC in DKA; it is the Jaffe result that is falsely high, and the normal BUN argues against a large fall in GFR.
  • D. Beta-hydroxybutyrate is the most abundant ketone in DKA, which makes this tempting, but studies found it adds no independent interference; the reactive ketone is acetoacetate, the one the nitroprusside test detects.

The Jaffe reaction measures creatinine by the red-orange color it forms with picrate in alkaline solution, but other compounds react too. In diabetic ketoacidosis, acetoacetate is the main offender: in a study of patients presenting with DKA, the Jaffe method gave falsely high creatinine results that correlated with acetoacetate, while enzymatic and HPLC methods did not, and beta-hydroxybutyrate added no independent error. A creatinine that has risen out of proportion to BUN in ketoacidosis should therefore be confirmed by an enzymatic method before it is read as kidney injury. The interference fades as ketosis resolves, so serial Jaffe values can appear to show rapid renal recovery.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Diabetic Ketoacidosis (2025); Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.

Question 6 · Chemistry

An outpatient with long-standing hypertension who takes no diuretics is found to have a low potassium on routine testing. Results are: Arterial pH 7.51 pCO2 47 mm Hg (6.3 kPa) HCO3 36 mmol/L Potassium 2.9 mmol/L Random urine chloride 48 mmol/L Which cause is MOST consistent with these results?

  1. Persistent vomiting with loss of gastric acid
  2. Recovery from chronic hypercapnia after mechanical ventilation
  3. Primary hyperaldosteronism, a chloride-resistant metabolic alkalosis
  4. Sweat chloride loss from cystic fibrosis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Vomiting is the classic cause of metabolic alkalosis with hypokalemia, but the kidneys conserve chloride in the resulting volume depletion, so urine chloride falls below 20 mmol/L.
  • B. Posthypercapnic alkalosis leaves retained bicarbonate after the pCO2 is lowered, but it is chloride responsive with a low urine chloride, and it needs a history of ventilatory failure.
  • C. Correct. Aldosterone excess drives distal sodium reabsorption with hydrogen ion and potassium secretion, producing hypertension, hypokalemia and a metabolic alkalosis in which urine chloride stays above 20 mmol/L.
  • D. Heavy sweat chloride loss can cause a chloride-responsive alkalosis, but urine chloride would be low and the setting does not fit an adult with hypertension.

The pH and bicarbonate are high, so the primary process is a metabolic alkalosis, and a pCO2 of 47 mm Hg fits the expected rise of about 0.6 to 0.75 mm Hg per 1 mmol/L of bicarbonate. Urine chloride then sorts the cause. Below 20 mmol/L, the kidneys are holding on to chloride because of volume or chloride depletion, as with vomiting, gastric suction, past diuretic use or posthypercapnia. Above 20 mmol/L in a patient not taking diuretics points to mineralocorticoid excess or a tubular disorder such as Bartter or Gitelman syndrome. Hypertension with hypokalemia favors primary hyperaldosteronism.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Metabolic Alkalosis (2025); Merck Manual Professional: Acid-Base Disorders (2025)

Question 7 · Chemistry

An outpatient clinic laboratory receives a random urine from a patient with type 2 diabetes for albuminuria screening. Results are: Urine albumin 9.6 mg/dL (96 mg/L) Urine creatinine 120 mg/dL (10.6 mmol/L) What is the albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and which KDIGO albuminuria category does it fall in?

  1. 80 mg/g; severely increased albuminuria (A3)
  2. 80 mg/g; moderately increased (A2)
  3. 800 mg/g; severely increased (A3)
  4. 8 mg/g; normal to mildly increased (A1)

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The ratio is right, but the severely increased category starts at 300 mg/g (30 mg/mmol); 80 mg/g is moderately increased.
  • B. Correct. 9.6 ÷ 120 = 0.08 mg albumin per mg creatinine, and × 1,000 mg/g = 80 mg/g (about 9 mg/mmol), which is in the 30 to 299 mg/g band.
  • C. This multiplies by 10,000, overstating the ratio tenfold; the correct value is 80 mg/g.
  • D. This multiplies by 100 instead of 1,000 when converting milligrams of creatinine to grams, understating the ratio tenfold.

The albumin-to-creatinine ratio corrects a spot urine for concentration. With both values in mg/dL, the units cancel to mg of albumin per mg of creatinine, and multiplying by 1,000 gives mg/g: 9.6 ÷ 120 × 1,000 = 80 mg/g. In SI units, 96 mg/L ÷ 10.6 mmol/L = 9.1 mg/mmol. KDIGO categories are A1 below 30 mg/g (3 mg/mmol), A2 30 to 299 mg/g and A3 at least 300 mg/g (30 mg/mmol). A raised ratio on a random specimen should be confirmed on a first morning void, since exercise, infection and menstruation can raise urine albumin.

Sources: ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026); ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2025)

Question 8 · Hematology

An outpatient clinic laboratory runs a PT on a patient taking warfarin. The patient's PT is 27.5 s. The laboratory's mean normal PT for the current reagent lot is 12.5 s, and the reagent's International Sensitivity Index (ISI) is 1.30. What INR should be reported?

  1. 2.2
  2. 2.9
  3. 1.8
  4. 2.8

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. This is the PT ratio (27.5 ÷ 12.5) without raising it to the ISI, the step that adjusts for the reagent's sensitivity.
  • B. This multiplies the PT ratio by the ISI (2.2 × 1.30 = 2.86); the ISI is an exponent, not a multiplier.
  • C. This raises the ratio to 1 ÷ 1.30 instead of 1.30, inverting the ISI correction.
  • D. Correct. (27.5 ÷ 12.5)^1.30 = 2.2^1.30 = 2.79, reported as 2.8.

The INR standardizes PT results across thromboplastin reagents and instruments: INR = (patient PT ÷ mean normal PT) raised to the power of the ISI. The ISI describes how responsive a reagent is compared with the international reference thromboplastin (ISI 1.0); a less sensitive reagent has a higher ISI. Working: 27.5 ÷ 12.5 = 2.2; 2.2^1.30 = 2.79, so the INR is 2.8. The mean normal PT must be re-established and the ISI updated with each new reagent lot, or every INR in that lot is biased.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 9 · Hematology

A urine sample and a blood sample arrive together from a patient seen after finishing a marathon. Results are: Urine dipstick blood large (3+) Urine specific gravity 1.018 Urine RBC 0-2/HPF; no red cell ghosts seen Plasma after centrifugation: red-brown Haptoglobin less than 10 mg/dL (0.1 g/L) CK 310 U/L (5.2 µkat/L; reference 30-200 U/L, 0.5-3.3 µkat/L) Which interpretation BEST fits these results?

  1. Myoglobinuria from exertional muscle injury
  2. Hemoglobinuria from intravascular red cell hemolysis
  3. Porphyrins excreted after exercise, giving a false-positive blood reaction on the strip
  4. Hematuria with red cells lysed in the bladder before testing

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Myoglobin also gives a positive dipstick blood reaction without red cells, but it is cleared quickly and does not color the plasma, it does not lower haptoglobin, and rhabdomyolysis raises CK many times above the 310 U/L here.
  • B. Correct. Red-brown plasma, absent haptoglobin and a positive blood reaction with no urine red cells show free hemoglobin in the plasma that has spilled into the urine, as in foot-strike (march) hemoglobinuria.
  • C. Porphyrins can darken urine, but they have no peroxidase-like activity, so they do not produce a positive dipstick blood result, and they do not lower haptoglobin.
  • D. Red cells can lyse in very dilute urine, but the specific gravity is 1.018, no ghosts are seen, and lysis in the bladder would not make the plasma red-brown or consume haptoglobin.

The dipstick blood pad detects the peroxidase-like activity of heme, so intact red cells, free hemoglobin and myoglobin all react. Microscopy separates hematuria (red cells present) from pigmenturia (red cells absent). Plasma separates the two pigments: free hemoglobin binds haptoglobin, and once haptoglobin is used up the plasma turns red-brown and hemoglobin passes into the urine; myoglobin is small, does not bind haptoglobin and is cleared so quickly that the plasma stays clear. Later, iron stored in shed tubular cells appears as urine hemosiderin. A mildly raised CK after a marathon is expected and does not indicate rhabdomyolysis.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 10 · Hematology

An outpatient clinic laboratory reports results on a 67-year-old patient with numbness in both feet: WBC 3.1 × 10³/µL (3.1 × 10⁹/L) Hgb 9.4 g/dL (94 g/L) MCV 117 fL Platelets 118 × 10³/µL (118 × 10⁹/L) Reticulocytes 0.4% (0.004) Vitamin B12 240 pg/mL (177 pmol/L; reference 200-900 pg/mL, 148-664 pmol/L) Serum folate 11.2 ng/mL (25.4 nmol/L; reference greater than 4 ng/mL, greater than 9 nmol/L) Methylmalonic acid 1.10 µmol/L (reference 0.07-0.27 µmol/L) Homocysteine 31 µmol/L (reference 5-15 µmol/L) Creatinine 0.9 mg/dL (80 µmol/L) The smear has oval macrocytes and neutrophils with six nuclear lobes. Which interpretation do these results BEST support?

  1. Folate deficiency hidden by a serum folate raised from a recent meal
  2. Vitamin B12 deficiency despite a serum B12 inside its interval
  3. Myelodysplastic syndrome, as the serum B12 and folate are normal
  4. Methylmalonic acid raised by reduced renal clearance

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because serum folate swings with recent intake, but folate deficiency raises homocysteine alone; methylmalonic acid stays normal because its conversion depends on B12, not folate.
  • B. Correct. A serum B12 of 200-300 pg/mL is nondiagnostic; raised methylmalonic acid and homocysteine with normal renal function show tissue B12 deficiency, which fits the oval macrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, low reticulocytes and the neuropathy.
  • C. Myelodysplasia does cause macrocytic cytopenias with normal vitamin levels, but it does not raise methylmalonic acid, and the six-lobed neutrophils and oval macrocytes point to a megaloblastic process.
  • D. Renal insufficiency does raise methylmalonic acid on its own, which is why creatinine is checked, but a creatinine of 0.9 mg/dL (80 µmol/L) is normal, so the kidney does not explain the result.

Serum B12 is an insensitive screen: values under 200 pg/mL (148 pmol/L) are generally diagnostic, while 200-300 pg/mL leaves the question open. The metabolites settle it. B12 is the cofactor for both methylmalonyl-CoA mutase and methionine synthase, so its deficiency raises methylmalonic acid and homocysteine; folate deficiency raises homocysteine alone. Renal failure can raise methylmalonic acid, so a normal creatinine is needed before reading it. The CBC adds the megaloblastic picture: an MCV well over 100 fL, oval macrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, reticulocytopenia from ineffective erythropoiesis, and cytopenias in other lines.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.

Question 11 · Immunology

A patient with hemoptysis and rapidly rising creatinine has a kidney biopsy. Direct immunofluorescence with FITC-labeled anti-human IgG shows smooth, continuous, ribbon-like linear staining along the glomerular capillary basement membranes, with no granular or lumpy deposits. The serum anti-glomerular basement membrane antibody immunoassay is strongly positive. Which Gell and Coombs hypersensitivity type does this disease represent?

  1. Type IV, from sensitized T cells releasing cytokines
  2. Type II, from antibody against the glomerular basement membrane
  3. Type I, from IgE-armed mast cells in the kidney
  4. Type III, from circulating immune complexes trapped in the glomeruli

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Delayed T-cell hypersensitivity involves no antibody, so it would not explain IgG bound to the basement membrane or a positive serum antibody.
  • B. Correct. IgG directed against a fixed tissue antigen, the basement membrane collagen, binds in place and activates complement and inflammatory cells, which is antibody-dependent cytotoxic hypersensitivity.
  • C. IgE-mediated reactions cause immediate mediator release, such as anaphylaxis and asthma; they do not deposit IgG along basement membranes.
  • D. Immune-complex deposition gives granular, lumpy-bumpy immunofluorescence, as in lupus nephritis or post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, not a smooth linear ribbon.

Anti-GBM disease is a type II hypersensitivity: IgG binds an antigen that is part of the tissue itself (the noncollagenous domain of type IV collagen in glomerular and alveolar basement membranes), then recruits complement, neutrophils and macrophages, which damages the kidney and lung. Because the antigen is evenly distributed along the membrane, direct immunofluorescence shows a continuous linear pattern. In type III disease, antigen-antibody complexes form in the circulation or in situ and lodge unevenly, so the immunofluorescence is granular. Other type II examples include Coombs-positive hemolytic anemia and hyperacute graft rejection.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 12 · Microbiology

A reference laboratory is replacing its overnight biochemical identification panels with a MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer. A colony is smeared on the target plate, overlaid with matrix and loaded into the instrument. Which property of the organism does the instrument measure to make the identification?

  1. Melting temperatures of amplified species-specific gene targets
  2. Mass-to-charge ratios of abundant ribosomal proteins ionized from the colony
  3. Fluorescence of labeled probes hybridized to species-specific 16S rRNA sequences
  4. Color changes from carbohydrate and amino acid substrates read over 18 hours

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Melt-curve analysis belongs to some real-time PCR panels; MALDI-TOF does not amplify nucleic acid.
  • B. Correct. The laser ionizes the matrix and the cell proteins, and the time each ion takes to reach the detector gives its mass-to-charge ratio; the peaks come mostly from ribosomal proteins and are matched against a reference spectrum library.
  • C. This describes fluorescence in situ hybridization (for example PNA-FISH on positive blood cultures), which reads probe binding under a fluorescence microscope, not ion flight time.
  • D. This is the conventional biochemical or automated card method the laboratory is replacing; MALDI-TOF needs no growth in substrates and gives a result in minutes.

In matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry, the sample is mixed with a small organic matrix, dried on a metal plate and pulsed with an ultraviolet laser. Matrix and microbial proteins sublimate and ionize, and the ions drift down a flight tube; drift time increases with mass-to-charge ratio, so lighter ions arrive first. The resulting spectrum, dominated by ribosomal proteins characteristic of a genus or species, is compared with a database of reference spectra and scored. Because it reads preformed protein, the method needs only a colony and minutes, but it can identify only organisms in its database and struggles with closely related species.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 13 · Microbiology

A swab from a hand laceration sustained by punching another person in the mouth (a clenched-fist injury) is plated on sheep blood, chocolate and MacConkey agars in 5% CO2. Among alpha-hemolytic streptococci, at 48 hours there are small, gray, nonhemolytic colonies that pit the agar. Results: Gram stain: slender gram-negative rods MacConkey: no growth Oxidase: positive Catalase: negative Indole: negative Which organism is MOST likely?

  1. Eikenella corrodens
  2. Capnocytophaga canimorsus
  3. Pasteurella multocida
  4. Kingella kingae

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A slow-growing, nonhemolytic, agar-pitting gram-negative rod that is oxidase positive, catalase negative and indole negative from a human bite is E. corrodens, part of the oral flora.
  • B. C. canimorsus is a fastidious, slow-growing gram-negative rod, but it comes from dog or cat bites and is catalase and oxidase positive; it is most dangerous in asplenic patients.
  • C. P. multocida is the classic cat and dog bite organism and is oxidase positive, but it is catalase positive and indole positive.
  • D. Kingella shares the oxidase-positive, catalase-negative profile and can pit agar, but it is beta-hemolytic and is mainly a cause of bone and joint infection in young children.

Eikenella corrodens is a fastidious member of the oral flora that is typically recovered from human bites and clenched-fist injuries, head and neck infections and endocarditis (it is the E of HACEK). Colonies grow slowly in carbon dioxide, are nonhemolytic, gray and moist, and about half of them pit the agar; the organism does not grow on MacConkey agar. It is oxidase positive, catalase negative and indole negative. Bite cultures are usually mixed, and the small colonies are easy to overlook beneath streptococci and staphylococci, so plates from bite wounds are held and examined at 48 hours or longer.

Sources: CDC: Clinical Overview of Capnocytophaga (2025); Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Question 14 · Microbiology

A urethral swab from a patient with purulent discharge is collected at an urgent care clinic at 17:00 for Neisseria gonorrhoeae culture and susceptibility testing. A direct Gram stain done at the clinic shows many neutrophils with intracellular gram-negative diplococci. The swab, in Amies transport medium, is placed in the clinic refrigerator overnight and couriered to the laboratory at 09:00. It is plated on modified Thayer-Martin agar and incubated at 35 °C in 5% CO2 in a humid atmosphere. At 72 hours there is no growth. What is the MOST likely cause of the failed culture?

  1. Refrigeration and the long transport delay killed the fastidious gonococci
  2. Vancomycin in the modified Thayer-Martin agar inhibited the strain
  3. Incubation in 5% CO2 suppressed the growth of the gonococci
  4. Modified Thayer-Martin agar lacks the nutrients the organism needs

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. N. gonorrhoeae is fragile and cold-sensitive; culture swabs are held at room temperature and plated promptly, or inoculated directly at the bedside.
  • B. Some gonococcal strains are inhibited by the vancomycin in selective media, so this is a real limitation, but the cold, 16-hour delay is a far more likely and avoidable cause here.
  • C. Pathogenic Neisseria are capnophilic and need 3-7% CO2 with humidity, so these incubation conditions were correct rather than inhibitory.
  • D. Modified Thayer-Martin is an enriched chocolate-agar base with added antibiotics, designed to supply the growth factors that gonococci require.

Neisseria gonorrhoeae survives poorly outside the body: it is sensitive to drying, cold and temperature swings. IDSA/ASM guidance is to send culture swabs in transport medium at room temperature, not refrigerated, and to plate them promptly, and recovery improves when the swab is plated at the bedside onto selective medium in a CO2-generating system. Modified Thayer-Martin agar is chocolate agar with vancomycin, colistin, nystatin and trimethoprim to suppress commensal flora; the vancomycin inhibits occasional strains, which is why a nonselective chocolate plate is added for sterile sites. Culture remains necessary for gonococcal susceptibility testing, which CDC uses to monitor resistance.

Sources: CDC STI Treatment Guidelines (2021)

Question 15 · Laboratory Operations

A hospital's glucose meters are CLIA waived, and their package insert lists capillary and venous whole blood as the acceptable specimens. The ICU asks the point-of-care coordinator to approve testing arterial line samples on the same meters. Under CLIA, how is this arterial testing categorized?

  1. Moderate complexity, because licensed nurses perform it
  2. High complexity, because an unlisted specimen type makes it a modified test
  3. Waived, because the meter is cleared for home use and categorization follows the device
  4. Waived, if the laboratory first verifies agreement with core laboratory arterial glucose

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because the operator's training seems relevant, but complexity is assigned to the test system and its use, not to the operator. The moderate and high categories differ in personnel requirements, not in who requests the test.
  • B. Correct. A test modified from the manufacturer's instructions, including use of an unapproved specimen type, defaults to high complexity, which brings nonwaived QC, PT, verification and personnel requirements.
  • C. Tempting because waived status comes with the device, but waived status depends on following the manufacturer's instructions. A specimen type outside the insert is a modification of the test.
  • D. Tempting because a comparison study is good practice, but verification does not restore waived status. The laboratory would be performing a modified, high-complexity test and must meet those requirements, including establishing performance specifications.

Waived tests are simple tests with a low risk of an incorrect result, and a waived site's main CLIA duty is to follow the current manufacturer's instructions without modification. Using a specimen type, sample handling or patient population that the insert does not claim changes the test. CDC states that tests modified from the manufacturer's approved instructions default to high complexity. The laboratory must then hold the right certificate and meet nonwaived requirements: establishing performance specifications, two levels of QC each testing day unless an approved alternative applies, proficiency testing or alternative assessment, and testing personnel qualified for high complexity.

Sources: CDC: Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA); 42 CFR Part 493 (CLIA regulations)
