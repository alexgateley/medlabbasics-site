Acid-base in four steps

  1. pH: below 7.35 acidemia, above 7.45 alkalemia.
  2. Primary process: the value that moved in the direction of the pH. Low pH with high pCO₂ is respiratory acidosis; low pH with low HCO₃ is metabolic acidosis.
  3. Compensation: compare the other value with the expected one (table below). Off by more than about 10%, or outside Winter's ± 2: a second primary disorder.
  4. In a metabolic acidosis, calculate the anion gap (corrected for albumin), then the delta ratio.

Sodium, osmolality and pseudohyponatremia

Hyponatremia is a sodium below 136 mmol/L and hypernatremia a sodium above 145 mmol/L (Merck), matching the ASCP range. Sodium is the main determinant of serum osmolality, so most true hyponatremia is hypo-osmolar (water excess, as in SIADH) and most hypernatremia is water loss (diarrhea, sweating, osmotic diuresis, diabetes insipidus).

TypeMeasured osmolalityMechanismLab clue
True (hypotonic)Low (< 275)Water excess relative to sodiumDirect and indirect ISE agree
PseudohyponatremiaNormalLipid or protein occupies plasma volume; indirect ISE assumes 93% waterDirect ISE sodium normal; lipemic sample or very high protein
Translocational (hyperglycemia, mannitol)HighGlucose draws water out of cellsSodium falls about 1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL glucose rise

Indirect ISE (high-throughput chemistry analyzers) dilutes a fixed sample volume and back-calculates using an assumed plasma water fraction of about 93% (7% solids). When lipid or protein raises the solid fraction, the plasma water fraction falls and the indirect result is falsely low; the sodium in the plasma water is unchanged. Direct ISE (blood gas analyzers, most point-of-care analyzers) measures undiluted sample, reads activity in plasma water and is not affected. In a spiking study, protein was the cause: albumin enrichment produced a mean indirect-minus-direct sodium bias of about −5.7 mmol/L, lipid alone gave no significant bias, and lipid acted mainly as an amplifier of the protein effect (Chopra 2026).

Osmolality is measured by freezing point depression in most laboratories. Vapor pressure osmometers cannot be used for alcohol-containing samples (Lin 2018), so they will not reveal an osmolal gap from ethanol or a toxic alcohol. An osmolal gap above 10 mOsm/kg means an osmotically active substance is present; with a high anion gap acidosis, methanol and ethylene glycol are the concern (Merck). Glucose itself widens the gap at very high concentrations: with glucose above 450 mg/dL the mean osmolal gap by freezing point was 19 mOsm/kg (Lin 2018), and a gap can also come from a transcription error, so check the numbers before calling a toxic alcohol (Tan 2026).

Exam tip Worked osmolal gap: Na 140, glucose 108 mg/dL, BUN 14 mg/dL, measured 328 mOsm/kg. Calculated = 280 + 6 + 5 = 291; gap = 37. In SI units the formula is 2 × Na + glucose + urea, all in mmol/L. Ethanol divisors of 3.8, 4.6 and 5 are all in use; the Osmolar Gap calculator uses 4.6.

Potassium

Most potassium is inside cells, so anything that releases cell contents into the specimen raises it. Hyperkalemia (Merck: above 5.5 mmol/L; ASCP's upper limit is 5.1) causes peaked T waves, and above 6.5 mmol/L slower conduction, QRS widening and arrhythmias. True causes include kidney failure, adrenal insufficiency, potassium-retaining drugs, cell breakdown (rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis, burns), insulin deficiency and acidosis. Hypokalemia (below 3.5 mmol/L) comes from gastrointestinal loss, shifts into cells (insulin, beta-2 agonists, thyrotoxicosis) and renal loss (diuretics, aldosterone excess, magnesium deficiency); hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia travel together, and both deficits must be corrected to stop ongoing renal potassium loss (Merck).

Pseudohyperkalemia is a high result with a normal potassium in the patient. Suspect it when the patient has no reason for hyperkalemia and no ECG changes. Hemolysis is the most common cause and also raises LD, AST and iron. Platelets release potassium as a serum tube clots, so a very high platelet count (or extreme leukocytosis) raises serum but not heparin plasma potassium. Repeated fist clenching under a tourniquet can add up to 1.4 mmol/L. Cold storage of unseparated blood lets potassium leak from red cells; familial pseudohyperkalemia (ABCB6, SLC4A1 variants) exaggerates that leak at low temperature (Fujimaru 2025). Pneumatic tube acceleration can hemolyze specimens. Point-of-care and blood gas analyzers use direct ISE on whole blood; in one comparison with indirect ISE serum potassium, the blood gas potassium ran a mean 0.43 mmol/L below the laboratory value (Chothia 2020).

K₂EDTA is a potassium salt and a chelator. In an experiment adding EDTA blood to lithium heparin blood, potassium rose and calcium and magnesium fell significantly from 5% contamination; chloride, iron and LD also fell, and at 43% contamination potassium reached 11.7 mmol/L with calcium at zero (Lima-Oliveira 2014). Fujimaru adds low zinc and ALP. Draw serum and heparin tubes before EDTA tubes. Mild contamination can be confirmed only by measuring EDTA, so the usual answer is a new specimen.

Chloride, total CO₂ and bicarbonate

Total CO₂ on a chemistry panel is mostly bicarbonate plus dissolved CO₂ (0.03 × pCO₂, about 1.2 mmol/L at a pCO₂ of 40), which is one reason its range (22–33 mmol/L) differs from the blood gas HCO₃ range (23–29). The blood gas analyzer does not measure HCO₃: it calculates it from the measured pH and pCO₂ with the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. When the two disagree, the measured chemistry value is considered more accurate (Merck); the calculated value should fall within about 2 mmol/L of the serum value, and a larger difference is a reason to suspect the specimen (Tucker 2025).

Chloride usually moves opposite to bicarbonate. A high chloride with a low bicarbonate and a normal gap is a hyperchloremic (normal gap) acidosis. Bromide and iodide read as chloride on many methods, falsely raising chloride and producing a low or negative anion gap. The urine anion gap, Na + K − Cl in urine, helps separate gastrointestinal from renal bicarbonate loss in a normal-gap acidosis.

The anion gap in detail

The gap is the unmeasured anions (mainly albumin, plus phosphate, sulfate and organic acids) minus the unmeasured cations. Reference intervals fell when ion-selective electrodes replaced flame photometry: 1970s intervals were 8–16 and 9–17 mmol/L; ISE laboratories have reported 3–11 and 5–12 mmol/L, although many laboratories still list an upper limit of 15–16 mmol/L (Chionh 2026). Each laboratory's interval depends on its calibration, so use the interval a question gives; without one, use 12.

Albumin is the largest unmeasured anion. Hypoalbuminemia lowers the gap and can hide a lactic or ketoacidosis, so correct it: add 2.5 mmol/L for each 1 g/dL that albumin is below 4.0 g/dL (in g/L: gap + 0.25 × (40 − albumin)). Spurious high gaps come from severe hyperphosphatemia, severe hypertriglyceridemia, hyperalbuminemia and IgA paraprotein; true low gaps from cationic IgG paraproteins, hypercalcemia, hypermagnesemia, lithium and polymyxin B (Fong 2026).

Delta gap (Merck): the patient's gap minus the normal gap. Add it to the measured HCO₃; if the sum is above the normal HCO₃, a metabolic alkalosis is also present. The delta ratio (Tucker) says the same as a ratio: (gap − 12) ÷ (24 − HCO₃). Below 0.8, a normal-gap acidosis is added; 0.8–1.2, a pure high-gap acidosis; above 1.2, a metabolic alkalosis is added.

Acid-base physiology and the compensation rules

pH = −log[H⁺]; at pH 7.40, H⁺ is 40 nmol/L (normal 37–43 nmol/L, pH 7.43–7.37). The bicarbonate-CO₂ system is the main extracellular buffer; phosphates and proteins, including hemoglobin, buffer inside cells, and bone buffers prolonged acid loads. The lungs adjust CO₂ within minutes to hours, about 50–75% effectively; the kidneys reabsorb or excrete HCO₃ over hours to days (Merck). That difference is why respiratory disorders have an acute and a chronic compensation value.

Exam tip Henderson-Hasselbalch worked: pH 7.33, pCO₂ 50 mm Hg. HCO₃ = 0.03 × 50 × 10^(7.33 − 6.1) = 1.5 × 17.0 = 25.5 mmol/L. A bicarbonate about 1.5 mmol/L above 24 for a 10 mm Hg pCO₂ rise fits acute respiratory acidosis (expected 25–26).

Venous gases are common outside the ICU. In stable patients venous and arterial pH and pCO₂ differ only slightly, but in circulatory or severe respiratory failure the differences grow, and venous pO₂ says nothing about the lungs (Tucker 2025).

A saturation the analyzer calculates from pO₂ assumes all hemoglobin is normal. In carbon monoxide poisoning, the pO₂ and calculated saturation are normal while oxygen content is not, and the pulse oximeter cannot tell carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin, so it reads falsely high; carboxyhemoglobin must be measured by CO-oximetry, and a venous sample is acceptable (Merck). Methemoglobin absorbs most at 635 nm and absorbs the pulse oximeter's two wavelengths (660 and 940 nm) equally, so the pulse oximeter drifts toward about 85% while the calculated saturation stays normal. Normal methemoglobin is below 1%. Sulfhemoglobin (peak 614 nm) can be misread as methemoglobin by older CO-oximeters; adding cyanide turns methemoglobin bright red (cyanomethemoglobin) but leaves sulfhemoglobin unchanged (Twine 2026). Methylene blue, some vitamin B12 preparations and fetal hemoglobin can interfere with CO-oximetry (Baird 2013).

Exam tip CO poisoning: normal pO₂, normal calculated saturation, normal pulse oximetry, high COHb by CO-oximetry. Methemoglobinemia: normal pO₂, pulse oximetry near 85%, high MetHb by CO-oximetry.

Blood gas specimens

Room air has a pO₂ near 150 mm Hg and almost no CO₂. A bubble therefore pulls the specimen's pO₂ toward 150 (up if the patient's pO₂ is lower, down if it is higher on supplemental oxygen) and lets CO₂ escape, so pCO₂ falls and pH rises; the higher pH lowers ionized calcium. With a 20% bubble analyzed about 15 minutes after a venous draw, pO₂ rose 10.8%, sO₂ 11.2%, pCO₂ fell 4.8% and ionized calcium 1.3% (Pighi 2024). Expel bubbles immediately; a syringe that arrives with a bubble should not have its pO₂, sO₂, pCO₂ or ionized calcium reported.

Cells keep metabolizing in the syringe. At room temperature pO₂ falls about 2 mm Hg per hour, pCO₂ rises about 1 mm Hg per hour and pH falls 0.02–0.03 per hour; glucose falls and lactate rises as the sample turns anaerobic. Very high leukocyte or platelet counts make the pO₂ fall much faster. IFCC advice cited by Baird: analyze plastic syringes at room temperature within 15 minutes when pO₂ or saturation is wanted, otherwise within 30 minutes. Do not ice plastic syringes, which become more permeable to gas when cold; icing was a practice for glass syringes.

Use dry, electrolyte-balanced heparin. Liquid heparin dilutes the sample (lowering pCO₂ and HCO₃) and carries dissolved air, and unbalanced heparin binds calcium and other cations. EDTA and oxalate chelate divalent cations and are unsuitable. Mix well after collection and again before analysis to resuspend red cells for hemoglobin and CO-oximetry. Pneumatic tube transport has raised pO₂ and caused hemolysis in some studies.

Calcium

Total serum calcium is 8.8–10.4 mg/dL (2.20–2.60 mmol/L) and ionized calcium 4.7–5.2 mg/dL (1.17–1.30 mmol/L), ranges that vary by laboratory (Merck). About 40% of calcium is bound to protein, mainly albumin, and ionized calcium is about half the total. Low albumin lowers total calcium by about 0.8 mg/dL (0.2 mmol/L) per 1 g/dL, without changing ionized calcium; corrected calcium estimates the effect, but ionized calcium is the preferred test. Acidosis raises ionized calcium by reducing protein binding and alkalosis lowers it, so an ionized calcium specimen is collected anaerobically and analyzed promptly. Convert mg/dL to mmol/L by multiplying by 0.25.

PTH raises calcium within minutes (renal and intestinal calcium absorption, mobilization of calcium and phosphate from bone, conversion of vitamin D to calcitriol); calcitriol raises intestinal absorption; calcitonin lowers calcium, weakly (Merck). Magnesium below about 1.0 mg/dL impairs PTH release and action, so hypocalcemia with hypomagnesemia corrects only after magnesium is replaced. Citrate in large transfusions and EDTA in some contrast agents lower ionized calcium; EDTA contamination of a specimen lowers it in vitro.

Exam tip Worked: calcium 7.4 mg/dL, albumin 2.0 g/dL. Corrected = 7.4 + 0.8 × (4.0 − 2.0) = 9.0 mg/dL (2.25 mmol/L): the low total reflects low albumin. Subtracting the correction (5.8) is the usual wrong answer.

Iron, TIBC, UIBC and saturation

Serum iron is iron carried on transferrin. TIBC is how much iron transferrin could carry, a functional measure of transferrin; UIBC is the unused part, so TIBC = iron + UIBC. Saturation is iron ÷ TIBC × 100. In iron deficiency the liver makes more transferrin, so TIBC rises while iron and saturation fall. In inflammation iron is held in stores, so iron falls, TIBC is normal or low and ferritin, an acute-phase reactant, is normal or high. In iron overload iron and saturation are high and TIBC low; TIBC is also low in liver disease, malnutrition and protein loss, and high in late pregnancy.

Exam tip Worked: iron 40 mcg/dL, UIBC 410 mcg/dL. TIBC = 450; saturation = 40 ÷ 450 × 100 = 8.9%, an iron-deficiency pattern. Another: iron 196, UIBC 44, TIBC 240, saturation 82%, an overload pattern. Iron mcg/dL × 0.179 = µmol/L.

Serum iron is highest in the morning and falls through the day, and recent meals or supplements raise it, so iron studies are drawn fasting in the morning with supplements stopped at least 24 hours. Hemolysis raises serum iron (red cells contain it), and EDTA contamination lowers it. Ferritin below 30 ng/mL is specific for iron deficiency; with inflammation, values up to 100 ng/mL can still mean deficiency (Merck).
