Gram stain: the steps and why each matters

Times follow the CDC meningitis manual; UK SMI TP 39 (Hucker modification) uses 30 s steps. Follow the laboratory's validated procedure or the manufacturer's.
StepReagent and timeWhat it does
1. SmearThin, even film from 1-2 drops (centrifuged sediment for CSF); air dry completelyA monolayer lets single cells and their arrangement be seen; a wet or thick smear washes off or reads falsely
2. Fix95% methanol for at least 2 min, or pass gently through a flameSticks cells to the slide; overheating distorts cells and can make gram-positives stain negative
3. Primary stainCrystal violet, about 1 min (30 s in the Hucker method); rinseStains every cell purple
4. MordantGram's (Lugol's) iodine, about 1 min (30 s in Hucker); rinseForms a crystal violet-iodine complex that the thick peptidoglycan of gram-positive walls holds
5. Decolorize95% ethanol (or acetone or acetone-alcohol) until no more color runs, about 5-10 s; rinse at onceThe critical step: removes the complex from gram-negative cells; time depends on smear thickness
6. CounterstainSafranin about 30 s (carbol fuchsin 10-15 s, or neutral red about 2 min); rinse, blot dryStains the now colorless gram-negative cells pink-red
7. ReadOil immersion (100x)Gram-positive: deep blue-purple. Gram-negative: pink-red. Note cells, arrangement, and host cells (neutrophils, squamous cells)
Exam tip Run a control slide with a gram-positive and a gram-negative organism (CDC suggests S. aureus and E. coli) with each batch. If both control organisms are pink, the run was over-decolorized (or the crystal violet or iodine failed): no patient result from that run is valid. Restain the patient smear with the control.

Gram stain errors

ErrorWhat you seeFix
Over-decolorizing (too long, or decolorizer left on)Gram-positives look pink: false gram-negativeShorter decolorizing, rinse promptly; check the control
Under-decolorizing, or a smear too thickGram-negatives look purple: false gram-positive; thick areas unreadableThinner smear; read thin areas; decolorize until color stops running
Too much heat when fixingDistorted cells; gram-positives may stain negativeMethanol fixation or gentle heat only
Weak crystal violet, too little iodine, heavy rinsing between stepsGram-positives lose the dye-iodine complex and stain pinkFresh reagents at the right strength; gentle rinses
Too much counterstain, too little reagentUneven staining and decolorizingFlood the whole smear; follow counterstain times
Old cultures and certain organismsRapidly growing streptococci, involution forms of S. pneumoniae and some Bacillus strains appear gram-negative in part or wholeStain very young growth from noninhibitory media
Expired or badly made reagents, poor microscope techniqueHard-to-read stains in every runCheck dates, concentrations and the microscope
  • Gram-variable organisms can stain either way. Acid-fast bacteria are gram-indeterminate: use an acid-fast stain.
  • Yeasts such as Candida and Cryptococcus stain gram-positive.
  • Delicate gram-negatives such as Haemophilus are easy to miss; Brucella can hold crystal violet and look like gram-positive cocci; Francisella counterstains poorly.

Try one

Gram Stain and Bacterial Identification practice question

A swab from a hand laceration sustained by punching another person in the mouth (a clenched-fist injury) is plated on sheep blood, chocolate and MacConkey agars in 5% CO2. Among alpha-hemolytic streptococci, at 48 hours there are small, gray, nonhemolytic colonies that pit the agar. Results: Gram stain: slender gram-negative rods MacConkey: no growth Oxidase: positive Catalase: negative Indole: negative Which organism is MOST likely?

  1. Eikenella corrodens
  2. Capnocytophaga canimorsus
  3. Pasteurella multocida
  4. Kingella kingae

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A slow-growing, nonhemolytic, agar-pitting gram-negative rod that is oxidase positive, catalase negative and indole negative from a human bite is E. corrodens, part of the oral flora.
  • B. C. canimorsus is a fastidious, slow-growing gram-negative rod, but it comes from dog or cat bites and is catalase and oxidase positive; it is most dangerous in asplenic patients.
  • C. P. multocida is the classic cat and dog bite organism and is oxidase positive, but it is catalase positive and indole positive.
  • D. Kingella shares the oxidase-positive, catalase-negative profile and can pit agar, but it is beta-hemolytic and is mainly a cause of bone and joint infection in young children.

Eikenella corrodens is a fastidious member of the oral flora that is typically recovered from human bites and clenched-fist injuries, head and neck infections and endocarditis (it is the E of HACEK). Colonies grow slowly in carbon dioxide, are nonhemolytic, gray and moist, and about half of them pit the agar; the organism does not grow on MacConkey agar. It is oxidase positive, catalase negative and indole negative. Bite cultures are usually mixed, and the small colonies are easy to overlook beneath streptococci and staphylococci, so plates from bite wounds are held and examined at 48 hours or longer.

Sources: CDC: Clinical Overview of Capnocytophaga (2025); Merck Manual, Professional Version; ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026)

Anaerobes

  • Special-potency disks on anaerobic blood agar: vancomycin 5 ug, kanamycin 1,000 ug (1 mg), colistin 10 ug. They help group anaerobes; the Bacteroides fragilis group is resistant to all three.
  • B. fragilis group: pleomorphic gram-negative rods, nonmotile, grows in 20% bile; on Bacteroides bile esculin (BBE) agar it forms brown to black colonies (Phocaeicola vulgatus may not blacken).
  • Clostridium: catalase-negative, spore-forming gram-positive rods. C. perfringens: nonmotile, double-zone hemolysis on blood agar.
  • Clostridioides difficile is diagnosed from stool by toxin and molecular tests (see the Specimens, Cultures and Susceptibility Testing page).
  • MALDI-TOF performs well for anaerobes, including the B. fragilis group.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Why the Gram stain works, and where it fails

The Gram reaction reflects the cell wall. Gram-positive bacteria have thick, dense peptidoglycan; iodine enters and binds the crystal violet into a complex that the decolorizer cannot wash out of an intact wall. Gram-negative walls lose the complex and take up the counterstain. Anything that damages the wall (excess heat, involution forms in aging cultures) or removes too much dye (long decolorizing, heavy rinsing) pushes gram-positives toward pink. Anything that leaves too much dye (thick smears, short decolorizing) pushes gram-negatives toward purple.

  • Decolorizing has no fixed time in many laboratories: drop on decolorizer until it runs clear, then rinse. Thicker smears need longer. This is why the control slide matters.
  • Smears that are too thin give false negatives; clumped cultures that were not mixed are hard to read.
  • When a specimen probably holds bacteria but none are seen, an alternative counterstain (Sandiford's, Giemsa), India ink or a wet preparation can help.
  • Modifications for anaerobes (Kopeloff and others) improve their staining.

More on streptococci

  • Lancefield group antigens are not species names. Group A antigen is also carried by S. dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis and some S. anginosus group strains; group C and G antigens occur in several species. Use hemolysis, group and phenotypic tests together.
  • S. pyogenes is bacitracin susceptible and PYR positive; S. agalactiae is CAMP and hippurate positive. Neither marker is exclusive: S. canis, S. porcinus and S. iniae can be CAMP positive; S. porcinus and S. iniae are PYR positive.
  • The S. anginosus group (S. anginosus, S. constellatus, S. intermedius) has beta-hemolytic and nonhemolytic strains, may carry group A, C, F or G antigen or none, and is VP positive.
  • S. bovis group: S. gallolyticus (formerly S. bovis biotype I) is bile esculin positive and does not grow in 6.5% NaCl; S. infantarius can be bile esculin negative.
  • Nutritionally variant streptococci (Abiotrophia, Granulicatella) need pyridoxal and can grow as satellites near other bacteria, like Haemophilus.
  • Optochin QC: S. pneumoniae ATCC 49619 inhibited, S. mitis ATCC 49456 not. Measure the zone with the lid off. Bile solubility uses 0.5 mL of 2% sodium deoxycholate added to 0.5 mL of a 0.5-1.0 McFarland suspension, with a saline control tube; read at 10 min and up to 2 h.

More on staphylococci

  • CoNS: gram-positive, catalase-positive, mostly oxidase-negative, coagulase-negative cocci in pairs, tetrads and clusters. Most colonies are nonpigmented, smooth and opaque; S. lugdunensis and S. haemolyticus can be hemolytic.
  • Third-generation latex tests (clumping factor, protein A, capsule types 5 and 8) are over 98% sensitive but less specific; S. lugdunensis and S. schleiferi carry clumping factor and can test positive, and some S. haemolyticus and S. hominis strains carry type 8 capsule.
  • Small-colony variants grow as pinpoint, poorly pigmented colonies beside normal ones and can look like a mixed culture.
  • Novobiocin is a 5 ug disk on Mueller-Hinton or sheep blood agar, used mainly on urine CoNS to pick out S. saprophyticus.
  • Commercial systems name common CoNS well but are less reliable for S. hominis and S. warneri.

More on Neisseria and Haemophilus

  • Primary plates: BAP and CHOC at 35-37 C in about 5% CO2 (or a candle jar) for 18-24 h. N. meningitidis likes humidity. Its colonies on BAP are gray, moist, glistening and convex; H. influenzae grows only on CHOC as colorless-to-gray opaque colonies; S. pneumoniae grows on both, alpha-hemolytic.
  • A positive oxidase alone does not identify N. meningitidis: other Neisseria and unrelated bacteria are positive too.
  • Rarely, N. meningitidis uses only glucose or only maltose; some N. gonorrhoeae strains are weak acid producers and look glucose negative.
  • N. meningitidis needs BSL-2 practices; many steps open live plates, so use a BSC where possible.
  • Haemophilus factor testing: 1.0 McFarland suspension on heart infusion or trypticase soy agar with hemin, NAD and hemin-plus-NAD disks; or a Quad plate (0.5 McFarland). Growth in every quadrant suggests it is not Haemophilus. H. influenzae may show slight growth on the NAD-only area.
  • H. haemolyticus can lose its hemolysis in culture, so a both-factors isolate from CSF is presumed H. influenzae until proved otherwise; molecular methods may be needed.
  • The porphyrin (ALA) test measures the hemin requirement without the carryover problem of X disks. H. parainfluenzae is ornithine decarboxylase positive.

More on the gram-positive bacilli

  • Corynebacterium and other coryneforms from skin are frequent contaminants but cause device, prosthetic and immunocompromised-host infections; identify them to species when they come from sterile sites or several cultures. 16S rRNA sequencing is the recommended starting point for difficult taxa.
  • Laboratories with little diphtheria experience should review cystine-tellurite blood or fresh Tinsdale agar procedures or send the specimen to a reference center. C. ulcerans and C. pseudotuberculosis can also produce diphtheria toxin.
  • Listeria: confirm by MALDI-TOF or PCR today; the CAMP test was the historical confirmation.
  • Erysipelothrix is easily mistaken for streptococci or enterococci; its H2S in TSI (black after up to 72 h) and catalase-negative reaction separate it from Corynebacterium, Listeria and Lactobacillus. Its vancomycin resistance is intrinsic (vanC).
  • Nocardia: Gram stain is the most sensitive smear; make a duplicate slide for the modified Kinyoun stain, which can be unreliable on direct specimens. Harsh mycobacterial digestion can kill Nocardia. Colonies range from smooth (N. farcinica, orange with age) to chalky white with aerial hyphae.
  • Bacillus anthracis versus B. cereus biovar anthracis: test large, catalase-positive, nonhemolytic Bacillus for motility in semisolid medium; B. anthracis is nonmotile. Isolates can look weakly hemolytic after 48 h.

The Laboratory Response Network

The LRN for biological threats was founded in 1999 by CDC, the FBI and APHL.

SentinelHospital and commercial laboratoriesRecognize, rule out or refer possible biothreat agents; package and ship Category A and B substances; annual competency (e.g. CAP LPX)

NationalCDC, USAMRIID, Naval Medical Research CenterSpecialized characterization, bioforensics, the most dangerous agents
  • BSL-3 practices in a BSL-2 laboratory: restricted access, solid-front gown, gloves, eye and face protection and fit-tested respiratory protection, all work in the BSC, plates taped shut, waste decontaminated.
  • Aerosol-producing steps to avoid or contain: opening and sniffing plates, heat-fixing, vortexing, centrifuging, subculturing positive blood bottles, catalase testing, loading automated ID systems.
  • If an automated system with a tube extraction is used (most often MALDI-TOF), APHL advises filtering the extract through a 0.2 um filter.
  • Paperwork: APHIS/CDC Form 4 documents a confirmed select agent identification (and its destruction), Form 2 a transfer, Form 3 an exposure. The reference laboratory guides destruction or transfer of remaining material.
  • Clostridium botulinum, staphylococcal enterotoxin B, Coxiella burnetii, smallpox and novel influenza are handled by consulting the reference laboratory under the ASM sentinel guidelines.
