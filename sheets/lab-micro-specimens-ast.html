Specimens, Cultures and Susceptibility Testing

Exam tip The two classic "never refrigerate" specimens are blood cultures and CSF for bacterial culture (Neisseria meningitidis and Haemophilus influenzae die in the cold). Gonococcal swabs are cold-sensitive too. Urine and sputum are the opposite: refrigerate them if they cannot be plated promptly.

Specimen quality and rejection

  • Sputum screen (routine bacterial culture): Gram stain at low power (10x objective, 100x total). The adult ideal is fewer than 10 squamous epithelial cells and more than 25 neutrophils per LPF. Many squamous cells mean saliva: reject and ask for a new deep-cough specimen. Neutrophils cannot rescue a specimen that fails the squamous cell limit.
  • The screen applies to routine bacterial culture; laboratories do not reject mycobacterial or Legionella requests on squamous cells.
  • Swabs are poor specimens for tissue, fluids, abscesses and deep infections: few organisms, drying, and no anaerobic protection. Ask for tissue, fluid or an aspirate.
  • Mislabeled or unlabeled specimens, leaking containers, wrong transport, and delays past the stability limit are rejected under written criteria.
  • Before rejecting, call the care team: some specimens (CSF, tissue, operative samples) cannot be recollected. If an irreplaceable specimen is tested anyway, add a comment such as "Specimen cultured; volume inadequate for optimal recovery".
Safety Processing priority: CSF for bacterial culture and other irreplaceable specimens (tissue, sterile fluids) go to the front of the line. A positive CSF Gram stain is called to the provider at once.

Blood cultures

  • Volume, not timing, matters most: adult bacteremia is often low grade. 20-30 mL per set split between an aerobic and an anaerobic bottle (8-10 mL each). If 10 mL or less is drawn, put it all in the aerobic bottle.
  • Two or more sets from separate venipunctures let the reader tell infection from contamination. Single sets are discouraged.
  • Skin antisepsis with an alcohol-containing agent (alcoholic chlorhexidine or iodine tincture), allowed to dry; wipe bottle tops with 70% isopropyl alcohol.
  • Continuous-monitoring systems flag a positive bottle, which gets a Gram stain, a call, subculture and rapid ID or resistance tests.
  • Contamination rate: 3% or less is the accepted benchmark; 1% or less has been proposed as the new standard. Initial specimen diversion devices lower it.
  • Catheter-related infection: paired sets drawn at the same time through the catheter and by venipuncture. If both grow the same organism and the catheter set turns positive more than 2 h before the peripheral set, catheter-related bloodstream infection is likely (differential time to positivity).

Exam tip For a probable contaminant the laboratory does an abbreviated workup with no susceptibility testing and a report comment (for example "Coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, probable contaminant; call within 2 days if further workup is needed").

Urine culture: what a colony count means

Specimen or settingCount that matters
Asymptomatic bacteriuria (voided), women10⁵ CFU/mL or more (10⁸ CFU/L) of the same organism in 2 consecutive specimens
Asymptomatic bacteriuria (voided), men10⁵ CFU/mL or more in a single specimen
Indwelling catheter, asymptomatic10⁵ CFU/mL or more
Suprapubic aspirate or cystoscopic specimen (arranged with the laboratory)Counts below 1,000 CFU/mL can matter; a single potential pathogen as low as 200 CFU/mL may be significant
Febrile infant (2-24 months), catheter or suprapubic specimenMore than 50,000 CFU/mL of a single organism (10⁴ with reliable pyuria finds more cases)
Symptomatic patient, midstream specimenLower counts of one uropathogen can be significant; reports below 10,000 CFU/mL are arranged with the laboratory
  • Three or more species usually means contamination at collection: report as mixed flora and ask for a new specimen rather than identifying each.
  • Correlate with the urinalysis: pyuria and nitrite support the culture; many squamous cells and no pyuria point to collection carryover.
  • A delay at room temperature lets a few contaminants multiply into a "significant" count. That is why the 30-minute to 1-hour limit exists.
  • Main uropathogens in the ASCP outline: Enterobacterales, Enterococcus, S. saprophyticus, S. agalactiae, Candida.

CSF, sterile fluids and respiratory counts

  • CSF: the Gram stain finds organisms in 60-80% of bacterial meningitis before antibiotics and 40-60% after. Draw blood cultures too. Correlate with cell count, glucose and protein (see the Body Fluids page).
  • Shunt infections: coagulase-negative staphylococci, Corynebacterium and Cutibacterium are real pathogens here. Shunt or drain parts are cultured only when the patient has signs of CNS infection.
  • Pleural fluid in blood culture bottles raises recovery by about 20%, but send a separate tube for the Gram stain and for non-bacterial pathogens.
  • Quantitative lower respiratory cultures (ventilated patients): endotracheal aspirate 10⁶ CFU/mL, bronchoalveolar lavage 10⁴ CFU/mL, protected specimen brush 10³ CFU/mL. Above the threshold favors pneumonia; below favors colonization. Report the count, not a yes or no.

Exam tip Breakpoints change (CLSI M100 is revised every year) and the FDA does not always recognize them, so a commercial panel may run older breakpoints. Example from M100: piperacillin-tazobactam for Enterobacterales is S ≤ 8/4, SDD 16/4, R ≥ 32/4 µg/mL. EUCAST calls I "susceptible, increased exposure".

Exam tip An "unexpected" result (for example vancomycin-susceptible Leuconostoc, or a carbapenem-susceptible S. maltophilia) means check the identification and repeat the test before reporting. Drugs on the expected-resistant list are not tested; they are reported resistant or suppressed.

Reporting

  • Test only clinically significant isolates; contaminants and normal flora do not get AST.
  • Cascade reporting: broader agents are suppressed when narrower first-line agents test susceptible (for example meropenem released only if the Enterobacterales isolate is resistant to ceftriaxone and cefepime). Selective reporting suppresses by organism, site or setting (for example aminoglycosides are not reported for Salmonella or Shigella). Rules are set with the stewardship program; antibiograms use all tested results.
  • CLSI M100 Table 1 now sorts drugs into Tiers 1-4 (routine, cascade, high-resistance settings, on request) instead of Groups A, B and C; agents for urine isolates only are still marked U.
  • Typical critical calls: positive blood culture Gram stain, positive CSF Gram stain or culture, and other organisms on the laboratory critical list. Read back the result and document who was told and when.
  • Infection prevention calls: MRSA, VRE, CRE or carbapenemase producers, C. difficile, and other organisms on the facility list. Public health: notifiable organisms (state lists) and isolate submission, including culture of CIDT-positive stools when requested.
  • A corrected report (for example a Gram stain misread as cocci that grows yeast) is called to the provider and issued as a documented corrected report.

Resistance notes the exam likes

  • Why cefoxitin for MRSA: it is a very sensitive and specific marker of mecA/mecC resistance, including strains that express it heterogeneously and can show falsely low oxacillin MICs; the oxacillin disk correlates poorly with mecA, so neither EUCAST nor CLSI uses it for S. aureus. Low-level oxacillin-resistant S. aureus without mecA or mecC (borderline, BORSA) is rare; the mechanism may be beta-lactamase hyperproduction or altered native PBPs. mecC strains are missed by mecA PCR and PBP2a latex.
  • Staphylococci other than S. aureus and S. lugdunensis: read the cefoxitin disk at 24 h (it may be reported earlier only if resistant). For species outside S. epidermidis, S. pseudintermedius and S. schleiferi the oxacillin MIC can overcall resistance; isolates from serious infections with MICs of 1-2 µg/mL may be checked for mecA or PBP2a.
  • D-test spacing differs by standard: EUCAST uses 12-20 mm edge to edge for staphylococci and 12-16 mm for streptococci; flattening is clearest at 15-20 mm and less reliable at 26-28 mm. Constitutive MLSB resistance needs no D-test: the isolate is resistant to both erythromycin and clindamycin on the plate.
  • AmpC derepression: Enterobacter, K. aerogenes and C. freundii can turn resistant to cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime and piperacillin-tazobactam during therapy. M100 makes cefepime a Tier 1 drug for these organisms.
  • ESBL tests and AmpC: an isolate with high-level AmpC may screen positive for ESBL and fail confirmation, because clavulanate does not inhibit AmpC and may even induce it.
  • Beta-lactam combinations: susceptibility to the beta-lactam alone predicts susceptibility to its combination, not the reverse.
  • VRE screening: rectal or perianal swab or stool on chromogenic or vancomycin screening agar, or vanA/vanB PCR. A single rectal swab misses many carriers.
  • Very major errors (R reported as S) are the error type that QC and method verification are designed to prevent; FDA performance criteria for AST devices cap very major errors (1.5% or less at the lower 95% confidence limit, 7.5% or less at the upper) and major errors (3% or less).
Exam tip MLT note: the MLT outline drops mechanisms of action of the antibiotic classes, but keeps AST methods, phenotypic resistance tests (beta-lactamase, ESBL, D-test, carbapenemases), resistance genes (mecA, vanA, blaKPC), intrinsic resistance and MRSA/VRE/CRE screening.
