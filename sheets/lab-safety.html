Laboratory Safety and Biosafety

Bloodborne pathogens and exposures, biosafety levels and safety cabinets, chemical labels and SDS, fire, electrical and radiation safety, waste, shipping specimens, and the sentinel laboratory

Checked against current sources November 2026

Bloodborne pathogens: the kinds of control

ControlWhat it is (OSHA)Laboratory examples
EngineeringIsolates or removes the hazard from the workplaceSharps containers, self-sheathing needles, safety-engineered sharps, BSCs, splash shields, centrifuge safety cups
Work practiceChanges the way a task is doneNo two-handed recapping, no mouth pipetting, handwashing after gloves come off, no eating or drinking in the work area
Personal protective equipmentBarrier worn by the worker, provided at no costGloves, fluid-resistant gowns or lab coats, face shields, masks with goggles
AdministrativeWritten programs and trainingExposure control plan, chemical hygiene plan, SOPs, annual training
  • Exposure control plan: written, reviewed and updated at least annually and when tasks change; front-line workers help choose safer devices.
  • Hepatitis B vaccine: offered at no cost within 10 working days of assignment. A worker who declines signs a declination and can still get the vaccine later.
  • Training: at assignment and at least annually. Training records kept 3 years; medical records for the length of employment plus 30 years.
  • Treat all blood and other potentially infectious materials (OPIM) as infectious. OPIM include CSF, synovial, pleural, pericardial, peritoneal and amniotic fluid, semen, vaginal secretions and any fluid visibly bloody.
Exam tip Exams ask which control type a measure is. A sharps container or safety needle is engineering; a rule about how to do the task is work practice; gloves and face shields are PPE.

After a needlestick or splash

  1. Needlestick or cut: wash the site at once and thoroughly with soap and water.
  2. Splash to the nose, mouth or skin: flush with water. Eyes and mucous membranes: irrigate at the eyewash with water or saline for 15 minutes (OSHA laboratory guidance).
  3. Report to the supervisor at once.
  4. The employer makes a confidential medical evaluation available immediately and at no cost: route and circumstances documented, source patient identified and tested for HBV and HIV after consent, worker's blood collected after consent.
  5. Post-exposure prophylaxis, when indicated, follows U.S. Public Health Service recommendations and starts as soon as possible; counseling and evaluation of any later illness follow.
  6. The worker receives the health care professional's written opinion within 15 days of the evaluation.
Safety If the worker agrees to a baseline blood draw but not yet to HIV testing, the sample is kept for at least 90 days. If the worker consents within those 90 days, it is tested as soon as feasible.

Safety A laminar flow clean bench is not a BSC. It blows filtered air toward the worker and protects only the product: never use it for specimens or cultures.
  • Certify BSCs at least annually and whenever moved. No open flames in a BSC; use an electric loop sterilizer or disposable loops.
  • UV lamps are never the only disinfection method, and they are off when the room is occupied.
  • Use a BSC for pipetting, centrifuging, grinding, blending, shaking, mixing, sonicating and opening containers of infectious material. Wait 10 minutes after the rotor stops before opening a centrifuge used for infectious material.

  • A shipped container label carries six things: product identifier, signal word ('Danger' for severe, 'Warning' for less severe), hazard statements, pictograms, precautionary statements, and the responsible party's name, address and phone.
  • SDSs must be readily accessible to workers on every shift; electronic access is acceptable if nothing blocks immediate access.
Exam tip The numbers run in opposite directions. NFPA 704 diamond: 0 least to 4 most hazardous (blue health, red flammability, yellow instability, white special: OX, W, SA). HazCom categories: 1 most severe to 4 least severe.

Exam tip Triple packaging is a leakproof primary receptacle, a leakproof secondary packaging with enough absorbent for all the liquid, and a rigid outer packaging. Dry ice goes outside the secondary packaging, in a box that vents CO₂.

Sentinel laboratories (LRN)

Sentinel laboratories in the Laboratory Response Network provide routine diagnostic services, rule out and refer: when an isolate cannot be ruled out as a threat agent (such as Bacillus anthracis, Brucella, Francisella tularensis or Yersinia pestis), further work moves into the BSC and the isolate goes to an LRN reference laboratory, such as the state public health laboratory. National laboratories such as CDC do the specialized characterization.

Safety Do not use commercial identification systems (manual or automated, including MALDI-TOF) on a suspected threat agent, and never sniff plates. BMBL warns that their databases may not include these agents, MALDI-TOF can make aerosols, and sniffing Brucella cultures has caused laboratory infections.

The bloodborne pathogens standard in detail

TopicWhat 29 CFR 1910.1030 requires
Exposure incidentEye, mouth, other mucous membrane, non-intact skin or parenteral contact with blood or OPIM during work
OPIMSemen, vaginal secretions, CSF, synovial, pleural, pericardial, peritoneal and amniotic fluid, saliva in dental work, any visibly bloody fluid, fluids that cannot be told apart, unfixed human tissue, HIV- or HBV-containing cultures
Needles and sharpsContaminated needles are not bent, recapped or removed unless no alternative exists, and then only one-handed or with a device; never sheared or broken
Sharps containersClosable, puncture resistant, leakproof on sides and bottom, labeled or color-coded; upright, easily reached, replaced before they overfill
Broken glassNever picked up by hand; use a brush and dustpan, tongs or forceps
HandsWashed immediately or as soon as feasible after removing gloves or other PPE
Work areaNo eating, drinking, smoking, cosmetics, lip balm or contact lens handling; no food in refrigerators or on benches where blood is kept; no mouth pipetting
PPEProvided, cleaned, repaired and replaced at no cost; removed before leaving the work area; masks with eye protection or chin-length face shields whenever splashes or droplets can reach eyes, nose or mouth
SurfacesDecontaminated after any spill and at the end of the shift if they may have been contaminated
LabelsBiohazard symbol, fluorescent orange or orange-red; red bags or containers may replace labels
Regulated wasteLiquid or semi-liquid blood or OPIM, items that would release it if compressed, items caked with dried blood, contaminated sharps, pathological and microbiological waste
Sharps injury logDevice type and brand, work area and how the injury happened, kept confidential

OSHA's term is Universal Precautions: all human blood and certain body fluids are treated as if known to carry HIV, HBV and other bloodborne pathogens. In a laboratory this means every specimen, because the source is rarely known. BMBL places work with human blood, body fluids, tissues and primary cell lines at BSL-2 and points to the bloodborne pathogens standard for the specific precautions.

Risk assessment, aerosols and specific agents

Biosafety starts with a risk assessment of the agent and the procedure. BMBL notes that an agent's risk group does not by itself fix the laboratory level. The WHO Laboratory Biosafety Manual (4th edition, 2020) organizes controls by assessed risk rather than by fixed level: core requirements for all laboratories, heightened control measures when the assessed risk stays too high, and maximum containment measures for the highest-risk work.

Agent or situationBMBL recommendation
Mycobacterium tuberculosis complexClinical specimens treated as infectious (infective dose under 10 bacilli); BSL-3 for propagating and manipulating cultures; fix slides on a warming tray, not in a flame; bacilli can survive heat fixing
Brucella speciesBSL-3 for all culture work and for specimens suspected to contain Brucella; BSL-2 for routine clinical specimens; infections have followed sniffing cultures and open-bench work
Francisella tularensisBSL-3 for all work with suspect cultures, including setting up automated identification
Coccidioides speciesBSL-3 for sporulating mold cultures once identified; never make a slide culture from an unknown hyaline mold
Blastomyces dermatitidisBSL-3 for identified sporulating mold-form cultures; BSL-2 for clinical material and yeast-form cultures
  • An unknown white, cottony mold from a respiratory specimen, especially from an endemic area, stays closed (taped plate) and is opened only in a BSC.
  • Centrifuges: use safety buckets or sealed rotors when available, check the sealing rings, balance the load, load and unload the cups in the BSC, and decontaminate at once after a breakage.
  • Clinical laboratories keep work surfaces wiped with a disinfectant active against the agent, left on for its contact time. Spills of infectious material are cleaned by trained staff following a posted spill procedure.
  • HEPA filters remove at least 99.99% of particles at the most penetrating size (about 0.3 µm). They do not capture chemical vapors or gases.

Each pictogram is a black symbol on a white background inside a red square set on its point, and each appears only once on a label. NFPA 704 is a different system for emergency responders: it rates acute hazards only, while HazCom labels cover acute and chronic hazards for workers. OSHA warns against copying SDS hazard category numbers onto the NFPA diamond.

Laboratory Standard (29 CFR 1910.1450)Requirement
Chemical hygiene planWritten procedures, equipment, PPE and work practices that protect workers from the chemicals in that laboratory; reviewed at least annually
Plan contentsSOPs, criteria for exposure controls, fume hood performance checks, training, when prior approval is needed, medical consultation, a chemical hygiene officer, extra protection for select carcinogens, reproductive toxins and highly toxic substances
Chemical hygiene officerEmployee qualified by training or experience who guides development and use of the plan
Medical consultation or examinationOffered when a worker has symptoms of exposure, when monitoring routinely exceeds the action level (or PEL), and after a spill, leak or explosion likely to have caused a hazardous exposure; free, without loss of pay, by or under a licensed physician
Exposure monitoringWhen there is reason to believe exposure exceeds the action level or PEL; workers told of results within 15 working days
  • Eyewash and drench shower: facilities for quick drenching or flushing of the eyes and body are required within the work area, for immediate emergency use, wherever eyes or body can be exposed to injurious corrosive materials.
  • Fume hood vs BSC: use a chemical fume hood for volatile chemicals when biological containment is not needed. A BSC's HEPA filter does not stop vapors, and its electrical parts are not spark-proof.
  • Formaldehyde: OSHA's permissible exposure limit is 0.75 ppm as an 8-hour time-weighted average.
  • Chemical waste: collected at or near where it is made, in compatible, labeled containers kept closed; incompatibles kept apart; never evaporated in a hood.
  • Compressed gases: inert gases such as nitrogen can displace oxygen; a cylinder with a broken valve can become a projectile, so cylinders are secured and listed in the chemical inventory.
  • Cryogens and dry ice: liquid nitrogen boils at -196 °C and dry ice sublimes at -78 °C. Hazards are cold burns and asphyxiation from oxygen displacement.
Exam tip A chemical spill with no symptoms still triggers the offer of a medical consultation when the event made a hazardous exposure likely. Waiting for symptoms is the wrong answer.

Fire, electrical, radiation and ergonomics

  • OSHA calls fire the most common serious hazard in a typical laboratory. During a fire, put infectious materials in an autoclave, incubator, refrigerator or freezer if it is safe to do so.
  • Extinguishers are placed so that no one travels more than 75 ft (22.9 m) to one for Class A hazards or 50 ft (15.2 m) for Class B hazards; they are checked monthly and maintained yearly, and designated users are trained at least annually.
  • RACE wording varies between hospitals (the last step is written Extinguish, Evacuate or both). Clothing fire: stop, drop and roll, or use a fire blanket or safety shower when no chemicals or electricity are involved.
  • Electrical: remove from service equipment with frayed cords, damaged enclosures or a missing ground prong; do not plug or unplug equipment with wet hands; GFCIs on 120 V receptacles; outlets inside a BSC are GFCI-protected. Servicing equipment requires lockout/tagout.
  • Radiation: users of radioactive isotopes must be licensed by the NRC or an Agreement State. Exposures are kept as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA), applied through time, distance and shielding. The adult occupational limit is 5 rem (0.05 Sv) a year; areas are posted with the radiation symbol and access is restricted.
  • Ergonomics: repetitive pipetting, sorting and computer work and static posture cause musculoskeletal disorders. Controls include ergonomic pipettes and microscopes, arm supports, adjustable workstations, anti-fatigue mats, task rotation and devices for lifting full carboys.
  • Latex allergy: latex-free gloves, liners or powder-free alternatives must be readily available to allergic workers.

Waste and decontamination

Waste streamContainer and handling
Sharps (needles, blades, broken glass, slides, capillary tubes)Rigid, puncture-resistant, leakproof, labeled sharps container; replaced before full
Biohazard (regulated) wasteRed or biohazard-labeled bags or containers; decontaminated, usually by autoclave, or incinerated
Cultures and stocksDecontaminated before disposal by a verified method
Chemical wasteCompatible, labeled, closed containers; segregated by compatibility; handled through the safety office; never evaporated in the hood
Radioactive wasteHandled under the radiation license

A steam cycle for clean items can be 20 minutes at 121 °C, but loads of infectious waste need longer cycles that are validated for the typical load with thermocouples and biological indicators, then verified by monitoring each run (time, temperature, pressure) and periodic biological indicators.

Packaging and transport in detail

  • Assignment to Category A is based on the patient's history or symptoms, endemic local conditions or professional judgment. 49 CFR defines a culture as a pathogen intentionally propagated and keeps it separate from patient specimens (including specimens in transport media and blood culture bottles).
  • Category B (49 CFR 173.199): primary receptacles packed so they cannot break or leak into the secondary; secondary packaging cushioned inside a rigid outer packaging; at least one outer surface 100 mm by 100 mm; UN3373 square-on-point mark with sides of at least 50 mm and the name 'Biological substances, Category B' beside it; a name and phone number of a knowledgeable person on the paperwork or package.
  • Liquids by air: primary or secondary packaging withstands a 95 kPa pressure difference; no more than 1 L per primary receptacle and 4 L per outer package (refrigerant not counted). Solids by air: no more than 4 kg per outer package.
  • Category A packaging also withstands 95 kPa and -40 °C to +55 °C, has an itemized contents list between the secondary and outer packaging, and screw caps secured with tape or another positive means.
  • Dry ice: outside the secondary packaging, interior supports keep the secondary in place as the ice sublimes, the box lets CO₂ escape, and the package is marked 'Dry ice' or 'Carbon dioxide, solid'.
  • Every person who prepares or ships Category B packages must know these requirements. U.S. ground rules (49 CFR) and air rules differ in detail: 49 CFR excepts samples sent for routine testing unrelated to infectious disease, and WHO describes these as exempt specimens that still need three layers of packaging.
  • Received packages are inspected; a broken or leaking package is placed in a sealable container, the outside decontaminated, and handled by trained staff.

Select agents and the Laboratory Response Network

LRN levelRole
SentinelRoutine diagnostic services, rule-out and referral; decide which isolates go to a reference laboratory
ReferenceInvestigation or referral of specimens with LRN reagents and protocols; test high-risk clinical and environmental samples; train sentinel laboratories
NationalSpecialized characterization, bioforensics, select agent work and highly infectious agents (for example CDC)

The federal select agent list (42 CFR 73.3 and 73.4) includes Bacillus anthracis, Francisella tularensis, Yersinia pestis, Burkholderia mallei and pseudomallei, Coxiella burnetii, botulinum neurotoxins, Ebola, Marburg and variola viruses, among others. Brucella species are no longer on the list but remain BSL-3 culture agents that sentinel laboratories rule out and refer.

  • A clinical laboratory that identifies a select agent in a diagnostic specimen is exempt from the select agent rules for that specimen only if it reports the identification to CDC or APHIS and the specimen provider, submits APHIS/CDC Form 4 within 7 calendar days, secures the agent, and transfers or destroys it within 7 calendar days (30 days for botulinum neurotoxin and staphylococcal enterotoxins).
  • Identification of Bacillus anthracis, Bacillus cereus biovar anthracis, Burkholderia mallei or pseudomallei, botulinum neurotoxins or toxin-producing Clostridium, Ebola, Marburg, variola viruses, F. tularensis or Y. pestis is reported immediately by phone, fax or email, then Form 4 within 7 days (42 CFR 73.5 and 73.6).
