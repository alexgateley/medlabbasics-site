Cheat Sheet

Compatibility Testing and Blood Components

Pretransfusion testing, the 3-day rule, crossmatch types, emergency release, ABO compatibility for every component, storage and dating, modifications, QC and donor eligibility

Checked against current sources November 2026

Pretransfusion testing, step by step

  1. Positive identification of the patient and the sample at collection: the tube is labeled at the bedside with the patient's identifiers.
  2. Check the sample: correctly labeled, not too old (3-day rule), not grossly hemolyzed.
  3. Review the history: previous ABO/Rh, antibodies and special needs (irradiated, antigen-negative). A historical antibody stays on the record even if today's screen is negative.
  4. ABO/Rh typing of the patient (forward and reverse grouping, D typing).
  5. Antibody screen (indirect antiglobulin test against group O screening cells).
  6. Select units: ABO/Rh compatible, antigen-negative for any clinically significant antibody, modified as ordered.
  7. Confirm the ABO of each red cell unit (and D of units labeled D-negative) by retyping a segment.
  8. Crossmatch: electronic or immediate-spin if the screen is negative and there is no antibody history; AHG crossmatch if there is a clinically significant antibody.
  9. Label, inspect and issue; the sample and a donor sample are kept after transfusion.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Pretransfusion testing in detail

The federal rule for compatibility testing is short. 21 CFR 606.151 asks for a method that positively identifies recipient samples, a sample less than 3 days old when the recipient has been pregnant or transfused in the previous 3 months, a procedure that demonstrates incompatibility between donor cells and recipient serum or plasma, and procedures to expedite transfusion in life-threatening emergencies with a physician-signed record. AABB Standards and each facility's procedures fill in the detail.

Identification is where most fatal errors start. The Circular names ABO-incompatible transfusion from identification errors as the most common cause of severe acute hemolysis; serologic incompatibility missed in testing is much less common. The tube is labeled at the bedside with two patient identifiers and the date, and the label must match the request exactly. A blood bank never corrects a label; it recollects. Many facilities add a second, independently drawn sample before the first transfusion of group-specific red cells, because two typings on the same tube cannot detect wrong blood in tube.

The 3-day rule exists because transfusion or pregnancy in the previous 3 months may have started a primary or anamnestic antibody response that is still climbing. A sample from last week can be screen-negative while the patient's plasma today already contains antibody. When the history is unknown, the rule is applied as though exposure occurred.

The antibody screen tests the patient's plasma against two or three group O reagent red cells of known phenotype through the antiglobulin phase. A positive screen leads to antibody identification (see the Antibody Screen and Identification page) and to units negative for the antigen. A positive autocontrol or DAT leads to a DAT workup. Units are then confirmed: the ABO of every red cell unit and the D type of units labeled D-negative are retested from an attached segment, because the transfusion service cannot rely on the label alone.

Drug interference is now a routine cause of a panreactive screen. Daratumumab and other anti-CD38 antibodies bind CD38 on reagent red cells and make every screening and panel cell react at the antiglobulin phase, while the DAT is often negative. The usual fix is to treat the reagent cells with dithiothreitol (DTT), which denatures CD38. DTT also destroys Kell antigens (and other DTT-sensitive antigens such as Lutheran, Dombrock, Cartwright and Knops), so anti-K cannot be excluded: the patient gets K-negative units unless known to be K-positive. A baseline phenotype or genotype before therapy starts allows antigen-matched units later. If the screen with DTT-treated cells is negative and there is no antibody history, an electronic or immediate-spin crossmatch with K-matched units is acceptable.

Electronic, immediate-spin and AHG crossmatch

The full crossmatch has two jobs: confirm ABO compatibility (immediate spin) and detect unexpected IgG antibodies to donor antigens (antiglobulin phase). Once antibody screens became sensitive enough, the second job could be left to the screen in patients without clinically significant antibodies. For those patients only a check of ABO compatibility is required, either serologic (immediate spin) or computer.

Immediate-spin crossmatch mixes patient plasma with a saline suspension of donor cells, centrifuges for 15 to 30 seconds and reads for agglutination or hemolysis. It is fast but serologically fragile: cold agglutinins and rouleaux give false positives, and a very strong antibody can give a false negative through prozone. A positive IS crossmatch in a patient with a negative screen points first to ABO incompatibility (clerical error, wrong unit, A2 or A2B patient with anti-A1) or to a cold antibody; check the patient and unit types before anything else.

The AHG crossmatch adds 37 °C incubation (with LISS, PEG or another enhancement), washing and antihuman globulin, so it detects IgG coating of donor cells. Negative tube results are confirmed with IgG-coated check cells; if the check cells do not agglutinate, the AHG was neutralized or not added and the test is repeated. A positive AHG crossmatch with a negative screen suggests an antibody to a low-prevalence antigen on that donor's cells, a donor unit with a positive DAT, or a passive antibody. Electronic crossmatch is also more efficient: less sample, less handling, faster turnaround.

Emergency release, massive transfusion and Rh policy

When waiting for testing would be life-threatening, uncrossmatched group O red cells, or group-specific red cells once a current type is known, may be transfused before testing is complete (Circular). The physician signs a record justifying the emergency (21 CFR 606.151(e)). The blood bank draws or receives a sample as early as possible, completes ABO/Rh, screen and crossmatch on the units already given, and notifies the physician at once of any incompatibility. Units are treated as uncrossmatched until the screen is done.

The D-negative question is policy, not law. Only about 7% of the US population is group O D-negative. D-negative units are usually reserved for females of childbearing potential and children, to avoid anti-D and later hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn; males and older females receive O-positive. A Red Cross survey of its largest low-titer whole blood users found the policies for females of childbearing potential split three ways: O-positive whole blood allowed, O-negative whole blood only, or O-negative red cell components until typed. In D-negative patients who received D-positive emergency units, the anti-D rate in followed survivors was much higher than the rate quoted across all emergency patients (45% versus about 4% in the study the commentary cites).

Plasma for a patient of unknown group is group AB, because AB plasma has no anti-A or anti-B. Group AB donors are scarce, so many massive transfusion protocols use group A plasma with a low anti-B titer instead. Low-titer group O whole blood follows the same logic for its plasma. The Circular warns that 'low' titers are not standardized and that hemolysis has occurred even at low titers; each facility sets its titer cutoff (for example below 1:200 in one supplier's program), its indications and a maximum number of units per patient.

Massive transfusion was once defined as 10 or more red cell units in 24 hours, or more than one blood volume in 24 hours; newer definitions count units over shorter periods. Modeling and trial data support giving red cells, plasma and platelets in ratios between 1:1:1 and 1:1:2, where 'platelets' means single whole-blood-derived units (an apheresis dose counts as about six). Fibrinogen falls early and may need cryoprecipitate. The laboratory supports the protocol with rapid CBC, PT/INR, fibrinogen, ionized calcium and, where available, viscoelastic testing.

Component preparation

Whole blood is collected into 14 mL of anticoagulant-preservative per 100 mL of blood (63 mL for a 450 mL collection, 70 mL for 500 mL). Citrate chelates calcium (anticoagulant), phosphate buffers, dextrose feeds glycolysis, and adenine (CPDA-1 and the additive solutions) lets the cells keep making ATP, which is why dating rises from 21 days (CPD) to 35 days (CPDA-1) to 42 days (additive solution). A low-volume collection (300–404 mL into anticoagulant for 450 mL) is labeled Red Blood Cells Low Volume; plasma and platelets are not made from it, because the anticoagulant ratio is wrong.

  • Whole blood intended for platelets is held at 20–24 °C, and platelets are separated within 4 hours (or the system's time frame). Platelet-rich plasma is first separated from the red cells by a gentler spin; a harder second spin packs the platelets, which are resuspended in a small volume of plasma. Buffy coat pooling is the other route.
  • Red cells left after plasma removal go to 1–6 °C at once, with additive solution added if the system includes it.
  • Plasma for FFP is frozen at −18 °C or colder within 8 hours of collection (or the system's time frame); plasma frozen within 24 hours is PF24.
  • Cryoprecipitate: FFP is thawed at 1–6 °C, the cold-insoluble precipitate is recovered by centrifugation, and it is refrozen within 1 hour. The remaining plasma is Plasma Cryoprecipitate Reduced.
  • Apheresis collects platelets, plasma, red cells (single or double) or granulocytes with ACD-A anticoagulant and returns the rest to the donor. Granulocyte donors usually receive G-CSF or steroids, and hydroxyethyl starch is used to sediment red cells.
  • Frozen red cells: glycerol is added before freezing at −65 °C or colder; before use it is washed out with decreasing saline concentrations. Poor deglycerolization causes hemolysis in the patient. Frozen storage keeps rare phenotypes for up to 10 years.

Fractionation products (albumin, IVIG, clotting factor concentrates, RhIG) are made from pooled plasma by manufacturers and treated for viral inactivation; they are drugs, not blood components, and need no ABO compatibility.

Plasma, cryo and platelet details

Plasma is thawed at 30–37 °C in a water bath with an overwrap or in an FDA-cleared device. Thawed FFP, PF24 or PF24RT24 is kept at 1–6 °C and expires after 24 hours, or, if it was collected in a closed system, is relabeled Thawed Plasma and kept up to 4 more days. Factor V and especially factor VIII fall during that time; Thawed Plasma is not used for isolated factor deficiencies. Plasma is not given for volume expansion or to correct an INR of 1.7 or less.

Cryoprecipitated AHF is the fibrinogen source of choice when concentrate is not at hand; it also contains factor VIII, factor XIII and von Willebrand factor, but factor concentrates are preferred for hemophilia A, von Willebrand disease and factor XIII deficiency. Fibrinogen recovery is 50–60%; one unit per 7–10 kg raises fibrinogen by about 50–75 mg/dL (0.5–0.75 g/L). For pooling, each bag is rinsed with 10–15 mL of 0.9% saline. Large volumes of ABO-incompatible cryo can make the DAT positive. Pathogen Reduced Cryoprecipitated Fibrinogen Complex is a newer product made from pathogen-reduced plasma; it has a 5-day post-thaw shelf life and is not used for factor VIII replacement.

Room-temperature platelets carry the highest risk of bacterial sepsis of any component, mostly from gram-positive skin flora, because bacteria grow at 20–24 °C. Federal rules require every collector and transfusion service to control that risk with bacterial culture or rapid testing, pathogen reduction, or cold storage (21 CFR 606.145); which strategy is used sets dating at 5, 6 or 7 days, and some strategies need a secondary test before issue. A contaminated unit is quarantined with every component from the same collection, and the blood center is told. Cold-stored apheresis platelets (1–6 °C within 4 hours of collection, agitation optional, up to 14 days) are allowed only for actively bleeding patients when conventional platelets are unavailable or impractical; they must not be returned to cold inventory once warmed.

One apheresis unit raises an average adult's count by about 30,000–60,000/µL (30–60 × 10⁹/L); one whole-blood unit raises it by 5,000–10,000/µL. Response is judged with the corrected count increment: CCI = (post-count − pre-count) × body surface area (m²) ÷ platelets given (× 10¹¹). Example: (29,000 − 2,000) × 1.4 ÷ 4.5 = 8,400. In a stable patient the CCI is usually above 7,500 at 10–60 minutes and above 4,500 at 24 hours. Poor recovery at 1 hour suggests immune refractoriness (HLA class I or HPA antibodies: give HLA-matched or crossmatched platelets); good 1-hour recovery with poor 24-hour survival suggests a nonimmune cause (fever, sepsis, splenomegaly, DIC).

Safety Platelet transfusion is avoided in HIT, TTP and ITP unless bleeding is life-threatening. Granulocytes must never go through a leukocyte reduction or microaggregate filter, which removes them.

Leukocyte reduction, irradiation, CMV and pathogen reduction

A unit of whole blood contains about 1–10 × 10⁹ white cells. Leukocyte reduction by filter brings that below 5.0 × 10⁶ per unit (below 8.3 × 10⁵ for a single whole-blood platelet unit). It can be done during apheresis collection, soon after collection (prestorage), later in the laboratory, or at the bedside; bedside filtration escapes laboratory QC and can cause sudden hypotension, especially in patients taking ACE inhibitors. Prestorage reduction also removes white cells before they release cytokines into the bag, which is why it reduces febrile reactions better than filtering later. FDA expects QC plans to show with 95% confidence that more than 95% of units meet the white cell limit.

TA-GVHD happens when donor T lymphocytes engraft and attack a patient who cannot reject them: a severely immunodeficient patient, or an immunologically normal patient who shares an HLA haplotype with a donor homozygous for it (the reason for irradiating units from relatives and HLA-matched units). It is nearly always fatal, from marrow aplasia and infection. Leukocyte-reduced units still contain enough T cells to cause it. Irradiation (25 Gy to the center, at least 15 Gy anywhere in the bag) or a pathogen reduction process shown to inactivate lymphocytes prevents it. Irradiation shortens red cell dating to 28 days because of membrane damage and potassium leak; removing the supernatant helps neonates and others at risk of hyperkalemia.

CMV lives in donor mononuclear leukocytes, so only cellular components transmit it; plasma and cryo do not. Prestorage leukocyte reduction reduces CMV transmission to a level not significantly different from seronegative units and is accepted as an alternative. At-risk CMV-seronegative patients include pregnant women and their fetuses, low-birthweight infants, HPC and solid-organ transplant recipients, severely immunosuppressed patients and patients with HIV.

The FDA-approved pathogen reduction system for platelets and plasma adds amotosalen, a psoralen that intercalates into DNA and RNA and cross-links it under UVA light; an adsorption device then removes leftover amotosalen. It controls bacterial risk, replaces Babesia NAT in states that require it, and can replace irradiation. It does not inactivate everything: hepatitis A and E, parvovirus B19, poliovirus and Bacillus cereus spores resist some processes. Contraindications are psoralen allergy and neonates under phototherapy devices that emit below 425 nm. Pathogen-reduced platelets give somewhat lower count increments.

Issue, inspection and administration

  • Inspect before issue and before use: abnormal color, hemolysis, a bag whose color differs clearly from its segments (a possible sign of bacterial growth), clots, clumps, floccular material, cloudiness or a leak all stop the unit.
  • A compatibility tag stays on the unit until the transfusion is finished; final identification of patient and unit at the bedside rests with the transfusionist.
  • Every component goes through a 150–260 micron filter; only 0.9% sodium chloride may share the line.
  • Warm blood only with an FDA-cleared warmer (massive or exchange transfusion, cold agglutinins); overheating causes hemolysis.
  • A transfusion is completed within 4 hours of spiking the bag; red cells should not sit at room temperature longer than that.
  • Units returned to the blood bank can be reissued only if the seal is intact, a segment is attached, storage stayed at 1–6 °C (1–10 °C in transit), and the unit passes inspection (21 CFR 640.2).
  • Shipping temperatures: liquid red cells and whole blood 1–10 °C; frozen plasma and cryo −18 °C or colder; frozen red cells −65 °C or colder; room-temperature platelets as close to 20–24 °C as possible (21 CFR 600.15).

Donor eligibility and donor testing in detail

Eligibility is decided on the day of donation and before collection (except components that cannot be stored more than 24 hours, such as granulocytes, where it may be done up to 2 days before). The collector checks the deferral records, the interval since the last donation, the medical history and a physical assessment, and gets proof of identity, an address for 8 weeks and the donor's acknowledgement of the educational material. A donor who appears under the influence, gives unreliable answers or says the purpose is to get infectious disease test results is not eligible. So is a xenotransplant recipient, and anyone held 72 hours or more in a correctional institution in the past 12 months.

Deferrals: permanent for a positive HIV test or any antiretroviral treatment; 3 months after the last dose of oral PrEP or PEP and 2 years after the last injectable PrEP; 3 months after the last sexual contact for a new partner or more than one partner in the past 3 months combined with anal sex in the past 3 months; 3 months for exchanging sex for money or drugs, non-prescription injection drug use, sex with someone in those groups or with someone HIV-positive, allogeneic transfusion, a needlestick or contact with another person's blood, a tattoo or piercing not done with sterile single-use equipment in a state-regulated setting, and syphilis or gonorrhea (from completion of treatment). People with hemophilia who need factor concentrates are deferred for their own safety.

Donation testing must be nonreactive before a unit is labeled for routine use. Units tested at a reactive level are not released, the donor is deferred and notified, and further (supplemental) testing is done. Donor ABO is determined by two tests (different methods or reagent lots) that agree; D-negative units are tested for weak D, and any unit positive for weak D is labeled D-positive. Infectious disease markers, look-back and confirmatory algorithms are covered on the Transfusion Reactions and HDFN page.
