Try one

Urinalysis practice question

A patient arrives 14 hours after the sudden onset of a severe headache. CSF is collected in four sequential tubes and centrifuged within 30 minutes of collection. Results are: Tube 1 RBC 12,000/µL (12,000 × 10⁶/L) Tube 2 RBC 11,500/µL (11,500 × 10⁶/L) Tube 3 RBC 11,800/µL (11,800 × 10⁶/L) Tube 4 RBC 11,200/µL (11,200 × 10⁶/L) Supernatant: yellow after centrifugation CSF protein 62 mg/dL (620 mg/L) Serum total bilirubin 0.8 mg/dL (14 µmol/L) Which interpretation BEST fits these findings?

  1. Xanthochromia caused by a raised serum bilirubin
  2. A traumatic puncture of a vein during collection
  3. Red cell lysis from a delay in centrifugation
  4. Subarachnoid hemorrhage before the puncture

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Bilirubin crosses into CSF when serum levels are very high, as in severe neonatal jaundice; a serum bilirubin of 0.8 mg/dL is normal.
  • B. Blood from the needle clears progressively from tube 1 to tube 4 and leaves a colorless supernatant when the specimen is spun promptly.
  • C. Standing blood-stained CSF can turn the supernatant pink or yellow, but this specimen was spun within 30 minutes.
  • D. Correct. A count that stays level across tubes, xanthochromia and crenated cells mean the blood was in the CSF for hours before the needle entered.

Red cells that enter CSF during a bleed begin to lyse after roughly 6 hours, releasing oxyhemoglobin and then bilirubin that tint the supernatant yellow (xanthochromia). A traumatic tap introduces blood at the needle, so counts fall from the first to the last tube and the supernatant is clear if centrifuged promptly. Comparing the first and last tubes, examining the supernatant soon after collection, and excluding hyperbilirubinemia or very high CSF protein (above about 150 mg/dL) as other causes of yellow fluid are the laboratory steps that separate the two; here the protein is only mildly raised, as expected from the added blood. Macrophages containing red cells or hemosiderin also point to an earlier bleed. When doubt remains, hemorrhage is assumed or the puncture is repeated.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Collection and handling in detail

  • Midstream clean catch: void the first portion into the toilet and collect the middle. First-void urine carries more squamous cells, white cells and red cells. One older study found cleansing the genitals made little difference to contamination (32% vs 29%).
  • Catheterized patients (CDC CAUTI guideline, recommendation U): aspirate small volumes for urinalysis or culture from the needleless sampling port with a sterile syringe after disinfecting the port. Large volumes for special non-culture tests may be taken aseptically from the bag. Urine sitting in a drainage bag has grown at room temperature: reject it for culture.
  • 24-hour urine: at the start time empty the bladder into the toilet, then save every void, including the void at the same time the next day. Keep the container capped and cold. Saving the first void adds urine made before the start and overestimates clearance; a missed void underestimates it. Mix two containers before sampling.
  • Stability (Delanghe 2014): sediment red cells 1–4 h refrigerated; white cells last 1 hour at pH over 7.5 but up to 24 hours at pH under 6.5; bacteria double quickly at room temperature (stable only 1–2 h). Lysis is fastest in alkaline, dilute urine (a Proteus infection, a young child's urine).
  • Preservatives: boric acid (culture tubes; false-negative leukocyte esterase, protein and ketones), formaldehyde (false-negative blood, false-positive leukocyte esterase at 0.4 g/L, urobilinogen interference), sodium azide (false-positive leukocyte esterase). Too much urine dilutes the preservative.

Physical properties in detail

  • Clarity: cloudiness comes from phosphates (alkaline, white, dissolve in acid), urates (acid, pink or yellow-brown, dissolve on warming), white cells, red cells, bacteria, lipid, chyle, mucus or contaminants. A clear red urine suggests a dissolved pigment; a cloudy red urine suggests intact red cells.
  • Odor: ammonia (old urine, urea-splitting bacteria), fruity (ketones), sweet or maple syrup (maple syrup urine disease), musty or mousy (phenylketonuria), sulfur (cystine breakdown), fecal (a bowel-bladder fistula).
  • Specific gravity compares urine density with water. Glomerular filtrate starts near 1.010; tubules dilute it toward 1.003 in a water load and concentrate it above 1.020 when water is withheld. Below 1.010 suggests relative hydration and above 1.020 relative dehydration.
  • Refractometer QC: read deionized water (1.000) and a control urine of known value each shift.
  • Osmolality: random urine ranges widely (MedlinePlus gives 50–1,200 mOsm/kg); after 12–14 hours without fluid it should exceed 850 mOsm/kg. Compare it with serum osmolality: urine far below serum with high serum sodium points to diabetes insipidus.

Reagent strip details

  • Strips are read by eye against the color chart at the stated time, or by reflectance photometry. Protect strips from moisture: after one week of air exposure about a third of nitrite pads read falsely positive (Simerville).
  • Protein: tetrabromophenol blue or a similar indicator is held at a fixed pH; protein binding releases protons and shifts the color. Albumin reacts strongly; globulins and free light chains barely react. Approximate grades: trace 5–10 mg/dL, 1+ 30 mg/dL (0.3 g/L), 2+ 100 mg/dL (1.0 g/L), 3+ 300 mg/dL (3.0 g/L), 4+ 1,000 mg/dL (10 g/L); brands differ. A pad needs about 100–150 mg/L albumin to turn positive.
  • Glucose: glucose oxidase is specific for glucose. Glucose appears in urine when plasma glucose exceeds the renal threshold, about 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L), or when proximal reabsorption fails (renal glycosuria, Fanconi syndrome).
  • Ketones: in diabetic ketoacidosis most ketone is beta-hydroxybutyrate, which nitroprusside never detects. As treatment converts it back to acetoacetate the strip can rise while the patient improves: follow the acidosis with blood beta-hydroxybutyrate, not the urine strip.
  • Blood: the pad reacts with free hemoglobin and myoglobin as well as red cells, and is very sensitive; transient hematuria (exercise, menstruation) is common, so an isolated positive is repeated and confirmed by microscopy.
  • Bilirubin: only conjugated bilirubin is water soluble and filtered, so a positive pad means hepatocellular or obstructive disease, not hemolysis.
  • Urobilinogen: made by gut bacteria from bilirubin in bile. Hemolysis and hepatocellular disease raise it; complete bile duct obstruction or antibiotics lower it. The pad detects down to about 0.2 mg/dL, a normal level, so it cannot show that urobilinogen is absent (Multistix insert). It is not a reliable test for porphobilinogen.
  • Nitrite: gram-negative rods (Enterobacterales) reduce dietary nitrate to nitrite if urine stays in the bladder long enough, so first morning urine is best. Specific but insensitive (19–48% sensitivity for culture-proven infection in one review); a negative result does not rule out infection.
  • Leukocyte esterase: the pad reads granulocyte esterase, mainly from neutrophils, so a lymphocyte-rich urine can be negative. It still reacts after the cells lyse. Allow the full read time (up to 2 minutes).

Confirmatory tests in detail

  • SSA: acid added to urine denatures protein, and the turbidity is graded by eye or measured (3% to 25% SSA are in use). It detects about 50–100 mg/L of any protein, so it catches light chains, globulins and uromodulin that the albumin-sensitive pad misses. Strip negative with SSA strongly positive in an older patient with anemia, hypercalcemia and renal impairment suggests free light chains (Bence Jones protein); confirm with protein electrophoresis and immunofixation.
  • Ictotest: 10 drops of urine on the mat, one tablet, two drops of water; blue to purple on the mat within 60 seconds is positive. Phenazopyridine gives a red-orange that can hide small amounts of bilirubin; large doses of chlorpromazine and etodolac metabolites can give false or atypical results.
  • Clinitest (where still used): reduction of copper sulfate gives a color from blue (negative) through green and yellow to orange. It detects any reducing substance, including glucose, galactose, lactose, fructose and pentoses, and homogentisic acid. Watch the tube during the whole reaction: at high sugar concentrations the color passes rapidly through orange and ends greenish brown (pass-through), which can be read as a low result. With the 5-drop method this happens above about 4% sugar; the 2-drop method reads up to about 5% and delays pass-through to above about 10%. Its traditional use is screening children for inborn errors of carbohydrate metabolism such as galactosemia; newborn screening has narrowed that role.
  • Acetest: a tablet test for acetoacetate and acetone, usable on urine, serum or plasma; like the strip it misses beta-hydroxybutyrate.

Microscopic examination: method and look-alikes

  • Standard method: centrifuge about 10 mL at 400 g for 5 minutes, remove the supernatant by suction to leave a fixed volume, resuspend gently (rough handling breaks casts), and examine at least 10 fields at low power (100×) for casts and at high power (400×) for cells and crystals.
  • Phase contrast shows low-refractile elements (hyaline casts, dysmorphic red cells). Polarized light identifies lipids, crystals, starch and fibers. Sternheimer-Malbin stain separates nucleated cells; Sudan III or IV stains cholesterol and triglyceride, oil red O stains triglyceride.
  • Dysmorphic red cells: glomerular passage plus osmotic stress gives irregular outlines, blebs and ring forms. Criteria vary; one review proposes at least 40% dysmorphic cells, or at least 5% acanthocytes, or at least one red cell cast in 50 low-power fields, to call hematuria glomerular.
  • Maltese crosses: cholesterol and its esters (oval fat bodies, fatty casts) give a cross with equal, symmetric arms. Starch from powders gives an off-center cross with unequal arms on free, faceted granules with a central dimple. Leucine spheres can show a pseudo-Maltese cross; 2,8-dihydroxyadenine spheres show a Maltese cross too. Triglyceride droplets do not polarize.
  • Yeast vs red cells: yeast varies in size, buds and forms pseudohyphae, resists dilute acetic acid (which lyses red cells), and leaves the blood pad negative.
  • Pseudocasts: clumped urates or cells, hair, fibers, glass and other debris. True casts have parallel sides and the cylindrical shape of a tubule. Hemosiderin granules (coarse yellow-brown) mimic amorphous urates.
  • Eosinophiluria (Hansel or Wright stain) was taught as a marker of acute interstitial nephritis; it is now considered neither sensitive nor specific and is no longer recommended.
Exam tip Cells inside a cast came from the kidney, because casts form in the tubules from uromodulin (Tamm-Horsfall protein). White cells with white cell casts mean pyelonephritis or interstitial nephritis; the same white cells with no casts can be cystitis.

  • Uric acid forms when pH is 5.5 or lower; it is seen in normal urine, gout, diabetes and tumor lysis. Its hexagonal form is yellow, polarizes strongly and resists HCl; cystine is colorless, barely polarizes and dissolves in HCl and ammonia.
  • Calcium oxalate forms largely independent of pH. Many monohydrate crystals without dihydrate suggest very high oxalate excretion (primary or enteric hyperoxaluria). Elongated hexagons or shuttle-shaped monohydrate crystals suggest ethylene glycol poisoning.
  • Triple phosphate (struvite) in alkaline urine with infection points to a urease-producing organism such as Proteus; these patients form staghorn stones.
  • Cystine crystals are pathognomonic for cystinuria, an inherited defect in tubular reabsorption of cystine and the dibasic amino acids; even a few are significant.
  • 2,8-Dihydroxyadenine: round reddish-brown crystals with dark outlines and radiating "tree-ring" lines, Maltese cross under polarized light; diagnostic of APRT deficiency, which can cause crystal nephropathy.
  • Tyrosine and leucine: liver failure and inherited tyrosine disorders. Bilirubin crystals and bile casts mark cholemic nephropathy in severe liver failure.
  • Cholesterol plates accompany lipiduria (nephrotic syndrome, chyluria) and can also form in refrigerated urine.
  • Drug crystals (most in acid urine, most polarize): sulfonamides as striated "sheaves of wheat" or shells; amoxicillin as needle bundles like a brush; acyclovir as needles; ciprofloxacin as star or fan-shaped needles; indinavir as plates, stars or fans. Report them: they can precipitate in tubules and cause acute kidney injury.

Renal physiology the exam asks

  • Clearance is the plasma volume cleared of a substance per minute: C = (U × V) ÷ P, with V in mL/min (24-hour volume ÷ 1,440), and U and P in the same units. Normalize to body surface area: × 1.73 ÷ patient BSA. See the Measured Creatinine Clearance and eGFR CKD-EPI 2021 calculators.
  • Proximal tubule: reabsorbs nearly all filtered glucose, amino acids, phosphate and bicarbonate. Generalized failure (Fanconi syndrome) gives glycosuria with normal plasma glucose, aminoaciduria, phosphaturia and bicarbonate loss.
  • Concentration: antidiuretic hormone opens water channels in the collecting duct so urine can reach about 1,200 mOsm/kg. Without ADH (central diabetes insipidus, for example after pituitary surgery) or without a renal response (nephrogenic), urine stays dilute (SG near 1.003, osmolality far below serum) while serum sodium rises.
  • Acidification: urine pH runs 4.5–8.0. Inability to acidify urine below 5.5 after an acid load suggests renal tubular acidosis (distal type: acid blood, alkaline urine).
  • Proteinuria types: glomerular (albumin, most common), tubular (low-molecular-weight proteins, rarely over 2 g/day), overflow (light chains, hemoglobin or myoglobin exceeding reabsorption). Transient proteinuria follows fever, exercise, dehydration and stress; orthostatic proteinuria disappears after 8 hours lying down.
