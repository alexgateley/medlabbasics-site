Transfusion Reactions and HDFN

Reaction types with timing, mechanism and lab findings, the reaction workup, NHSN definitions, donor infectious disease testing, HDFN, RhIG and fetal bleed testing

Safety Any fever or chills during transfusion is treated as a possible acute hemolytic reaction until the workup says otherwise. Stop the transfusion, keep the line open with saline, and send the unit, the set and a new sample to the blood bank.

Workup of a suspected reaction

  1. Clerical check first: patient identity, sample label, unit label, tag and records. A mismatch means another patient may be at risk of the swapped unit.
  2. Visual check: centrifuge the postreaction sample and compare its plasma with the pretransfusion sample. New pink or red plasma means hemolysis; check urine for hemoglobin.
  3. DAT on the postreaction EDTA sample. If positive, run a DAT on the pretransfusion sample; positive after and negative before points to coated donor cells, so do an elution.
  4. Repeat ABO and Rh on the postreaction sample (and on the pretransfusion sample and the unit when indicated).
  5. Further tests when the first three are abnormal or symptoms suggest it: antibody screen and crossmatch on pre and post samples, LD, bilirubin, haptoglobin, DIC screen; Gram stain and cultures of the bag for sepsis; BNP for TACO; IgA and anti-IgA for anaphylaxis.
Exam tip Positive DAT after, negative before, with mixed-field reaction: transfused cells are coated. Eluate shows the antibody. Recheck the clerical work before anything else.
Safety Report suspected TRALI and septic reactions to the blood supplier at once so co-components from the same donation can be pulled. A transfusion fatality goes to the FDA (CBER) as soon as possible, with a written report within 7 days.

Exam tip CMV is not on the list: up to 70% of donors are seropositive. CMV-safe means leukoreduced or CMV-seronegative (or pathogen reduced) for at-risk patients such as low-birthweight infants and seronegative transplant recipients.

Exam tip Anti-K disease brings more anemia and fewer reticulocytes and less bilirubin than the titer would suggest, because the antibody destroys red cell precursors in the marrow as well as circulating cells.

RhIG and the fetal bleed

  • Candidate: D-negative mother, not already immunized to D (anti-D from RhIG given earlier does not count), baby D-positive or not known to be D-negative.
  • Not a candidate: D-positive mother, including weak D types 1, 2 and 3 by genotype; mother already immunized to D. Partial D mothers are managed as D-negative.
  • Doses: 300 µg at 26-28 weeks and within 72 hours of delivery (or of a miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, invasive procedure or trauma). If 72 hours is missed, give it anyway, up to 28 days.
  • Screen every D-negative mother after delivery of a D-positive baby with the rosette test. Positive means a bleed over about 10 mL of whole blood: quantify by Kleihauer-Betke or flow cytometry.

In depth

NHSN hemovigilance case definitions

CDC's NHSN Hemovigilance Module (protocol v3.0, January 2026) now asks U.S. facilities to report four groups: acute hemolytic reactions, TACO, TRALI and transfusion-transmitted infections, with everything else under 'Other'. The earlier definitions are kept in an archive section and are still what textbooks and exam items use. Each reaction is graded three ways: case definition (definitive, probable, possible), severity (non-severe, severe, life-threatening, death) and imputability (definite, probable, possible, doubtful, ruled out).

A transfusion-transmitted infection alert goes to CDC within 72 hours when a pathogen of interest is found in a patient transfused in the previous 30 days, and the investigation form is started within 7 days. Adverse reaction forms are submitted within 30 days of onset. NHSN reporting is voluntary unless a state requires it; FDA fatality reporting is mandatory.

Nonimmune hemolysis gives a hemolysis picture with negative serology. Causes are hypotonic fluids in the line, drugs given in the same line, overheating in a warmer that is not FDA-cleared, freezing, and pressure through small or damaged needles. In patients with sickle cell disease a hemolytic reaction can destroy the patient's own cells too (hyperhemolysis), leaving hemoglobin lower than before the transfusion; the antibody may not be identifiable.

Febrile reactions occur after less than 1% of leukoreduced red cell units and about 5% of leukoreduced apheresis platelets. Allergic reactions follow 1-3% of plasma-containing components. Anaphylaxis is rare, under 10 per 100,000 units. TA-GVHD risk groups are fetuses getting intrauterine transfusions, at-risk neonates, stem cell transplant recipients, severe cellular immunodeficiency (not HIV), patients on purine analogs such as fludarabine, and anyone receiving units from relatives or HLA-matched donors.

Massive transfusion adds metabolic risks: citrate binding ionized calcium (worse in liver disease; measure ionized calcium, because total calcium does not show it), high or low potassium, acid-base change, and hypothermia from cold blood, which should be warmed only in an FDA-cleared warmer.

Donor testing rules

  • 21 CFR 610.40 requires testing each donation for HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C; and for HTLV, syphilis, West Nile virus and Chagas disease, with exceptions for Source Plasma and FDA-accepted alternatives.
  • Tests must be FDA licensed, approved or cleared and run by the manufacturer's instructions. A reactive screen is followed by a supplemental test when one exists.
  • Autologous units need not be tested unless they could be crossed over to other patients or are shipped to a facility that allows that; then every donation is tested. Crossed-over units must meet all allogeneic requirements.
  • Release before testing is allowed only in a documented medical emergency, with results reported promptly.
  • Babesia NAT applies to donations in the 14 states (and DC) that FDA named in 2019 as endemic or next to an endemic state.
  • Donor plasma is also screened for unexpected red cell antibodies on every donation.

HDFN mechanism and monitoring

HDFN happens when a maternal IgG antibody crosses the placenta and binds an antigen on fetal red cells. The coated cells are removed by the fetal spleen, causing anemia; severe anemia leads to hydrops. Before birth the placenta clears bilirubin; after birth the newborn liver cannot keep up, and unconjugated bilirubin can damage the brain (kernicterus). Anti-D, anti-K and anti-E are the antibodies most often found; anti-c also causes severe disease. IgM antibodies (most anti-M, anti-Lewis) do not cross; IgG forms of anti-M can, rarely.

TestWhen and why
ABO, Rh and antibody screenAt the first prenatal visit; D-negative patients are tested again later in pregnancy
Antibody identificationAny positive screen; separate passive anti-D after RhIG from active immunization by history
Titer (indirect antiglobulin, doubling dilutions)Clinically significant IgG antibody; repeated about monthly to 24 weeks, then every 2 weeks; at or above the critical titer the fetus is watched by Doppler
Father's phenotype or fetal genotype (cell-free DNA)Shows whether the fetus carries the antigen; an antigen-negative fetus needs no monitoring
Middle cerebral artery DopplerPeak systolic velocity 1.5 MoM or more suggests moderate to severe fetal anemia; leads to fetal blood sampling and intrauterine transfusion

Blood for intrauterine transfusion must be negative for the antigen, compatible with the mother's plasma, irradiated (the fetus is at risk of TA-GVHD) and CMV-safe.

Cord blood and newborn testing

  • Cord blood ABO, D and DAT for babies of D-negative mothers (the baby's D type decides RhIG) and when HDFN is suspected.
  • ABO HDFN: in one study of 4,122 newborns, the DAT detected 66% of cases but its negative predictive value was 99.6%, and positive DATs from ABO incompatibility occurred only with group O mothers. Diagnosis rests on the course of the jaundice, not the DAT alone.
  • A positive DAT with no ABO or known alloantibody cause: test the eluate; a negative eluate against A and B cells excludes ABO antibody as the cause.
  • RhIG given to the mother before delivery can make the baby's DAT positive and the mother's antibody screen positive. Neither stops the postpartum dose.
  • RhIG is never given to the newborn.

Fetal bleed tests in detail

TestPrincipleLimits
Rosette (screen)Maternal blood is incubated with anti-D, which coats any D-positive fetal cells; D-positive indicator cells then cluster around them and the rosettes are counted. Detects about 10 mL of fetal whole blood (5 mL of red cells).Only for a D-negative mother and D-positive baby. False negative with fetal weak D or variant D; false positive with maternal weak D or a positive maternal DAT.
Kleihauer-Betke (quantitative)Fixed smear in acid buffer: adult hemoglobin elutes and maternal cells become pale ghosts; fetal hemoglobin resists, and fetal cells stain pink. Count fetal cells against total cells.Labor-intensive and observer-dependent; overestimates when the mother has raised F cells (HPFH, sickle cell disease, beta thalassemia, and pregnancy itself).
Flow cytometry (quantitative)Fluorescent anti-HbF (with anti-carbonic anhydrase to mark adult F cells) or anti-DMore precise; not available in every lab; may still overestimate with high maternal F cells unless dual labeling is used

Most bleeds at delivery are under 30 mL, so a negative rosette test means the standard 300 µg vial is enough. The 5,000 mL maternal blood volume is a fixed estimate and may underestimate the bleed in larger patients. A second example: 24 fetal cells in 2,000 counted is 1.2%, which is 60 mL; 60 ÷ 30 = 2.0, which stays 2, plus 1 = 3 vials. The RhoGAM label gives a different rule (20 µg per mL of fetal red cells, rounded up to the next whole syringe); exam questions use the round-and-add-one rule.

Weak D and RhIG: women whose weak D is typed by genotype as type 1, 2 or 3 (some recent guidance also includes 4.0 and 4.1) are managed as D-positive and do not need RhIG. Partial D and other variants with missing epitopes can make anti-D, so these women are managed as D-negative. A large fetal bleed into a D-negative mother can make her weak D test weakly positive with a mixed-field look; this is fetal cells, not maternal weak D, and calls for a fetal bleed quantification.

Exam tip RhIG is also given within 72 hours after a D-negative patient receives D-positive red cells or platelets by necessity; the dose depends on the volume of D-positive red cells.
