Red Cell Morphology and the Anemias

Size, color, shape and inclusions on the smear, anemia by MCV with the tests that sort it, hemoglobin variants and thalassemias, hemolytic anemias and erythrocytosis

Checked against current sources November 2026

Exam tip Burr cell versus acanthocyte: burr cells have many short, even scallops all round and keep central pallor; acanthocytes have a few irregular spikes of uneven length and look dense. Burr cells in only one part of the slide are usually a drying artifact.
Safety Schistocytes with a falling platelet count suggest a thrombotic microangiopathy (TTP, HUS) or DIC. Report them promptly and tell the provider.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Iron deficiency in stages

StageWhat has changedResults
1. Stores depletedMarrow iron fallsFerritin under 30 ng/mL (µg/L), TIBC rising; Hb and serum iron still normal
2. Iron-deficient erythropoiesisSupply to the marrow is shortSerum iron under 50 mcg/dL (9 µmol/L), transferrin saturation under 15%, soluble transferrin receptor above 8.5 mg/L; indices may still be normal
3. Iron deficiency anemiaHemoglobin fallsMicrocytic, hypochromic cells, high RDW, pencil cells and elliptocytes when severe; no polychromasia or stippling

Merck reference values for adults: serum iron 75–150 mcg/dL (13–27 µmol/L) in men and 60–140 mcg/dL (11–25 µmol/L) in women, TIBC 250–450 mcg/dL (45–81 µmol/L), transferrin saturation 20–50%, ferritin 30–300 ng/mL. The ASCP composite table has no iron studies, so the exam gives these when it needs them. Transferrin saturation = serum iron ÷ TIBC × 100 (see the Electrolytes, Acid-Base and Blood Gases page for UIBC and TIBC). The most sensitive and specific sign is absent stainable iron in the marrow, but marrow is rarely needed.

Sideroblastic anemia and lead

  • Ringed sideroblasts are marrow erythroblasts whose iron-loaded mitochondria sit in a ring round the nucleus, shown with Prussian blue (Perls) iron stain.
  • Peripheral blood: a dimorphic picture (a hypochromic population with a normal one), stippling and Pappenheimer bodies. Congenital forms are usually microcytic; acquired forms are normocytic or macrocytic.
  • Iron, ferritin and transferrin saturation are high.
  • Causes: X-linked ALAS2 mutation (hereditary), myelodysplasia (acquired), alcohol, lead, zinc excess or copper deficiency, vitamin B6 deficiency, drugs (isoniazid, linezolid, chloramphenicol).
  • Lead inhibits pyrimidine 5′-nucleotidase, so ribosomal RNA is not broken down: coarse basophilic stippling. Lead also blocks iron insertion into protoporphyrin, so zinc protoporphyrin (ZPP) rises. Blood lead is measured on EDTA whole blood.

Exam tip MMA separates the two: high in B12 deficiency, normal in folate deficiency. Kidney failure also raises MMA.

Non-megaloblastic macrocytosis has round (not oval) macrocytes and no hypersegmented neutrophils. Chronic alcohol use gives an MCV of about 100–105 fL; liver disease adds membrane lipid (round macrocytes and target cells); a high reticulocyte count raises the MCV because reticulocytes are large.

Safety A transfusion in the last 3–4 months mixes donor hemoglobin into the sample and can hide or dilute a variant (and give a false-negative solubility test). Iron deficiency lowers Hb A2 and can hide beta-thalassemia trait: treat the iron deficiency and retest.

In beta-thalassemia, chronic transfusion and increased absorption cause iron overload: ferritin is followed, and chelation usually starts when it passes 1000 ng/mL.

Exam tip Thalassemia trait versus iron deficiency: both are microcytic, but trait has a normal or high RBC count, a normal RDW and normal ferritin. Iron deficiency has a low RBC count, a high RDW and low ferritin.

Exam tip EPO is the sorting test: low in polycythemia vera, high in secondary erythrocytosis. A microcytic erythrocytosis (high RBC count, low MCV) is a hallmark of PV with iron deficiency.

Reticulocyte calculations

  • Absolute reticulocytes (× 10³/µL) = retic % × RBC (× 10⁶/µL) × 10. ASCP range 20–115 × 10³/µL.
  • Corrected reticulocyte % = retic % × (patient Hct ÷ 45).
  • Reticulocyte production index (RPI) = corrected retic % ÷ maturation time in days (Hct 40% or more: 1.0; 35–39: 1.5; 25–34: 2.0; 15–24: 2.5; under 15: 3.0).
  • RPI 2 or more: the marrow is responding (bleeding or hemolysis). RPI under 2: a production problem.
  • Worked example: retic 6%, Hct 27%, RBC 3.0 × 10⁶/µL. Absolute = 6 × 3.0 × 10 = 180 × 10³/µL. Corrected = 6 × 27 ÷ 45 = 3.6%. Maturation time at Hct 27% = 2.0 days, so RPI = 1.8: an inadequate response despite a raised percentage.
  • See the Reticulocyte Count and Red Cell Indices calculators.

Smear artifacts and traps

  • Burr cells and stomatocytes appear when a smear dries slowly in humid air or a sample is old; look in a well-made area and on a fresh smear before reporting them.
  • Poor or late fixation gives crenated cells with refractile borders that hide hypochromia. Fix within an hour of making the smear (no later than 4 hours).
  • Malaria can be present with a normal automated CBC: a smear review is the test when it is suspected.
