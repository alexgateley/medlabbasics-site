Indices, RDW and the rule of three

  • MCV (fL) = Hct (%) × 10 ÷ RBC (×10⁶/µL). Under 76 fL is microcytic and over 100 fL macrocytic on the ASCP range (many laboratories use 80 fL as the lower limit).
  • MCH (pg) = Hgb (g/dL) × 10 ÷ RBC (×10⁶/µL).
  • MCHC (g/dL) = Hgb (g/dL) × 100 ÷ Hct (%).
  • RDW is the spread of red cell sizes. RDW-CV = SD of the size curve ÷ MCV × 100, so it moves with MCV; RDW-SD (in fL) does not.
  • A high RDW with a normal MCV can be the only sign of two populations (one small, one large) whose average looks normal.
  • Rule of three: RBC × 3 ≈ Hgb, and Hgb × 3 ≈ Hct ± 3. It holds only for normocytic, normochromic cells; a failure in such a patient means a result needs checking.
Exam tip An MCHC above 36 g/dL is the classic flag of a spurious CBC: think cold agglutinin, lipemia, hemolysis or icterus first. Only marked spherocytosis or sickle cells (possibly acanthocytes) raise it for real, and a smear tells you which. An MCHC under 28 g/dL is the other alarm.

Try one

CBC, Analyzers and Spurious Results practice question

A blood center refers a donor who has given whole blood every 8 weeks for 2 years to an outpatient clinic laboratory. Results are: Hgb 13.1 g/dL (131 g/L) MCV 84 fL RDW 13.8% (0.138) Ferritin 9 ng/mL (9 µg/L; reference 30-300 ng/mL) Serum iron 78 µg/dL (14.0 µmol/L; reference 60-150 µg/dL, 10.7-26.9 µmol/L) TIBC 420 µg/dL (75.2 µmol/L; reference 250-450 µg/dL, 44.8-80.6 µmol/L) Which interpretation BEST fits these results?

  1. Iron-deficient erythropoiesis, with transferrin saturation already under 15%
  2. Anemia of chronic inflammation with iron held in storage
  3. Iron deficiency anemia with microcytic, hypochromic red cells
  4. Depleted iron stores without anemia or iron-deficient erythropoiesis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Saturation is 78 ÷ 420 = 18.6%, not under 15%, and serum iron is within range. Iron-deficient erythropoiesis is the next stage, when serum iron and saturation fall.
  • B. Inflammation raises ferritin as an acute-phase reactant and tends to lower TIBC. A very low ferritin with a high-normal TIBC is the opposite pattern.
  • C. This is the last stage. The hemoglobin of 13.1 g/dL and MCV of 84 fL are within the reference ranges, so there is neither anemia nor microcytosis yet.
  • D. Correct. A ferritin of 9 ng/mL shows exhausted stores, while hemoglobin, MCV, serum iron and saturation are still normal. This is the first stage of iron deficiency.

Iron deficiency develops in stages. First, storage iron is used up: ferritin falls under 30 ng/mL and transferrin (TIBC) rises, but hemoglobin and serum iron are still normal. Next, iron-deficient erythropoiesis begins: serum iron and transferrin saturation fall and the soluble transferrin receptor rises. Last, microcytic, hypochromic anemia appears. Transferrin saturation is serum iron ÷ TIBC × 100, here 78 ÷ 420 × 100 = 18.6%. Ferritin is the first test to change, which is why frequent donors are monitored with it. Each whole blood donation removes iron that the donor must replace.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; CDC: Recommendations to Prevent and Control Iron Deficiency in the United States (1998)

How analyzers count

PrincipleWhat it measuresWeak point
Electrical impedance (Coulter principle)Each cell in an electrolyte passes through a small aperture between two electrodes; the pulse is proportional to cell volume. Particles in a set size window are counted as that cell.Anything of the right size is counted: microcytes, fragments, lipid particles and bacteria in the platelet window; giant platelets and clumps fall outside it.
Light scatter (flow cell)Low-angle (forward) scatter tracks size; high-angle (side) scatter tracks internal complexity and granularity. Two-dimensional plots separate cell types.Cells with unusual size or granules can land in the wrong cluster; the analyzer flags rather than identifies.
Fluorescence flow cytometryA nucleic acid dye stains RNA and DNA, so reticulocytes and platelets (separated from red cell fragments) are counted by fluorescence plus scatter; some analyzers run the whole WBC differential this way.Fewer interferences, but still needs smear review for flagged samples.
VCS (volume, conductivity, scatter)Impedance gives volume, radiofrequency conductivity gives internal composition (nucleus, N:C ratio), laser scatter gives granularity.Same limits as other flow methods: abnormal cells are flagged, not named.
Immunological platelet count (CD41/CD61 flow)Labeled antibodies to GPIIb-IIIa; the ICSH/ISLH reference method for platelets.A reference method, not a routine one.
Exam tip When impedance gives a falsely high platelet count from microcytes or fragments, an optical or fluorescent platelet count, or a smear estimate, is the fix. When it gives a falsely low count from giant platelets, use a smear estimate or a phase-microscopy manual count.

Safety Verify a new platelet count under 100 ×10³/µL, or a drop of 50% or more, on the smear before it is released. A falsely low count can lead to a platelet transfusion or a cancelled procedure, and a true fall may be heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.

Corrections and estimates to know by hand

  • Corrected WBC = uncorrected WBC × 100 ÷ (100 + nRBCs per 100 WBCs). The ASCP lists it for more than 10 nRBCs. Example: 15.0 ×10³/µL with 25 nRBCs → 15.0 × 100 ÷ 125 = 12.0 ×10³/µL.
  • Citrate (blue top) tube results: multiply WBC, RBC, Hgb, Hct and platelets by 1.1 (9 parts blood to 1 part citrate). Indices need no correction.
  • Diluted sample: multiply WBC, RBC, Hgb, Hct and platelets by the dilution factor; MCV, MCH, MCHC and RDW are unchanged.
  • Platelet estimate: average platelets per 100× oil field (10 fields) × 15 = ×10³/µL (laboratories use factors of 10 to 20).
  • Absolute count = WBC × percentage, or for retics, RBC × retic %. Example: 2.0% × 3.50 ×10⁶/µL × 10 = 70 ×10³/µL.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Histograms and scatterplots in words

A histogram plots cell size (x-axis) against the number of cells (y-axis). A scatterplot (scattergram) plots two measurements against each other, such as side scatter against fluorescence, and each cell type forms a cluster. The analyzer sets discriminators between populations and raises a flag when cells fall between or outside them.

PatternWhat it means
RBC curve shifted left (smaller)Microcytic population (low MCV)
RBC curve shifted right (larger)Macrocytic population; large numbers of reticulocytes also shift it right
RBC curve broad, or with two peaksHigh RDW: mixed populations (for example a combined microcytic and macrocytic picture with a normal MCV)
Platelet curve does not fall to baseline at the upper end and runs into the RBC curveMicrocytes or fragments counted as platelets, or giant platelets crossing the discriminator
Platelet curve with a long right tail and a low countLarge or giant platelets or clumps: the count is falsely low (one example in Cattaneo 2025 had platelets past a 25 fL discriminator)
WBC histogram or scatterplot with extra small particles at the lower WBC thresholdParticles counted as WBCs: nRBCs, platelet clumps or unlysed red cells
Cells between or outside clusters on the differential plotSuspect flags (blasts, immature granulocytes, atypical lymphocytes): do a smear
Exam tip Flags are prompts, not results. No analyzer reports target cells, sickle cells, Howell-Jolly bodies, Auer rods, toxic granulation or platelet satellitism, and none flags WBC clumps. The smear finds them.

Recognizing an unreliable CBC

  1. Read the flags, histograms and scatterplots for every sample.
  2. Check the delta: a large change from the patient's last result needs a reason (transfusion, dilution, wrong patient, clot).
  3. Check internal consistency: the rule of three, MCHC above 36 or under 28 g/dL, a result that does not fit the patient.
  4. Look at the tube: settled or spun, for lipemia, hemolysis, icterus, agglutinates, cryoprecipitate and clots. Chemistry HIL indices help.
  5. Look at the smear: clumps of platelets, WBCs or RBCs, fibrin strands, organisms, fragments, giant platelets, nRBCs, fat globules.
After Gulati 2013. Each laboratory writes its own criteria; the International Consensus Group for Hematology Review criteria are a starting point.

Worked example: a cold agglutinin

ResultFirst run (room temperature)After 15 min at 37 °C
RBC (×10⁶/µL)1.804.00

Hct (%)17.036.0
MCV (fL)94.490.0

MCHC (g/dL)70.633.3

The first run fails both rules of three (1.80 × 3 = 5.4, not 12.0; 12.0 × 3 = 36, not 17) and the MCHC is impossible. Hemoglobin is unchanged because the cells are lysed for that measurement; the cell-counting results are wrong because doublets and clumps are counted as single large cells or not at all. After warming, the indices are normal and the rules hold. Report the warmed results, and confirm on a smear made right after warming that the agglutination is gone.

Pseudothrombocytopenia in depth

  • About 0.1% of all blood counts, and 15–17% of low platelet counts in outpatients.
  • Mechanism: naturally occurring antibodies (usually IgG, sometimes IgM) bind hidden GPIIb-IIIa epitopes exposed in EDTA. Most act like cold agglutinins (best at 4–20 °C). The count falls within minutes at room temperature, reaches its low point at about 2 hours and does not recover on rewarming to 37 °C, unlike a red cell cold agglutinin, so warming is not the fix.
  • Not a disease: the patient has no bleeding tendency from it. Treating the number (transfusion, cancelled surgery) is the harm.
  • Citrate and heparin do not prevent clumping in every case, so a citrate count must still be checked on its own smear. The smear is the diagnostic standard.
  • Satellitism (platelets adhering to neutrophils) is also EDTA-dependent, much rarer (about 1 in 12,000 counts) and usually lowers the count only slightly; it is mediated by IgG bridging GPIIb-IIIa and neutrophil Fc receptors (FcγRIII). Analyzer flags for it are inconsistent, so the smear makes the diagnosis.
  • Giant platelets (wider than about 7 µm) occur in inherited macrothrombocytopenias such as MYH9-related disease and Bernard-Soulier syndrome, in MPN and MDS, after splenectomy and in immune destruction. Type 2B von Willebrand disease shows large platelets and clumps.

Reticulocytes and the IRF

  • Reticulocytes are young red cells that still hold RNA. A supravital stain shows the RNA as a blue network; on a Wright stain the same cells look blue-gray and larger (polychromasia).
  • Analyzers count reticulocytes by fluorescence: a nucleic acid dye marks RNA and the analyzer counts cells by fluorescence and scatter. Automated counts replaced the microscope from the mid-1990s.
  • Absolute retic = retic % × RBC (×10⁶/µL) × 10, in ×10³/µL. ASCP range 0.5–2.5% and 20–115 ×10³/µL.
  • In anemia the percentage overstates production. Corrected retic % = retic % × patient Hct ÷ 45 (the normal hematocrit). Reticulocytes released early in anemia (shift reticulocytes) stay longer in the blood, so the reticulocyte production index (RPI) divides the corrected count by the maturation time for the hematocrit: 1.0 day at Hct 40% or more, 1.5 at 35–39%, 2.0 at 25–34%, 2.5 at 15–24%, 3.0 under 15% (the Reticulocyte Count calculator uses these bands; some texts draw them slightly differently, so use the factor a question gives).
  • RPI over 3 means an appropriate marrow response (hemolysis or blood loss); under 2 means an inadequate response to the anemia. Example: retic 6.0%, Hct 27% → 6.0 × 27 ÷ 45 = 3.6 corrected; 3.6 ÷ 2.0 = 1.8, inadequate.
  • A high count in anemia means destruction, blood loss or recovery; a low or normal count in anemia means the marrow is not keeping up.
  • IRF (immature reticulocyte fraction): the share of reticulocytes that are youngest, with the most RNA. The analyzer sorts reticulocytes into low, medium and high fluorescence; IRF is the medium plus high fractions. It rises early when the marrow speeds up and is an early marker of engraftment after stem cell transplant.
  • Reticulocyte hemoglobin (CHr or Ret-He) shows the iron available to the marrow over the last 3–4 days. Values under 27 pg (CHr) point to iron-deficient erythropoiesis.

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)

  • What it measures: how far red cells fall in a vertical tube in one hour (mm/h). Proteins such as fibrinogen and immunoglobulins coat the cells, which then stack (rouleaux) and fall faster.
  • Westergren method (ICSH reference): blood diluted 4 parts to 1 part sodium citrate, read at 60 minutes. Black-top citrate tubes or EDTA tubes; testing.com gives two hours from collection as the processing limit.
  • Modified and alternate methods (automated, often on the EDTA CBC tube; some read in 20 minutes and convert to Westergren values) now outnumber Westergren: ICSH found 72% of laboratories using them and differences from Westergren of up to 142%, so each method needs its own reference values.
  • Raised by inflammation (fibrinogen, globulins), paraproteins, anemia, pregnancy, older age and female sex.
  • Lowered or unreliable with polycythemia, sickle cells, spherocytes and very high plasma viscosity; severe anemia also makes it unreliable.
  • Typical Westergren values: men under 50, 0–15 mm/h; men 50 and over, 0–20; women under 50, 0–20; women 50 and over, 0–30. Ranges vary by laboratory and method.
Exam tip Rouleaux on a smear (red cells stacked like coins in the thin area) point to high fibrinogen or globulin, as in myeloma, and go with a high ESR. They usually do not upset the CBC; agglutination (irregular clumps) does.

Manual counts with the hemocytometer

  • Cells/µL = cells counted × dilution factor ÷ (area counted in mm² × depth in mm). A Neubauer large square is 1 mm × 1 mm and the chamber is 0.1 mm deep, so each large square holds 0.1 µL.
  • Example: 150 cells in 8 large squares (4 per side), dilution 1:20 → 150 × 20 ÷ (8 × 0.1) = 3,750 cells/µL (3.75 ×10³/µL).
  • Count both sides; they must agree within the laboratory's limit before averaging.
  • Count cells touching the boundary lines on two sides only (for example top and left) so none is counted twice.
  • Giant platelets and clumps: a phase-microscopy manual platelet count, or a smear estimate, replaces the analyzer count.

Body fluid counts on analyzers

  • Many hematology analyzers have a body fluid mode (for example for CSF, pleural fluid and ascites), reporting total nucleated cells, WBC, RBC and a simple differential.
  • ICSH asks each laboratory to verify body fluid counting before use, the same way it verifies blood counts. Verification at the low counts typical of CSF (ASCP range 0–5/µL) matters most.
  • Macrophages, mesothelial cells and tumor cells fall outside the WBC differential and are counted as high-fluorescence body fluid cells in the total nucleated count; the analyzer cannot identify them.
  • The reference differential is manual: at least 100 cells on a cytocentrifuge slide (CLSI H56-A). Suspected malignant cells need slide review.
  • Body fluid cells, especially in CSF, deteriorate quickly: count promptly.
Exam tip A high-fluorescence cell count in a fluid can be macrophages, mesothelial cells or tumor cells. Only a stained slide tells them apart.
