  • Hydrate swap: g of new form = g of old form × (molar mass of new ÷ molar mass of old). 17.8 g Na₂HPO₄·2H₂O (177.99) = 17.8 × 268.07 ÷ 177.99 = 26.8 g of the heptahydrate.
  • Concentrated acid: M = specific gravity × 1000 × purity (as a fraction) ÷ molar mass. 96% H₂SO₄, SG 1.84: 1.84 × 1000 × 0.96 ÷ 98.08 = 18.0 M (36.0 N).
Safety When diluting a concentrated acid, add the acid slowly to the water, never water to acid: the heat released can make the liquid boil and spatter.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Concentration step by step

The SI name for molarity is amount (substance) concentration in mol/L. IUPAC and NIST call the words 'molarity' and 'normality' obsolete, but the exam and many procedures still use M and N, so learn both. Percent with no qualifier is ambiguous: always say w/v, v/v or w/w.

  1. Percent w/v to molarity (0.9% NaCl): 0.9 g per 100 mL = 9 g per L. 9 g/L ÷ 58.44 g/mol = 0.154 mol/L (154 mmol/L). Reading 0.9% as 0.9 g/L gives a tenfold error.
  2. Grams for a molar solution (250 mL of 0.20 M NaCl): 0.20 mol/L × 0.250 L × 58.44 g/mol = 2.92 g, dissolved and made up to 250 mL in a volumetric flask.
  3. Normality (500 mL of 0.1 N H₂SO₄): H₂SO₄ gives 2 H⁺, so 0.1 N = 0.05 M. 0.05 mol/L × 0.500 L × 98.08 g/mol = 2.45 g. Using 0.1 M by mistake doubles the acid.
  4. Equivalents of ions: mEq/L = mmol/L × charge. Sodium, potassium and chloride (charge 1) have the same number in both; calcium and magnesium (charge 2) have twice as many mEq as mmol.
  5. Molality: moles per kilogram of solvent. 0.100 mol NaCl in 500 g of water is 0.200 mol/kg. Osmolality (mOsm/kg) uses the same per-kilogram basis, counted in particles.
  6. Concentrated stock acid: the label gives purity (w/w) and specific gravity (density relative to water). 96% H₂SO₄, SG 1.84: each liter weighs 1840 g, of which 96% is acid = 1766 g; 1766 ÷ 98.08 = 18.0 mol/L, or 36.0 N.
  7. Diluting that stock (500 mL of 0.5 M, which is 1.0 N): V1 = 0.5 × 500 ÷ 18.0 = 13.9 mL of concentrated acid, added slowly to about 400 mL of water, cooled, then made up to 500 mL.
Each added water of crystallization adds 18.015 g to the molar mass: 141.96 + 2 × 18.015 = 177.99. The moles of salt must stay the same, so the mass rises with the water.

Dilutions: worked series and traps

A mixed serial dilution. If tube 3 reads 2,300 units, the patient value is 2,300 × 100 = 230,000. Adding the factors (5 + 2 + 10) instead of multiplying them is the usual wrong answer.
TubeMade fromTube dilutionTotal dilution
10.1 mL serum + 0.4 mL diluent1:51:5
20.5 mL of tube 1 + 0.5 mL diluent1:21:10
30.1 mL of tube 2 + 0.9 mL diluent1:101:100
  • Reading a diluted sample: dilute until the result falls inside the reportable (measuring) range, then multiply back. A result that is still above the range after dilution needs a further dilution, not extrapolation.
  • Several dilutions from one tube: a 1:4 dilution of a 1:10 dilution is 1:40.
  • Making a set volume of a stated dilution: 1:20 in 1 mL total is 0.05 mL sample + 0.95 mL diluent (the WHO manual's CSF and leukocyte dilution).
  • Titers: in a twofold series, each tube is half the one before. A fourfold rise (two tubes, for example 1:8 to 1:32) between acute and convalescent sera is the usual mark of a significant change.

Where the formulas come from

  • Beer-Lambert: decadic absorbance A = −log T, where T is transmitted ÷ incident light. With %T, A = −log(%T ÷ 100) = 2 − log %T. The molar absorption coefficient ε = A ÷ (c × l), so A = ε × l × c: at a fixed wavelength and path length, A is proportional to c. Transmittance is not linear with concentration; absorbance is.
  • One-standard calculation: because A ∝ c for both tubes, A unknown ÷ A standard = C unknown ÷ C standard. This holds only inside the linear range; above it, dilute the sample. A curve that bends at the top means the method is out of its linear range there.
  • Relative centrifugal force: RCF = r × ω² ÷ g, with ω = 2π × RPM ÷ 60 and g = 9.806 65 m/s². With r in cm converted to m: RCF = r(cm) ÷ 100 × (2π ÷ 60)² × RPM² ÷ 9.806 65 = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r × RPM². Measure r from the center of the rotor to the bottom of the tube.
  • Hemocytometer: each large Neubauer square is 1 mm × 1 mm and the chamber is 0.1 mm deep, so each square holds 0.1 mm³ = 0.1 µL. Cells per µL = cells counted ÷ volume counted × dilution. The WHO manual's leukocyte count: 188 cells in 4 squares (0.4 µL) at 1:20 = 188 ÷ 0.4 × 20 = 9,400/µL = 9.4 × 10⁹/L.
  • Temperature: °F = (9/5 × °C) + 32 and °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9. 56 °C (serum inactivation) = 132.8 °F.

Statistics in more depth

  • Mean, median and mode agree in a symmetric (Gaussian) distribution. In skewed data the mean is pulled toward the long tail and the median is the better summary.
  • SD uses n − 1 (degrees of freedom) because the mean was already estimated from the same data. In a Gaussian distribution about 68% of results fall within ±1 SD, 95% within ±2 SD and more than 99% within ±3 SD.
  • CV is SD relative to the mean, so it compares precision across levels and methods. CVs tend to be higher at low concentrations, so judge a CV with its mean.
  • Standard error (SD ÷ √n) shrinks as more runs are added; the SD does not. With n = 10 the 1.96 multiplier gives 85.3 to 90.1; a strict small-sample t value (2.26 for n = 10) widens it to 85.0 to 90.4.
  • A z-score is unitless and lets results from different levels share one chart. The Westgard example in the calculator: mean 100, SD 2, result 104.5 gives z = 2.25 (a 1-2s warning alone; with the previous run at +2.1 it is a 2-2s rejection).
Low prevalence lowers PPV, raises NPV and here slightly raises efficiency (because specificity is higher than sensitivity; efficiency moves toward specificity as prevalence falls), while sensitivity and specificity stay the same. That is why reactive screens in low-prevalence populations are confirmed by a more specific test.

  • Fastest route for prevalence problems: build a population of 10,000, fill in the diseased column from sensitivity and the non-diseased column from specificity, then read across the positive row.
  • Likelihood ratios: LR+ = sensitivity ÷ (1 − specificity); LR− = (1 − sensitivity) ÷ specificity. For the assay above, LR+ = 0.92 ÷ 0.05 = 18.4. They depend only on sensitivity and specificity, not on prevalence.

Counts, odds and timed urine in more depth

  • Absolute counts judge the differential: a lymphocyte share of 33% in a WBC of 8.0 is 2.64 × 10³/µL. Relative values in SI are fractions of 1 (0.33); absolute values have the same number in ×10³/µL and ×10⁹/L.
  • Reticulocytes: anemia raises the retic percentage because there are fewer mature red cells, so the corrected count multiplies by patient Hct ÷ 45. The RPI then divides by the maturation time for that hematocrit (2 days at an Hct of 25%; see the Reticulocyte Count calculator).
  • nRBC correction applies when the analyzer has counted nRBCs as white cells and has not corrected for them. The exam's calculation list names it for more than 10 nRBCs per 100 WBCs.
  • Rule of three as a check on indices: Hgb × 3 ≈ Hct (± 3) and RBC × 3 ≈ Hgb, for normocytic, normochromic cells only. In the indices example, 13.5 × 3 = 40.5, which matches the Hct.
  • Antigen-negative units: for independent antigens, multiply the fractions negative. One antigen on 9% of donors: 91% negative; 4 units ÷ 0.91 = 4.4, screen 5. The result is an average, so more may be needed; use the antigen frequencies of your own donor population.
  • Creatinine clearance with body size: U 120 mg/dL, 1500 mL in 24 h (1440 min), P 1.25 mg/dL. V = 1500 ÷ 1440 = 1.04 mL/min; 120 × 1.04 ÷ 1.25 = 100 mL/min; × 1.73 ÷ 1.73 m² = 100 mL/min/1.73 m². Urine and serum creatinine must be in the same unit so they cancel.
  • 24-hour collection: the patient empties the bladder on waking and discards that urine, collects everything after it, and ends with the first void the next morning, which is kept. A short or incomplete collection gives a falsely low clearance.
  • Timed urine totals: convert the volume to the concentration's unit before multiplying (1500 mL = 15 dL for a result in mg/dL). Creatinine 120 mg/dL × 15 dL = 1,800 mg in 24 h.
Safety A calculated result is only as good as its inputs. Before reporting a diluted, corrected or calculated value, check the units, the dilution actually made, and that the measured value was inside the reportable range.
