Try one

Clinical Chemistry: Analytes and Disease Patterns practice question

A reference laboratory validates a direct ultraviolet uricase method for serum uric acid. In the reaction, uricase converts uric acid, oxygen and water to allantoin, hydrogen peroxide and carbon dioxide, and no second enzyme or dye is added. Which measurement is proportional to the uric acid in the specimen?

  1. Rise in absorbance at 340 nm as NAD+ is reduced to NADH
  2. Fall in absorbance near 293 nm as uric acid is consumed
  3. Rise in absorbance near 500 nm as a quinoneimine dye forms
  4. Fall in absorbance at 340 nm as NADH is oxidized to NAD+

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. NADH formation is read in dehydrogenase methods such as the hexokinase glucose method; it is not part of the uricase reaction.
  • B. Correct. Uric acid absorbs strongly near 293 nm and allantoin does not, so the decrease in absorbance as uricase consumes it measures the uric acid present.
  • C. That is the coupled uricase method, in which the hydrogen peroxide drives a peroxidase (Trinder) dye reaction. The stem describes a direct method with no dye.
  • D. This is the readout of urease-glutamate dehydrogenase methods for urea, where the ammonia formed consumes NADH; uricase does not use NADH.

Uric acid, the end product of purine metabolism in humans, has a characteristic ultraviolet absorption peak near 293 nm. Uricase (urate oxidase) oxidizes it to allantoin, which lacks that peak, with hydrogen peroxide and carbon dioxide as by-products. In the direct method the analyzer reads the fall in absorbance at 293 nm, which is proportional to the uric acid consumed; a sample blank is needed because other serum components also absorb in the ultraviolet. Most automated analyzers instead couple the hydrogen peroxide to a peroxidase color reaction read in the visible range, which is more practical but is open to interference from reducing agents such as ascorbic acid.

Sources: Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.

Proteins and serum protein electrophoresis

  • Albumin is made in the liver and is about 60% of serum protein. Globulin = total protein − albumin; A/G ratio = albumin ÷ globulin, normally a little above 1.
  • Example: total protein 7.0 g/dL, albumin 4.0 g/dL: globulin 3.0 g/dL, A/G 1.3. A reversed ratio (globulin above albumin) points to low albumin, high globulin or both.
  • Dehydration raises total protein and albumin together; liver disease, malnutrition, malabsorption, nephrotic syndrome, protein-losing enteropathy and burns lower them.

Bilirubin and the jaundice table

  • Heme (mostly from old red cells) → biliverdin → unconjugated bilirubin, carried on albumin, insoluble in water, never in urine.
  • Liver conjugates it with glucuronic acid (UGT1A1) → conjugated bilirubin, water soluble, excreted in bile; it can appear in urine when the plasma level is high.
  • Gut bacteria reduce bilirubin to urobilinogens; most leave in feces (stool color), some are reabsorbed and re-excreted by the liver, and a small part (2–5%) reaches the urine.
  • Diazo method: direct bilirubin is what reacts without an accelerator (conjugated); total is measured with an accelerator (caffeine-benzoate); indirect = total − direct.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Hypoglycemia workup in detail

  • In treated diabetes, severity is graded by glucose: level 1 below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) but at least 54 (3.0); level 2 below 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L); level 3 needs another person's help.
  • Outside diabetes treatment, blood is drawn during symptoms into a tube with a glycolytic inhibitor. If glucose is normal, hypoglycemia is excluded. If low with no obvious cause, insulin, C-peptide, proinsulin, insulin antibodies and a sulfonylurea screen are run on the same specimen.
  • Supervised fast (up to 72 h; 48 h is usually enough): glucose at baseline, with symptoms and every 4–6 h (every 1–2 h once below 70 mg/dL). Insulin, C-peptide and proinsulin are drawn when glucose is below 55 mg/dL (3.05 mmol/L). The fast ends at 72 h or earlier at 45 mg/dL (2.5 mmol/L) or less with symptoms; end-of-fast tests are beta-hydroxybutyrate (low in insulinoma), a sulfonylurea level and the glucose response to IV glucagon.
  • Postprandial patterns (for example after bariatric surgery) are tested with a mixed meal: glucose, insulin and C-peptide every 30 minutes for up to 4 hours.
  • Home meters and continuous monitors are unreliable in the hypoglycemic range; fingersticks also read low with poor circulation to the digits.
  • Healthy women can have fasting glucose as low as 50 mg/dL (2.8 mmol/L) without symptoms.

C-peptide and proinsulin mark the patient's own insulin secretion: both are suppressed when the insulin is injected and not suppressed when the pancreas is being driven (secretagogue, insulinoma). For diabetes classification in insulin-treated patients (ADA), a C-peptide of at least 600 pmol/L (1.8 ng/mL) is valid whatever the circumstances of testing, and a very low result (under 80 pmol/L, 0.24 ng/mL) does not need to be repeated.

HbA1c in more depth

  • Glucose and HbA1c measure different things: glucose is an acute value with high within-person variation and poor preanalytical stability; HbA1c reflects about 2–3 months of glycemia, is well standardized and is unaffected by recent food, stress or exercise.
  • Methods must be NGSP certified and traceable to the DCCT reference; outside the US many are IFCC certified. IFCC results are in mmol/mol (6.5% = 48 mmol/mol).
  • When HbA1c and glucose disagree persistently, the laboratory should look for a variant or an interference before anyone trusts either number. Fructosamine and glycated albumin are the alternatives for monitoring.
  • Point-of-care HbA1c for screening and diagnosis is limited to FDA-approved devices used in CLIA-certified laboratories that perform moderate- or higher-complexity testing with trained personnel (ADA recommendation 2.2b).

Electrophoresis pitfalls

  • Immunofixation (or immunosubtraction on capillary systems) types a band as IgG, IgA or IgM and kappa or lambda; electrophoresis alone only shows that a band is there.
  • Light-chain myeloma may show little or no serum spike: the free light chains are small, are filtered and appear in urine as Bence Jones protein. Serum free light chains and urine electrophoresis find them.
  • Polyclonal IgG4 at high concentration can form a focal band that mimics a monoclonal one (Andre 2024).
  • Urine electrophoresis patterns: glomerular (albumin and other large proteins), tubular (small proteins such as beta-2 microglobulin, little albumin) and overflow (light chains, myoglobin, hemoglobin).
  • CSF electrophoresis: oligoclonal bands in CSF but not in serum support multiple sclerosis but are not specific.

Bilirubin methods and specimens

  • Four fractions exist: unconjugated, monoglucuronide, diglucuronide and delta bilirubin. Delta bilirubin is bilirubin covalently bound to albumin; it has a much longer half-life, so it is the fraction that lingers after cholestasis resolves and the direct bilirubin falls slowly.
  • Jendrassik-Grof (diazo with caffeine-benzoate accelerator) is the reference approach for total bilirubin. Other methods: bilirubin oxidase or vanadate oxidation, direct spectrophotometry (neonates) and transcutaneous meters (screening only).
  • Light degrades bilirubin (phototherapy works by photoisomerizing it), so protect specimens from light.
  • Urine bilirubin strip (diazo, detects about 0.5 mg/dL): test fresh urine. Light and room-temperature storage oxidize it to biliverdin (falsely negative); ascorbic acid and nitrite also cause false negatives (Merck). If delayed, keep dark at 2–8 °C for up to 24 h.
  • Urobilinogen pad (Ehrlich reagent): porphobilinogen gives the same red color, so a strongly positive urobilinogen with a near-normal serum bilirubin can be an acute porphyria (Song and Liu 2023).
  • Total bilirubin is mostly unconjugated normally, under 1.2 mg/dL (20 µmol/L) (Merck). Unconjugated rises without liver injury rarely exceed about 6 mg/dL (100 µmol/L).

Enzyme pitfalls worth a question

  • Macroamylasemia: amylase bound to immunoglobulin (IgA in most reported cases) is filtered slowly, so serum amylase stays high while urine amylase and lipase are normal. Amylase:creatinine clearance ratio = (urine amylase × serum creatinine) ÷ (serum amylase × urine creatinine) × 100; macroamylasemia gives a very low value (0.07% in Cho 2011).
  • Serum from patients with hypertriglyceridemia may contain an inhibitor that hides a raised amylase until the specimen is diluted (Merck).
  • Macro-CK and CK-BB can make the reported CK-MB exceed total CK. Macro-CK type 2 is found in 0.5–3.7% of people and, like a raised CK-BB, can go with malignancy; on CK electrophoresis it runs as an extra band beyond CK-MM toward the cathode (Wada 2017).
  • ALP is about 7 days in half-life, so it stays raised for days after an obstruction is relieved. Older adults with an isolated ALP rise and normal liver tests usually have a bone source.
  • GGT is induced by alcohol, some antiseizure drugs, warfarin and other drugs; a high GGT with normal ALP fits alcohol-associated disease.
  • AST is in red cells, heart and muscle; ALT is more liver-specific. Alcohol lowers ALT more than AST (pyridoxal phosphate deficiency), giving AST:ALT above 2.
  • In acute liver failure, aminotransferases can fall toward normal because few hepatocytes are left: a falling ALT with a rising bilirubin and INR is not recovery.
  • Very high enzyme activity can exhaust the reaction's substrate; see the Chemistry Methods page for kinetic assay flags and dilution.

Troponin and natriuretic peptides in more depth

  • hs-cTn assays detect troponin in many healthy people, so 'positive' means above the 99th percentile of a reference population, and many laboratories report sex-specific limits.
  • Kidney impairment: in the High-STEACS trial the share of patients with an elevated hs-cTnI rose from 10% at eGFR 90 or more to 66% at eGFR under 30, while the share with type 1 MI fell. The change over serial samples, not a single value, separates acute injury.
  • Skeletal muscle: in active chronic myopathy and myositis, hs-cTnT was above the upper limit in more than half of patients without heart disease (re-expression of the cardiac TnT gene in diseased muscle); cTnI was not. Troponin I is preferred in these patients.
  • Macrotroponin: troponin bound to immunoglobulin gives a persistent, unchanging and assay-dependent elevation. Clues are discordant results on another platform and a large fall after PEG precipitation.
  • BNP and NT-proBNP come from the same precursor. Neprilysin degrades mature BNP but not NT-proBNP, so sacubitril/valsartan can raise BNP without any worsening; commercial BNP assays also detect proBNP, which adds to the inconsistency (Nishikimi 2025).

Tumor marker pitfalls

  • CA 19-9 is sialyl Lewis a. Making it needs the Lewis fucosyltransferase (FUT3); people with the Le(a−b−) phenotype (about 10%) have undetectable CA 19-9 even with cancer, so a normal value means nothing in them.
  • Germ cell tumors use AFP, beta-hCG and LD together. AFP is a nonseminoma marker, so a clearly raised AFP in a patient labeled pure seminoma means a nonseminomatous element.
  • hCG in gestational trophoblastic disease is often far above what dates predict; falsely low results at extremely high hCG and false positives from heterophile antibodies are covered on the Chemistry Methods page.
  • Pre-evacuation hCG above 100,000 mIU/mL is one risk factor for persistent trophoblastic neoplasia after a mole (Merck).

Porphyria testing in more depth

  • Precursors (ALA, PBG) are linear and not fluorescent; porphyrins (uro-, copro-, protoporphyrin) are rings that fluoresce when excited. Precursors cause the neurovisceral attacks; porphyrins cause the skin disease.
  • Spot urine PBG and ALA have replaced 24-hour collections for acute attacks. Qualitative screens (Watson-Schwartz, Hoesch) use Ehrlich reagent; their sensitivity is about 10 mg/L, while the upper normal limit is about 2.5 mg/L, which is acceptable because attack values are 10 to 20 times normal. Watson-Schwartz can react with urobilinogen in liver disease; Hoesch does not.
  • Plasma fluorescence scanning (emission 580–700 nm) is positive in all symptomatic patients and the peak position points to the type: 618–622 nm acute porphyrias and PCT group, 626–628 nm variegate, 632–636 nm protoporphyrias.
  • Red cell zinc protoporphyrin rises in lead exposure, iron deficiency and sideroblastic anemia; in classic EPP the rise is metal-free protoporphyrin.
  • Plasma porphyrins in EPP and variegate porphyria are particularly light-labile: wrap and protect the tube.
