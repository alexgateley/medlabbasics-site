Chemistry Methods and Instrumentation

Beer's law and photometers, optical methods, immunoassay formats and their interferences, electrodes, osmometry, electrophoresis, chromatography and mass spectrometry, automation, point of care, and specimen interferences

Checked against current sources November 2026

Spectrophotometry and Beer's law

  • Beer's law: A = εbc. Absorbance (A) is proportional to concentration (c) when the path length (b, cm) and molar absorptivity (ε) are fixed.
  • Absorbance = log (I0 / I), where I0 is light reaching the detector through the blank and I through the sample. From percent transmittance: A = 2 − log %T.
  • Unknown concentration = (A unknown ÷ A standard) × concentration of the standard, valid only inside the method's linear range. Above it, dilute, re-read and multiply by the dilution factor (see the Beer's Law and Dilution Factor calculators).
  • Zero the photometer with a reagent blank so that color from the reagent itself is not counted as analyte.

Immunoassay formats and their interferences

FeatureCompetitive (reagent-limited)Sandwich, two-site (reagent excess)
DesignLabeled analyte competes with the patient's analyte for a limited amount of antibodyCapture antibody and labeled detection antibody bind two sites on the analyte
Signal vs concentrationInverse: more analyte, less signalDirect: more analyte, more signal

  • Heterogeneous assays separate bound from free label (a wash step) before reading; homogeneous assays read without separation because binding itself changes the signal.
  • Cross-reactivity: structurally similar compounds or metabolites are read as the analyte (cortisol assays and fludrocortisone, cyclosporine metabolites, digoxin-like factors). Drug screens detect a class by cross-reactivity, so positives are confirmed by GC-MS or LC-MS/MS.
  • Immunoassays are generally unaffected by hemolysis and icterus; lipemia matters most for nephelometric and turbidimetric methods.
Safety Biotin above 1 mg a day (hair and nail supplements up to 10 mg, much more for multiple sclerosis) can give a falsely LOW troponin on affected sandwich assays and a Graves-like thyroid pattern (low TSH, high free T4). An undetectable troponin in a patient with a proven infarction must be questioned before release.
Exam tip Clues to interference: results that do not fit the patient, disagreement between platforms, and a corrected result that changes with dilution. Rising corrected results on dilution = hook effect; falling corrected results on dilution or a large drop after blocking reagent = heterophile antibody.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Beer's law worked examples

  1. Within range. A 200 mg/dL standard reads A = 0.400 and a patient reads A = 0.300 against the same reagent blank. Concentration = 0.300 ÷ 0.400 × 200 = 150 mg/dL.
  2. Above range. The method is linear to A = 0.800 and a patient reads 1.250. The sample is diluted 1 part plus 4 parts saline (factor 5) and reads 0.250. 0.250 ÷ 0.400 × 200 = 125 mg/dL; × 5 = 625 mg/dL. The first reading (1.250) would have given 625 only if the curve stayed straight, which it does not.
  3. Transmittance. A = 2 − log %T, so 100%T gives A = 0, 10%T gives A = 1.0 and 1%T gives A = 2.0. Half the light through (50%T) is A = 0.301. Absorbance, not %T, is proportional to concentration.
  4. Molar absorptivity. If ε is known, c = A ÷ (ε × b). A compound with ε = 6,220 L/mol/cm (the usual NADH value at 340 nm) giving A = 0.311 in a 1 cm cuvette is at 0.311 ÷ 6,220 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L (50 µmol/L).

Why curves bend at the top: at high concentration molecules interact and scatter light, and stray light (light outside the selected band) is a larger share of the little light that still reaches the detector, so absorbance rises less than concentration. Lipemia, turbidity and bubbles add apparent absorbance that is not analyte. Wavelength error moves the reading off the absorbance peak, lowering sensitivity and linearity.

Exam tip Endpoint vs kinetic: an endpoint method reads once after the reaction is complete; a kinetic (rate) method reads the change in absorbance per minute, which is how enzyme activities and many NADH-linked assays are measured. A falling absorbance at 340 nm means NADH is being used up.

Immunoassay interference: recognizing and resolving it

ClueLikely causeNext step
Result far higher than the clinical picture; different on another platformHeterophile antibody or human anti-animal antibody (HAMA)Serial dilution (nonlinear recovery), heterophile blocking reagent, PEG precipitation, another platform with different antibodies
Result low or only mildly raised while the patient has a large tumor or very high level expectedHigh-dose hook effectRepeat at a dilution (for example 1:10 to 1:100); a rising corrected value confirms it
Sandwich results low and competitive results high in the same patientBiotinAsk about supplements; retest after the manufacturer's washout, on a biotin-free platform, or after streptavidin bead depletion
High prolactin without symptomsMacroprolactin (prolactin bound to IgG)PEG precipitation or gel filtration to estimate monomeric prolactin
Drug level higher than the parent drug can explainCross-reacting metabolites (cyclosporine immunoassays up to 174% above HPLC)Chromatographic or LC-MS/MS method
Digoxin result after anti-digoxin Fab treatmentTherapeutic antibody fragments bind digoxin in the sampleReport that the result is affected: the fragments compete with the assay antibody

Hook effect examples from the literature: a prolactin of 1,500 mU/L rose to 950,000 mU/L when the sample was diluted, and a urine myoglobin reading 100 nmol/L undiluted read 1,496 nmol/L at 1:50. Thyroglobulin is recommended to be run neat and at 1:10. The hook occurs in two-site assays where capture and detection antibodies are added together: excess free analyte saturates both antibodies separately, so fewer sandwiches form.

Heterophile antibodies are polyreactive natural antibodies or autoantibodies, including rheumatoid factor; HAMA follow exposure to mouse antibodies. In a two-site assay they link the animal capture and detection antibodies with no analyte present. Manufacturers add nonimmune animal immunoglobulin as a blocker, but not every interference is blocked. The laboratory reports suspected interference to the provider, comments the result, and keeps the patient's history on file.

Biotin: most multivitamins contain about 30 µg, which does not interfere. Supplements for hair and nails contain up to 10 mg, and multiple sclerosis studies have used far higher doses. Many laboratories ask patients to stop biotin for at least 48 hours before collection; the assay's package insert gives the interval and the biotin level that interferes, and some troponin assays listed by the FDA have not addressed the risk.

Electrodes in more depth

  • Nernst equation: E = E0 + (RT ÷ zF) ln a. For a monovalent ion at 25 °C this is about 59 mV per tenfold change in activity; for a divalent ion (Ca²⁺) about half that. ISEs measure activity, which is why ionized calcium is a direct ISE measurement.
  • Selectivity: an ISE membrane (glass, crystal or polymer with an ionophore) favors one ion but is not perfectly selective; other halides such as bromide are read as chloride by chloride electrodes and colorimetric methods.
  • pH electrode: glass membrane sensitive to H⁺, read against a reference electrode of constant potential.
  • pCO2: the Severinghaus electrode is a pH electrode behind a CO2-permeable membrane with a bicarbonate solution; the measured pH change is converted to pCO2. Bicarbonate on a blood gas report is calculated from pH and pCO2.
  • pO2: the Clark electrode reduces O2 at a platinum cathode (about −0.8 V); the current is proportional to pO2. Very high leukocyte counts keep consuming oxygen in a delayed syringe (see the Electrolytes, Acid-Base and Blood Gases page).
  • Coulometric chloride: Ag → Ag⁺ + e⁻ at the silver anode; Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl. The end point is the sudden rise in free Ag⁺. ICP-MS and ion chromatography are also acceptable for sweat chloride; sweat conductivity and sweat sodium or osmolality are not diagnostic.
  • Lithium: flame emission and atomic absorption were the original methods; lithium ISEs are precise but other ions can interfere.

Indirect ISE assumes a normal plasma water fraction. When lipids or proteins take up more of the plasma volume, the same aliquot holds less water and fewer electrolytes, and the dilution multiplies the error. Direct ISE senses the activity in plasma water and is unaffected. Very high total protein (myeloma, macroglobulinemia) causes most of the sodium bias, and high triglycerides add to it; potassium errors are much smaller. The laboratory confirms a doubtful sodium on a direct ISE analyzer or by measured osmolality.

Osmolality and the osmolal gap

  • Calculated osmolality = 2 × Na + glucose ÷ 18 + BUN ÷ 2.8 (glucose and BUN in mg/dL). Osmolal gap = measured − calculated; about 10 mOsm/kg or more suggests unmeasured osmoles (ethanol, methanol, ethylene glycol, mannitol, acetone). See the Osmolality and Osmolar Gap calculators.
  • Freezing point depression: one osmole of particles per kg of water lowers the freezing point by about 1.86 °C. The instrument supercools the sample, induces freezing and reads the plateau temperature with a thermistor.
  • Vapor pressure (dew point) osmometers may not detect volatile solutes such as ethanol and methanol: a patient with a high methanol level had no osmolal gap by vapor pressure osmometry, so the gap is sought only with a freezing point osmometer.
  • Serum and heparin plasma both work; specimens are stable at room temperature for up to 36 hours before separation, with cooling advised for longer delays.
  • One large study found the gap by freezing point rose with glucose above 110 mg/dL, most strongly above 450 mg/dL, while the vapor pressure gap did not: interpret gaps cautiously in severe hyperglycemia.

Serum indices and lipemia management

  • Analyzers dilute the sample and read absorbance at several wavelengths to report hemolysis (H), icterus (I) and lipemia (L) indices. Bilirubin and hemoglobin absorb in the lower range; only turbidity absorbs near 700 nm, so lipemia is read there.
  • Visual grading agrees poorly between staff and with the L-index; indices are better and can drive autoverification rules.
  • A high L-index with little triglyceride can come from a paraprotein; a very high triglyceride with clear serum and a normal L-index points to free glycerol (glycerol kinase deficiency, glycerol in drugs or nutrition) measured by a non-blanked enzymatic triglyceride method.
  • Lipid layers: chylomicrons cause most turbidity. Ultracentrifuges (100,000 x g and above) remove lipid best; high-speed centrifugation at about 10,000 x g is almost as good. VLDL-rich lipemia may need repeated spins. Extraction reagents (polyethylene glycol, cyclodextrin, commercial clearing agents) are alternatives with their own limits.
  • Interference thresholds in package inserts are a starting point; laboratories can set their own after testing. A small group of severely icteric patients accounts for about half of icteric index failures.

Point-of-care quality

  • CLIA waived tests are simple methods cleared for home use, or so simple and accurate that errors are negligible, or with no reasonable risk of harm if done wrong. The list includes non-automated dipstick urinalysis, fecal occult blood, visual urine pregnancy tests, home-use glucose meters, spun microhematocrit and single-analyte hemoglobin instruments.
  • Cartridge analyzers: run the electronic QC check each day of use before patients; if there is none, run cartridge controls daily. Check each delivery of cartridges or strips and run a QA sample at least monthly to check storage and operators.
  • Glucose meters read whole blood, which has less water than plasma: plasma glucose is about 11 to 12% higher at a hematocrit of 45%. Strip enzymes are damaged by heat, cold and humidity; strips and meters must be stored as directed.
  • Glucose oxidase meters: high pO2 gives low results and hypoxia high results; acetaminophen and ascorbic acid lower results on peroxide-based strips. Some glucose dehydrogenase strips (GDH-PQQ) read maltose, formed from icodextrin dialysate, as glucose.
  • Unfixed samples keep consuming glucose at about 5 to 7% per hour; fluoride takes 1 to 2 hours or more to stop glycolysis, so plasma is separated promptly for comparisons with meters.
Exam tip Waived does not mean unregulated: the site must hold a certificate of waiver and follow the manufacturer's instructions exactly.
