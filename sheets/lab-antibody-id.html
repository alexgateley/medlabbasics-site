Antibody Screen and Identification

Screen and panel workflow, phases, rule-outs and dosage, the 3+3 rule, autocontrol and DAT, enzymes, DTT, neutralization, adsorption, elution, the blood group systems and a worked panel

Checked against current sources November 2026

Practice 120 Antibody Screen and Identification questions free

Workflow

  1. Antibody screen: the patient's plasma against 2 or 3 group O screening cells by indirect antiglobulin test (IAT). The cells together carry the antigens of the common clinically significant antibodies.
  2. Screen positive: test a panel (often 10 to 12 cells plus an autocontrol) with the same method, reading every phase the method uses.
  3. Rule out: on each nonreactive cell, cross out the antigens it carries (homozygous cells where dosage applies).
  4. Match the pattern: the antigens left should fit the reactive cells. Add selected cells to exclude what remains.
  5. Confirm: at least 3 antigen-positive cells react and 3 antigen-negative cells do not (p = 0.05), and the patient types negative for the antigen.
  6. Give antigen-negative units that are compatible by AHG crossmatch, and record the antibody so it is honored on every later sample.
Exam tip A patient makes an antibody only to an antigen the patient lacks. A patient with anti-Jka must type Jk(a-). If the patient types positive, think autoantibody, partial antigen, recently transfused cells in the sample, or a wrong answer.

Try one

Antibody Screen and Identification practice question

A 69-year-old patient with multiple myeloma treated with daratumumab (anti-CD38) needs 2 units of red cells. She was last transfused 3 weeks ago, and no baseline phenotype or genotype is on file. Results: ABO/D: group B, D-positive, no discrepancy Antibody screen, gel IAT: cells I and III 1+, cell II 3+ Autocontrol 0 Panel with DTT-treated cells: 2+ with each of the 4 E+ cells, 0 with each of the 7 E− cells; every other common specificity except K is excluded Which red cells are MOST appropriate to issue?

  1. Group B, D-positive, E-negative units
  2. Group B, D-positive, K-negative units
  3. Group B, D-positive, E-negative units that are compatible by IAT crossmatch with untreated donor cells
  4. Group B, D-positive, E-negative, K-negative units

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because anti-E is the only antibody found, but DTT-treated cells lack intact Kell antigens, so an anti-K could be present and missed on that panel.
  • B. This covers the Kell gap left by DTT, but it ignores the anti-E that the DTT panel revealed; anti-E is a clinically significant IgG antibody, so E-negative units are needed too.
  • C. Daratumumab binds CD38 on every donor cell, so crossmatches with untreated cells are weakly incompatible; DTT-treated donor cells or an electronic or immediate-spin crossmatch per local procedure are used instead.
  • D. Correct. DTT removed the anti-CD38 interference and revealed anti-E, but DTT also denatures Kell antigens, so anti-K cannot be excluded; units negative for both E and K are given while her K type is unknown.

Daratumumab is an IgG monoclonal antibody against CD38, which is weakly expressed on red cells. In plasma it causes weak panreactivity in all antiglobulin methods, while ABO and D typing are unaffected. A screening cell that reacts more strongly than the others is a clue that a real alloantibody sits under the interference. Treating reagent cells with dithiothreitol denatures CD38 and removes the interference, but it also destroys Kell antigens and some lower-prevalence antigens (Lutheran, Yt, Dombrock, Knops and others). So the DTT panel can identify anti-E but cannot exclude anti-K, and patients not known to be K-positive receive K-negative units. Recent transfusion makes a serologic phenotype unreliable, which is why a baseline phenotype or genotype before the first dose is recommended.

Sources: ASCP BOC MLS Content Guideline (2026); FDA Blood Guidances

Rule-out and dosage

  • Cross out only on nonreactive cells. Reactive cells are for the pattern, not for exclusions.
  • Dosage: a homozygous cell (for example Jk(a+b-)) carries more antigen and reacts more strongly than a heterozygous Jk(a+b+) cell. A weak antibody may miss a heterozygous cell, so exclude on a homozygous cell.
  • Dosage is shown by Rh (C, c, E, e), Kidd, Duffy and MNS antibodies.
  • No dosage is listed for K, Lewis or P1 (Bio-Rad). Anti-K is excluded on one K+k- cell or two K+k+ cells (K+k- cells are scarce). Antigrams give no zygosity for Lewis and P1, so any antigen-positive cell is used, but P1 strength varies widely between cells (partly with zygosity), so a weak P1+ cell can miss a weak anti-P1.
  • Published protocols (Manduzio 2024) exclude on two homozygous nonreactive cells; some labs accept one. Follow the local procedure.

Confirming: the 3+3 rule

p is the one-sided Fisher exact probability that the pattern fits the antigen by chance when every cell agrees: 1 divided by the number of ways to choose the positive cells from all cells tested. Some authors (Harris and Hochman; Kanter, Poole and Garratty) argue p values should not be applied mechanically; the rule of three is the working standard.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Screen and panel cells

  • Screening cells are group O so that anti-A and anti-B do not react. Two or three cells together carry the antigens of the common clinically significant antibodies (Rh, Kell, Duffy, Kidd, MNS).
  • A panel is a set of group O cells with a printed antigram for one lot. Read the antigram for that lot only; cells and antigrams are not interchangeable between lots.
  • Tube IAT needs IgG-coated check cells added to every negative AHG tube: if they do not agglutinate, the wash failed or the AHG was neutralized and the test is invalid. Gel has no wash step and needs no check cells.
  • Gel and solid phase can be automated; tube is the most flexible (temperature, PEG, enzymes, DTT, sequential steps).
  • Enhancement media: LISS (low ionic strength saline) is built into gel and solid phase; PEG is a tube enhancer. Both promote IgG binding to the cells.
Exam tip A screen does not detect an antibody to a low-prevalence antigen missing from the screening cells. The AHG crossmatch with the donor unit is the backstop.

  • Antibodies to high-prevalence antigens range from benign (often anti-Yta, Ch/Rg, Knops, JMH: weak, variable, IAT-only reactions) to hemolytic (anti-U, anti-Vel, anti-Jsb). Reference labs use cellular assays such as the monocyte monolayer assay to judge whether incompatible blood can be given when no antigen-negative unit exists.
  • Ch/Rg antibodies are neutralized by pooled plasma, which proves the specificity; the antigens are carried on complement C4, so anti-Ch reacts strongly with C4-coated cells. They are not hemolytic, and enzymes weaken them.
  • An alloantibody to a high-prevalence antigen cannot be removed by allogeneic adsorption: every adsorbing cell carries the antigen and takes the antibody out.

  • Enzymes cleave sialoglycoproteins, which carry most of the red cell's negative surface charge, so cells come closer and antibodies to the remaining antigens react more strongly.
  • An enzyme panel separates mixtures: if anti-Fya plus a second antibody is suspected, the enzyme panel loses the anti-Fya and leaves the Rh or Kidd antibody clear.
  • DTT breaks disulfide bonds. On cells it removes Kell, Dombrock and Yt antigens and CD38. In plasma it breaks IgM pentamers: reactivity lost after DTT was IgM, reactivity kept is IgG (with a saline dilution control).
Safety Anti-CD38 drugs make every IAT react (screen, panel, AHG crossmatch) for up to 6 months after the last dose. DTT-treated cells remove the interference but also destroy Kell, so give K-negative units unless the patient is K-positive, and remember anti-k, anti-Yta and anti-Doa/Dob will be missed. A baseline type and screen (and phenotype or genotype) before the first dose helps most. Other fixes now sold: soluble CD38, anti-daratumumab idiotype reagents, Fab fragments that mask CD38, trypsin-treated cells and CD38-negative cord cells.

Neutralization (inhibition)

  • Principle: mix the plasma with a soluble form of the antigen. If the antibody binds the soluble antigen, it can no longer agglutinate antigen-positive cells. Loss of reactivity is the positive result.
  • Always run a dilution control: plasma plus the same volume of saline. The control must still react, or the loss may be only dilution of a weak antibody and the test is invalid.
  • Other antibodies in the sample are not neutralized, so neutralizing a Lewis or P1 antibody can reveal a clinically significant antibody under it.
  • Substances: secretor saliva (anti-A, anti-B, anti-H, anti-Lea, anti-Leb); hydatid cyst fluid or pigeon egg albumin (anti-P1); human or guinea pig urine (anti-Sda); pooled plasma from six or more donors (anti-Ch, anti-Rg); serum, plasma or concentrated urine (most Cromer antibodies). Recombinant blood group proteins now exist for several high-prevalence antigens.

Exam tip Typing a patient with a positive DAT: IgG-coated cells react falsely with AHG-phase antisera. Use direct-agglutinating (IgM monoclonal) antisera, remove the IgG first, or genotype.

Prewarm technique

  • Steps: warm the cells and plasma separately to 37 °C, mix, incubate, do not centrifuge at room temperature, wash with 37 °C saline, then add anti-IgG.
  • Purpose: keep cold autoantibodies from binding at room temperature, so potentially significant warm antibodies can be seen; also used to ask whether a cold antibody reacts at 37 °C.
  • Limits (Leger and Garratty, 94 antibodies): prewarm PBS-IAT and LISS-IAT weakened 40% and 47% of antibodies and lost 14% completely; prewarm PEG-IAT weakened 56% and lost all 7 PEG-dependent antibodies. Enhancement media do not make prewarm safe.
  • Other ways around a cold autoantibody: anti-IgG instead of polyspecific AHG, cold autoadsorption.

Reporting and units

  • Report the specificity, whether it is clinically significant (HTR, HDFN), any antibodies that could not be excluded, and how hard compatible blood will be to find.
  • Antigen-negative units: units needed divided by the frequency of the antigen-negative phenotype. For anti-Jka in a White donor pool, 23% are Jk(a-): 2 units means screening about 9 (see the Antigen-Negative Units calculator).
  • Once a clinically significant antibody is identified, give antigen-negative, AHG-compatible blood on every later transfusion, even if the antibody is no longer detected.
  • A new sample is needed for each pretransfusion workup within the specimen age limit (see the Compatibility Testing and Blood Components page). Prenatal titers and HDFN are on the Transfusion Reactions and HDFN page; ABO and Rh discrepancies are on the ABO, H and Rh Typing page.
