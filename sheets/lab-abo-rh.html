ABO, H and Rh Typing

ABO and H antigens, forward and reverse grouping, ABO discrepancies and how to resolve them, D, weak D, partial D, Rh nomenclature and Rh null

Checked against current sources November 2026

ABO and H antigens

  • ABH antigens are sugars added in steps to a precursor chain. The H gene (FUT1) adds fucose to make H on red cells; FUT2 (the secretor gene) makes H in saliva and other secretions.
  • The A transferase adds N-acetylgalactosamine to H; the B transferase adds galactose. A and B transferases differ at four amino acids (176, 235, 266, 268); 266 and 268 set the sugar.
  • The common O allele has a single-base deletion (261delG) that shifts the reading frame: no working enzyme, so H stays unconverted. Group O cells carry the most H; A1 and A1B cells the least.
  • A2 differs from A1 in quantity and quality of A antigen: fewer sites and missing A determinants, which is why some A2 and A2B people make anti-A1.
  • Anti-A and anti-B are 'naturally occurring', mostly IgM, made in response to gut flora and food antigens. They appear between 3 and 6 months of age.

Forward and reverse grouping

Exam tip The two halves check each other: after infancy a person has the antibody to whichever A or B antigen they lack. A missing, weak, mixed-field or extra reaction is a discrepancy, and the ABO group is not reported until it is resolved.

ABO discrepancies: cause, clue and fix

First rule out clerical and technical error: check the labels and the history, repeat the test on the same sample (a new sample if mix-up is possible). Only then look for a problem with the cells or the plasma.

Rh: D, weak D, partial D

  • Two neighboring genes on chromosome 1: RHD (D antigen) and RHCE (C or c with E or e). Both need RhAG (RHAG gene, chromosome 6) to reach the membrane.
  • D-negative usually means the whole RHD gene is deleted. About 17% of US donors are D-negative.
  • D is the most immunogenic antigen after A and B: more than 80% of D-negative people given D-positive red cells are expected to make anti-D.
  • Weak D: fewer D sites because amino acid changes in or under the membrane hinder the protein's insertion. Reacts 2+ or weaker (or not at all) with anti-D at immediate spin but moderately or strongly at the antiglobulin phase. No anti-D has been reported in weak D types 1, 2 and 3.
  • Partial D: changes on the outside of the cell remove some D epitopes (for example DVI). The person can make anti-D against the missing parts.
  • DEL: D so weak it is found only by adsorption-elution; types as D-negative; common in East Asian populations.

Exam tip Exams test the logic of weak D: a donor is called Rh positive so a weak D unit never goes to a D-negative patient; a patient is called D-negative so a partial D patient never gets D-positive blood. The safe call differs because the risk differs.

Rh null

  • No Rh antigens at all (D, C, c, E, e and the rest); LW is also missing.
  • Regulator type (more common): both RHAG alleles mutated, so no Rh complex reaches the membrane, even though RHD and RHCE are normal and can be passed on.
  • Amorph type (considerably rarer): normal RHAG, the common RHD deletion and a mutation in each RHCE allele.
  • Rh deficiency syndrome: chronic mild to moderate hemolytic anemia with stomatocytes and spherocytes.
  • After transfusion or pregnancy the patient can make anti-Rh29, which reacts with every cell except Rh null. Blood comes from family members, frozen rare units and rare donor registries.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Infants and the under-4-month rule

  • At birth A and B antigens are about 25-50% of adult strength and reach full strength by 2 to 4 years.
  • The infant's own anti-A and anti-B appear between 3 and 6 months and reach adult titers by 5 to 10 years. Maternal IgG crosses the placenta, can be detected in the infant and can suppress the infant's own antibody production.
  • So under 4 months of age only the forward ABO group is done; a reverse group would show maternal antibody, not the infant's. As described from the AABB Standards, repeat ABO typing is unnecessary for infants under 4 months.
  • If non-group-O red cells are to be given to an infant under 4 months, the infant's plasma is tested for maternal anti-A or anti-B with an antiglobulin method.

Working a discrepancy at the bench

  1. Check identity: armband, sample label, requisition, previous type on file.
  2. Repeat the test on the same sample, by tube if the first run was gel or automated; wash the cells if plasma proteins or cold antibodies are suspected.
  3. Decide which half is wrong: compare the reaction strengths with what the group needs, and look at the autocontrol and the O cells.
  4. Missing antibody: extend the room-temperature incubation or incubate at 4 C; always include group O cells and an autocontrol so a cold antibody is not mistaken for anti-A or anti-B.
  5. Missing antigen: anti-A,B, anti-A1 lectin, anti-H lectin, saliva inhibition in a secretor, adsorption-elution of anti-A or anti-B; molecular testing and a family study confirm the subgroup.
  6. Extra reaction in the reverse group: a reacting O cell means an alloantibody or cold autoantibody, so run an antibody screen and panel; a reacting A1 cell in an A person means anti-A1 (test with the anti-A1 lectin).
  7. Rouleaux: saline replacement. Spin, remove the plasma, add an equal volume of saline, mix and reread. True agglutination stays; rouleaux disperses.
  8. Cold autoantibody: wash the patient's cells in warm (37 C) saline for the forward group; keep the plasma, tubes, pipettes and reagent cells at 37 C for the reverse group; autoadsorb the plasma with the patient's own cells for crossmatching.

Anti-H lectinUlex europaeus seedsEvery group except Bombay; strongest with O, weakest with A1 and A1BIdentify Bombay; test secretor status in group O saliva
  • Secretors (working FUT2) put soluble A, B and H substances in saliva. Saliva inhibition (the saliva neutralizes the matching antiserum) shows a weak A or B that the cells hardly express.
  • Bombay people have no FUT1 and no FUT2 function: no H on cells or in saliva, so even a person who inherited A or B genes cannot make A or B. Their anti-H is strong.
  • Para-Bombay people have a weak or absent FUT1 but may be secretors (H, and sometimes A or B, in saliva); their anti-H may be weaker. In one 2026 case an Ah para-Bombay secretor with a 4 C-only anti-H received crossmatch-compatible group O cells safely.
  • Bombay is about 1 in 10,000 in parts of India and about 1 in a million in Europe; Indian cases often carry FUT1 c.725T>G with FUT2 deleted.

More on weak D, partial D and genotyping

  • The weak D test is an indirect antiglobulin test on the patient's cells: anti-D is incubated with the cells, they are washed and AHG is added. Cells already coated with IgG (a positive DAT) give a positive result whatever their D type, so a weak D result on DAT-positive cells cannot be read.
  • About 0.2-1% of people of European ancestry have a serologic weak D; types 1, 2 and 3 are most of them (about 80% of US weak D phenotypes). No anti-D alloimmunization has been reported in types 1, 2 or 3.
  • The AABB/CAP Work Group (2015) recommends RHD genotyping for any patient with a weak or discordant D type: types 1, 2 and 3 are managed as D-positive for transfusion and RhIG; all other types as D-negative. Some recent guidance (a 2021 CMAJ commentary) also includes 4.0 and 4.1; the Work Group left them out because anti-D (mostly autoanti-D) has been reported in type 4.0 and data on 4.1 were too few.
  • Partial D is common in Europe as DNB and DVI and in the US among people of African ancestry. DVI has caused fatal HDFN, which is why US licensed anti-D reagents must not type DVI as D-positive at immediate spin.
  • DEL red cells are missed by routine donor tests and labeled Rh negative, and DEL units have immunized D-negative recipients.
  • A large fetomaternal hemorrhage (over about 30 mL of fetal blood) can make a D-negative mother look weak D positive with mixed field after delivery. Quantify fetal cells before calling her weak D (see the Transfusion Reactions and HDFN page and the Fetal-Maternal Hemorrhage calculator).

Rh genetics and phenotypes

  • RhD and RhCE are 417-amino-acid proteins that cross the membrane 12 times and differ at 36 positions. E or e depends on position 226 (proline = E, alanine = e).
  • The RHD gene sits between two Rhesus boxes; unequal crossing over between them deletes RHD, the main cause of D-negative worldwide. Up to 30% of apparently D-negative East Asians carry DEL.
  • In Europeans the common haplotypes rank Ce > ce > cE > CE. Rh antigens are inherited as haplotypes (one from each parent), so a phenotype gives only a 'most probable genotype', chosen from the haplotype frequencies of the person's population. In a 2025 series of 1,000 Indian donors R1R1 (44%) and R1r (32%) were the most common, rr 5%.
  • Among the CcEe antigens, c and e are the more immunogenic; C, E and G mostly immunize together with D.
  • LW is on chromosome 19, separate from Rh, but its glycoprotein sits in the Rh complex and is absent from Rh null cells. DTT destroys LW but not D; papain and ficin do not affect LW.
