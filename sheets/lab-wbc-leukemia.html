Myeloid neoplasms (WHO 5th edition)

  • AML generally needs 20% or more blasts in blood or marrow. Blasts and blast equivalents are myeloblasts, monoblasts and promonocytes.
  • AML with a defining genetic abnormality needs NO set blast count, except AML with BCR::ABL1 and AML with CEBPA mutation, which still need 20%.
  • AML, myelodysplasia-related (AML-MR) needs 20% blasts plus MDS-type cytogenetics or mutations, or a history of MDS or MDS/MPN; dysplasia on the smear alone no longer defines it.
  • The 20% line between MDS and AML is kept. MDS has under 20% blasts.
  • Older names: "myelodysplastic syndromes" are now myelodysplastic neoplasms (still MDS); the accelerated phase of CML is dropped.

  • PNH: an acquired PIGA mutation leaves cells without GPI-anchored proteins, including CD55 and CD59 (complement regulators).
  • Flow cytometry is the reference test. Red cells: CD59 (type I normal, type II partial loss, type III complete loss). White cells: FLAER plus a GPI-anchored marker on neutrophils (CD24 or CD157) and monocytes (CD14 or CD157).
  • Report the clone size from granulocytes and monocytes: hemolysis and transfusion make the red cell clone look smaller. FLAER is not used on red cells.
  • Ham (acidified serum) and sucrose hemolysis tests are obsolete.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Counting and reporting the differential

  • Absolute count = total WBC × percentage. Example: WBC 12.0 × 10³/µL with 80% neutrophils gives 9.6 × 10³/µL, above the ASCP 1.7–7.5 range: an absolute neutrophilia. A relative change with a normal absolute count is often not a true increase.
  • nRBCs are counted per 100 WBCs. The ASCP outline expects a corrected WBC when more than 10 nRBCs per 100 WBCs are seen. When the analyzer has not removed them, corrected WBC = uncorrected WBC × 100 ÷ (100 + nRBCs per 100 WBCs) (see the WBC Corrected for nRBCs calculator).
  • Smear estimate: about 2 to 5 white cells per high-power field is normal. A field factor (WBC ÷ average cells per field over 10 fields) checks the analyzer count.
  • Broken (smudge) cells are included in the count; leaving them out gives a falsely low count of the cell type that breaks, usually CLL lymphocytes.
  • Pelger-Huët cells look like bands; mistaking them for bands gives a false left shift.

AML in more depth

  • Presentation: anemia, thrombocytopenia and granulocytopenia; blasts may reach 90% of the white count in blood and are typically 25–95% of marrow cells.
  • Auer rods (pink-red linear inclusions from crystallized MPO-rich granules) mean a myeloid blast; Merck calls them pathognomonic for AML. They also count toward MDS-IB2.
  • Before and during treatment, watch for tumor lysis: high uric acid, phosphate and potassium, low calcium. Very high blast counts can lower glucose in a tube left to sit (pseudohypoglycemia).
  • Complete remission: under 5% marrow blasts with ANC above 1000/µL and platelets above 100,000/µL.
  • A second classification, the International Consensus Classification (ICC 2022), differs: it uses 10% blasts for most AML with recurrent genetic abnormalities (including APL, which WHO defines at any blast count), a 10–19% "MDS/AML" group where WHO has MDS-IB2, and it keeps an accelerated phase of CML, which WHO dropped. This page follows WHO 5th edition; if a question gives ICC numbers, read it on ICC terms.

Safety APL coagulopathy: low fibrinogen, raised D-dimer and often a prolonged PT and aPTT, driven by tissue factor and annexin II on the leukemic cells (hyperfibrinolysis). Antithrombin and protein C are often normal, unlike sepsis-type DIC. A WBC above 10 × 10⁹/L at diagnosis marks high bleeding risk. Typical support while APL is suspected: cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen if fibrinogen is under 100–150 mg/dL, platelets if under 30–50 × 10⁹/L.
  • Nonspecific esterase is weak or negative in 10–20% of acute monocytic leukemias; flow markers (CD14, CD64, CD11c, CD4) settle the lineage.
  • Hypogranular neutrophils are easier to see with SBB or MPO staining, which shows the missing granules.
  • Pseudo-Chédiak-Higashi granules can appear in a few myeloid cells in acute leukemia, MPN and MDS; true Chédiak-Higashi affects all white cells.

  • CML: WBC usually under 50 × 10⁹/L when found by chance, 200–1000 × 10⁹/L when symptomatic; hemoglobin usually above 10 g/dL. The Philadelphia chromosome is seen in 90–95%; FISH or RT-PCR finds BCR::ABL1 in the rest.
  • CML marrow: myeloid to erythroid ratio 10:1 or more, small hypolobated megakaryocytes, pseudo-Gaucher cells.
  • CML monitoring: quantitative RT-PCR for BCR::ABL1 at 3, 6 and 12 months, then every 3–6 months. A major molecular response is BCR::ABL1 at 1/1000 of the untreated baseline or lower.
  • CML blast phase (WHO 5th edition): 20% or more myeloid blasts in blood or marrow, an extramedullary blast mass, or increased lymphoblasts. Blast-phase cells are myeloid in about 60%, lymphoid in about 30%.
  • Polycythemia vera: Merck gives hemoglobin above 16.5 g/dL in men or 16.0 g/dL in women as abnormal. WHO criteria pair high hemoglobin or hematocrit with a panmyelosis in the marrow and JAK2; red cell mass measurement was dropped. Iron deficiency can hide the high hemoglobin (microcytic erythrocytosis).
  • Essential thrombocythemia: JAK2 V617F allele burden stays at or below 50%; a higher burden suggests PV or PMF. Platelets near 1,000 × 10⁹/L can cause bleeding through acquired von Willebrand syndrome (loss of large multimers).
  • Primary myelofibrosis: fibrosis is made by normal fibroblasts reacting to the clone; reticulin or trichrome stain shows it. Extramedullary hematopoiesis enlarges the spleen. Myeloblasts can appear in blood in advanced disease without acute leukemia.
  • CMML: if monocytes are 0.5 to under 1.0 × 10⁹/L, a clonal abnormality and dysplasia are both required. CMML-1: blasts and promonocytes under 5% blood and under 10% marrow; CMML-2: 5–19% blood or 10–19% marrow. WBC 13 × 10⁹/L or more is the myeloproliferative subtype.

  • CLL markers regarded as essential: CD5, CD19, CD20, CD23 and kappa or lambda. Extra markers for the differential: CD10, CD43, CD79b, CD81, CD200, ROR1.

  • B-prolymphocytic leukemia was removed in WHO 5th edition. CLL with more than 15% prolymphocytes is now "prolymphocytic progression of CLL"; other cases are mantle cell lymphoma or splenic B-cell lymphoma/leukemia with prominent nucleoli (which also absorbed hairy cell leukemia variant).

  • Burkitt lymphoma: MYC rearrangement; WHO 5th edition separates EBV-positive and EBV-negative cases.
  • Classic Hodgkin lymphoma: few large Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg cells in a background of reactive immune cells; diagnosed on tissue.

PNH flow cytometry in more depth

  • Who is tested: intravascular hemolysis without another cause, aplastic anemia or other marrow failure, and thrombosis at unusual sites.
  • CD55 (decay accelerating factor) stops the C3 convertase; CD59 (membrane inhibitor of reactive lysis) stops the membrane attack complex. Without them, complement lyses the red cells.
  • FLAER is a fluorescent, nonlytic aerolysin that binds the GPI anchor itself. It is used on white cells only; red cells bind it weakly through glycophorin A.
  • Gating: red cells CD235a positive and CD45 negative; neutrophils CD15, CD10, CD33; monocytes CD64, CD33. Test at least CD59 on red cells and FLAER plus one GPI marker, or two GPI markers, for each white cell population.
  • Sensitivity: a routine (1%) assay diagnoses classic PNH; a high-sensitivity (0.01%) assay is needed to find small clones in aplastic anemia and MDS.
  • CD59 is brighter than CD55 and is the better red cell marker for separating type II from type III cells.
Exam tip If a question asks why the PNH red cell clone is smaller than the granulocyte clone, the answer is destruction of PNH red cells by hemolysis, or dilution by transfused red cells.

Flow cytometry principles and pitfalls

  • Forward scatter tracks cell size and side scatter tracks internal complexity (granularity). Populations are separated on CD45 versus side scatter; heavily granulated APL cells show high side scatter.
  • Lineage of a mixed-phenotype acute leukemia is assigned by: MPO (intensity above half of mature neutrophils) or two monocytic markers for myeloid; cytoplasmic or surface CD3 for T; strong CD19 with CD10, CD22 or CD79a for B.
  • A single antigen is rarely specific; a coordinated set (CD19, CD22 and CD10 together) is stronger evidence of B lineage than any one.
  • A B-cell clone shows only kappa or only lambda; a reactive population shows both.
