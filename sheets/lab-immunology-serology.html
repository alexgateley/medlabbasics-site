Immunoglobulins

Each immunoglobulin has two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains (kappa or lambda, never both on one molecule). The variable domains at the tips form the antigen-binding site from three CDRs on each chain; papain cuts IgG into two Fab fragments (bind antigen) and one Fc fragment (binds Fc receptors and C1q). The heavy-chain class sets the isotype.

Safety Complement is heat-labile and keeps activating in the tube. For CH50 and AH50 use serum (never EDTA plasma, which blocks the activity being measured), separate and process within about 1 hour on ice, and freeze at -80 °C (the strictest published guidance; laboratories set their own limits). Complement protein levels (C3, C4) by nephelometry survive storage, but hemolytic activity does not: an unspun tube left at room temperature for days can give an undetectable CH50 with normal C3 and C4. Reject and recollect per the procedure manual.

Try one

Immunology and Serology practice question

A patient referred for joint pain and palpable purpura of the legs has these results: Rheumatoid factor (latex agglutination) positive at 1:2,560 Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) negative Hepatitis C antibody reactive; HCV RNA detected C4 8 mg/dL (0.08 g/L) [reference 10–40 mg/dL; 0.10–0.40 g/L] Which is the MOST likely source of the rheumatoid factor?

  1. Seropositive rheumatoid arthritis early in its course
  2. Prozone in the anti-CCP immunoassay masking a positive result
  3. Heterophile antibodies agglutinating the latex particles
  4. Chronic hepatitis C infection with immune complexes

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Early seropositive rheumatoid arthritis usually carries anti-CCP, which can appear years before the arthritis; a negative anti-CCP with very high RF points away from rheumatoid arthritis.
  • B. Prozone affects agglutination reactions with antibody excess; anti-CCP is measured by solid-phase immunoassay, and nothing here suggests a false-negative reaction.
  • C. Heterophile antibodies react with animal immunoglobulins, whereas the latex test is coated with human IgG and detects antibody to its Fc region; the HCV result offers a better explanation.
  • D. Correct. Chronic hepatitis C drives very high RF titers, often with mixed cryoglobulins, purpura and low C4, while anti-CCP stays negative.

Rheumatoid factor is an antibody, usually IgM, directed against the Fc portion of IgG; latex agglutination detects it with IgG-coated particles. RF is present in about 70% of patients with rheumatoid arthritis but also in Sjögren disease, SLE, endocarditis, tuberculosis, cancer, older adults and chronic viral hepatitis, and very high titers occur in hepatitis C. Anti-CCP is about 90% specific for rheumatoid arthritis and is notably absent in hepatitis C even when RF is positive, so it separates the two. Purpura and low C4 in this patient fit immune complexes containing IgM RF, which consume the classical complement pathway.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Mosby's Diagnostic & Lab Reference, 17th ed.

Autoantibodies

ANA screening by indirect immunofluorescence on HEp-2 cells is reported as titer plus pattern (ICAP AC codes). A titer above 1:80 is found in over 95% of SLE, but healthy people are positive too: about 30% at 1:40 and about 3% at 1:320 (Merck). Positive patterns go on to antigen-specific tests.

  • Anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm are highly specific for SLE but not sensitive. Low C3 and C4 track active disease, lowest in nephritis.
  • Anti-Jo-1: polymyositis (about 30%), interstitial lung disease.
  • Rheumatoid factor (antibody to human IgG): about 70% of RA, but also Sjogren, SLE, granulomatous disease, chronic infection, cancer, about 3% of the general population and 20% of older adults; titer does not follow disease activity. Anti-CCP: specificity about 90%, sensitivity about 77-86%, and absent in hepatitis C, which can give a positive RF with joint swelling.
  • Thyroid: Hashimoto has high-titer anti-TPO (antithyroglobulin less often). Graves has TSH-receptor stimulating antibody (TSI) with suppressed TSH and high free T4 or T3; most Graves patients also have anti-TPO.

Hepatitis serology

CDC recommends the triple panel (HBsAg, anti-HBs, total anti-HBc) at least once for all adults aged 18 and over.
HBsAgTotal anti-HBcIgM anti-HBcAnti-HBsInterpretation (CDC)
+++-Acute infection
++--Chronic infection
-+not needed+Resolved infection (immune; can reactivate)
--not needed+Vaccinated (immune if over 10 mIU/mL after a complete series)
-+not needed-Isolated anti-HBc: waned anti-HBs after past infection, occult infection, passive transfer to an infant, false positive (susceptible) or an HBsAg mutant
--not needed-Susceptible, never infected
  • HBsAg means the person is infectious, except that it can be transiently positive within 30 days of a hepatitis B vaccine dose.
  • HBeAg marks viral replication and high infectivity; anti-HBe goes with lower viral load. HBV DNA is the viral load.
  • IgM anti-HBc means infection within about 6 months, but it can reappear in flares of chronic infection. Total anti-HBc persists for life and never follows vaccination.
  • Hepatitis A: IgM anti-HAV from 5-10 days before symptoms to about 6 months = acute; total anti-HAV positive with IgM negative = past infection or vaccine. Test IgM only in symptomatic patients.
  • Hepatitis C: anti-HCV first; a reactive result reflexes to HCV RNA on the same specimen. Reactive + RNA detected = current infection; reactive + RNA not detected = resolved infection or false positive. Antibody takes 8-11 weeks; RNA appears in 1-2 weeks, so test RNA after a recent exposure or in an immunocompromised patient.

Exam tip The Western blot is no longer used: it turns positive later than current immunoassays (missing acute infection, when viral RNA is at very high levels) and misclassifies most HIV-2 infections as HIV-1. A reactive step 1 with a nonreactive step 2 in a patient with fever, rash and pharyngitis is classic acute HIV: the NAT decides.

A positive TB response stands even with a low mitogen. Preferred after BCG and for people unlikely to return for a skin test reading. Neither IGRA nor TST separates latent infection from TB disease.
Exam tip MLT note: the MLT outline drops Treponema and Borrelia stages, TB/IGRA and cytokine testing; RPR and treponemal test principles stay.

Dendritic cells are the most effective antigen-presenting cells and start primary responses; class II MHC presents extracellular antigen, class I presents intracellular (for example viral) antigen.

Cytokine testing: cytokines are measured as proteins by sandwich immunoassay, one at a time (ELISA) or many at once on a multiplex bead array (capture antibodies on coded beads, a biotinylated detection antibody, then streptavidin-phycoerythrin read by a flow-based reader). Serum and plasma values for the same cytokine do not agree well, and a freeze-thaw cycle changes most cytokine concentrations, so keep one specimen type and one handling protocol for serial results and avoid repeated thawing. IGRAs are cytokine release assays: QFT-Plus measures IFN-gamma in plasma by ELISA, and T-SPOT.TB counts IFN-gamma-secreting cells as spots.

  • Initial screen: CBC with manual differential, quantitative IgG, IgA, IgM (and IgE), vaccine antibody titers. Lymphopenia means under 2000/µL at birth, under 4500/µL at 9 months, under 1000/µL in older children and adults.

  • EBV patterns: acute = VCA IgM + (VCA IgG and EA IgG usually +), EBNA negative; past = VCA IgG and EBNA IgG +, IgM negative; all negative = consider acute HIV or CMV. Reactive lymphocytes can reach about 30% of WBCs.
  • CMV: IgM seroconversion means new infection, but reactivation in immunocompromised patients causes disease too; in transplant recipients a high or rising blood viral load (PCR) or antigenemia is used, since detection alone does not prove disease. Congenital CMV: PCR of urine or saliva.
  • Rubella: IgG tests immunity (before, during and after pregnancy); IgM only for suspected cases; RT-PCR on throat or nasopharyngeal swab or urine collected as soon after onset as possible.
  • Measles (rubeola): confirm every suspected case by RT-PCR on a nasopharyngeal or throat swab (best from the first day of rash to 3 days after; still possible at 10-14 days; urine adds yield) plus serum measles IgM. IgM may not be detectable until 3 days after symptom onset, so an early negative IgM with a negative or missing RT-PCR calls for a second serum 3-10 days after onset. Genotyping is the only way to separate wild-type measles from a rash after recent vaccination.

Syphilis and TB testing detail

  • Stages: primary (painless chancre), secondary (rash including palms and soles, mucocutaneous lesions, lymphadenopathy), latent (serology only; early latent if acquired within the past year), tertiary (cardiac, gummas, tabes dorsalis, general paresis).
  • Biologic false-positive nontreponemal results (about 1-2%): acute (febrile illness, pregnancy, vaccination) or chronic (SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome, autoimmune disease, injection drug use, older age, HIV). False-positive syphilis serology occurs in 5-10% of SLE.
  • Prozone causes false-negative nontreponemal tests in about 1-2% of secondary syphilis: titrate when the clinical picture says syphilis and the undiluted RPR is nonreactive.
  • Neurosyphilis: CSF-VDRL is highly specific but insensitive (can be negative in up to half); a negative CSF FTA-ABS or TP-PA makes neurosyphilis very unlikely.
  • High EIA/CIA signal-to-cutoff ratios correlate with TP-PA positivity; cutoffs are assay-specific.
  • T-SPOT.TB (2010 criteria): positive with at least 8 spots, borderline 5-7, negative 4 or fewer (nil 10 or fewer); indeterminate if nil over 10 or mitogen under 20 spots. Processing limits (CDC, manufacturers): QFT-Plus tubes go into the 37 °C incubator within 16 hours of collection (then 16-24 hours of incubation); T-SPOT.TB blood within 8-32 hours. Advanced HIV or immunosuppression can give false negatives; a failed mitogen (positive control) response makes the result indeterminate, never negative. A negative result in a contact is repeated 8-10 weeks after the last exposure.

HLA, transplantation and tumor immunology

HLA genes are on the short arm of chromosome 6. For matching, HLA-DR matters most, then HLA-B, then HLA-A.
HLA classLociFound onPresents to
Class IHLA-A, -B, -C (beta-2 microglobulin from chromosome 15)Nearly all nucleated cellsCD8 T cells
Class IIHLA-DR, -DQ, -DPB cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, activated T cellsCD4 T cells
Kidney rejectionTimingMechanism
HyperacuteMinutes to hoursPreformed antibody to donor HLA; graft usually fails
AcceleratedDaysAntibody-mediated
AcuteDays to a weekT-cell or antibody-mediated; most episodes respond to a glucocorticoid bolus
ChronicMonths to yearsT-cell or antibody-mediated; proteinuria
  • Pretransplant testing looks for preformed antibody. PRA is the percentage of a cell panel the recipient serum reacts with (the chance of a positive crossmatch with an unrelated donor); single antigen beads name each HLA specificity and allow a virtual crossmatch. CDC crossmatch: recipient serum + donor lymphocytes + rabbit complement, lysis = donor-specific antibody; a positive CDC crossmatch contraindicates kidney transplant. The flow cytometry crossmatch is more sensitive. Pregnancy and earlier grafts sensitize; Merck advises avoiding red cell transfusion in kidney candidates when possible because it sensitizes.
  • HLA disease associations: B27 ankylosing spondylitis and reactive arthritis; DR4 rheumatoid arthritis; DQ2/DQ8 celiac disease and type 1 diabetes; B51 Behcet.
  • Graft-versus-host disease: donor T cells attack the recipient (after stem cell transplant, or transfusion-associated GVHD 4-30 days after transfusion with over 90% mortality); irradiating cellular blood components prevents TA-GVHD. See the Compatibility Testing and Blood Components page.
  • Tumor immunology: tumor-specific antigens occur only on tumor cells, tumor-associated antigens mainly on them. Cytotoxic T cells and NK cells kill tumor cells; NK cells attack cells that have lost MHC class I. Tregs and suppressive myeloid cells help tumors escape. No tumor marker is sensitive and specific enough for mass screening; markers serve diagnosis support and monitoring (see the Clinical Chemistry: Analytes page).
