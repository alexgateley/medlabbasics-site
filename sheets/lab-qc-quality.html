Exam tip Worked: control results 98, 100, 102, 99 and 101 mg/dL. Mean = 500 ÷ 5 = 100. Deviations −2, 0, +2, −1, +1; squares sum to 10; 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5; SD = √2.5 = 1.58 mg/dL. CV = 1.58 ÷ 100 × 100 = 1.6%. A result of 104 gives z = (104 − 100) ÷ 1.58 = +2.5.

Westgard multirules

Applied as in the Westgard Rules Check calculator: every rejection rule checked on every run, 1-2s reported as a warning only when nothing else fires. The calculator checks 4-1s within one level and does not check 10x; Westgard allows 4-1s and 10x to count across levels.
RuleViolated whenError typeAction
1-2sOne result beyond ±2 SDWarning onlyInspect with the other rules; do not reject on 1-2s alone
1-3sOne result beyond ±3 SDRandom (or the start of a large systematic error)Reject the run
2-2sTwo results beyond the same 2 SD limit (both above +2 or both below −2): the same level in two consecutive runs, or both levels in one runSystematicReject the run
R-4sWithin one run, one level beyond +2 SD and the other beyond −2 SD. Never across runsRandomReject the run
4-1sFour consecutive results beyond the same 1 SD limit (all above +1 or all below −1)SystematicReject the run
10xTen consecutive results on the same side of the meanSystematicReject the run
  • Use the laboratory's own mean and SD for each control lot, not the manufacturer's printed range.
  • 1-3s and R-4s point to random error: for example clots or debris in the pipette, inadequate cleaning, electronic noise or an operator error.
  • 2-2s, 4-1s and 10x point to systematic error: calibration, a new reagent or control lot, deteriorating reagent, wrong fluid levels or temperature.
  • After a rejection: stop reporting, test a fresh vial of control (rules out a spoiled vial), find and fix the cause, rerun controls, then review every patient result since the last acceptable run.
Safety Repeating the same control until it falls inside the limits is not troubleshooting. CLIA requires the laboratory to evaluate all patient results from the failed run and since the last acceptable run (42 CFR 493.1282).

Try one

Quality Control, Validation and Regulation practice question

An outpatient clinic laboratory performs a CLIA-waived visual urine hCG cassette test. Each cassette has an internal control line. A new shipment arrives with a new lot number, and a medical assistant who has completed training starts on the testing bench the same day. Which practice BEST follows good laboratory practice for waived testing before patient results are reported?

  1. Run positive and negative external controls on the new lot, performed by the newly trained operator
  2. Test two patient urines on both the old and new lots and compare the lines
  3. Rely on each cassette's internal control line, because it checks the device with each test
  4. Run external controls at the next monthly QC date already on the clinic schedule

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. CDC recommends external controls at least with each new shipment, each new lot number and each new operator before patient testing, in addition to the internal controls the insert requires.
  • B. Tempting as a lot-to-lot check, but patient urines of unknown hCG status cannot show that the new lot detects a positive. External controls of known reactivity are the recommended check.
  • C. Tempting because the internal control is built in, but it does not check the antibody reagents against known positive and negative samples. External controls are recommended with each new lot and shipment and by each new operator.
  • D. Tempting because a schedule exists, but waiting a month would release results on an unchecked lot and from an operator whose performance has not been checked with known material.

The only CLIA requirement for a certificate-of-waiver site is to follow the current manufacturer's instructions, but surveys found many waived sites skipping QC, using expired kits and keeping poor records. CDC's good laboratory practices recommend running internal controls as often as the insert specifies and external controls at least with each new shipment, each new lot number and each new operator, before patient results are reported. Results, lot numbers, expiration dates and corrective actions are documented, and patient testing stops when controls fail until the problem is resolved.

Sources: CDC MMWR: Good Laboratory Practices for Waived Testing Sites (2005)

Exam tip Method comparison: run at least 40 patient specimens across the range on both methods. With regression y = a + bx, systematic error at decision level Xc is (a + b·Xc) − Xc. The y-intercept points to constant error and a slope away from 1.00 to proportional error. r tells you whether the range was wide enough (0.99 or more), not whether the method is acceptable.

CLIA, accreditation and POCT

CategoryHow a test gets thereMain requirements
WaivedCleared for home use, or so simple and accurate that error is negligible, or no reasonable risk of harm if done wrong (examples: dipstick urinalysis, urine hCG by visual color, fecal occult blood, home-use glucose meters, spun microhematocrit)Certificate of waiver; follow the manufacturer's instructions exactly
Moderate complexity (includes PPM)FDA scores 7 criteria from 1 to 3; total 12 or lessQC, PT, verification, competency; testing personnel need at least a high school diploma with documented training
High complexityTotal score above 12; any test not yet categorizedAll of the above, stricter personnel rules (for example an associate degree in laboratory science or equivalent)
  • Agencies: FDA categorizes tests; CMS runs CLIA and certifies laboratories; CDC provides technical support, studies and the advisory committee.
  • Certificates: waiver, PPM, registration, compliance (state inspection) and accreditation (deemed status through a CMS-approved organization: CAP, The Joint Commission, COLA, AABB, A2LA, ACHC, ASHI).
  • Default QC for quantitative nonwaived tests is two levels each day of testing; qualitative tests need a positive and a negative control. IQCP is the voluntary risk-based alternative.
  • IQCP = risk assessment (specimen, test system, reagent, environment, testing personnel) + quality control plan + quality assessment. Every specialty except pathology is eligible.
  • POCT oversight: a POCT coordinator and director, operator records with certification dates, devices that lock out when QC is overdue or fails, connectivity to the LIS, and the same CLIA rules as any test of that complexity.

Errors, reporting and turnaround time

PhaseExamplesControls
Preanalytical (most errors)Wrong patient or mislabeled tube, wrong tube or additive, hemolysis, prolonged tourniquet or fist clenching (false high potassium), delayed transport, IV contaminationPositive patient identification, specimen rejection criteria, written collection procedures, delta checks
AnalyticalFailed QC, expired reagent, calibration error, result above the reportable range reported without dilution, interferenceQC and Westgard rules, calibration verification, maintenance, dilution protocols
PostanalyticalTranscription error, result sent to the wrong chart, critical value not called, delayed reportInterfaces and autoverification, critical value policy, corrected reports
  • Critical (panic) value: the laboratory must alert the person who ordered the test, or who uses the result, immediately. Laboratory policy usually adds read-back and a record of who was told and when.
  • Corrected report: promptly tell the ordering provider of the error, send a corrected report, and keep both the original and the corrected report.
  • Delta check: compares a result with the same patient's previous result; a change too large for biology suggests misidentification or another error.
  • Autoverification: rules in the LIS or middleware release results without manual review when they pass QC, flags, limit and delta checks (CLSI AUTO-10A and AUTO-15).
  • Turnaround time is right-skewed: report the median and the time by which 90% of tests are done, not the mean alone. Most delays are pre- or postanalytical.
  • Records: QC, PT, patient test records and reports at least 2 years; immunohematology and transfusion records follow the FDA blood rules in 21 CFR 606.160.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

A worked Levey-Jennings chart

A Levey-Jennings chart plots each control result against the day or run. A center line marks the mean, with lines at ±1, ±2 and ±3 SD. In control, points scatter evenly above and below the mean and about 95% fall within ±2 SD, which is why roughly 1 good run in 20 gives a 1-2s warning on a single level. A shift shows as points jumping to one side and staying there. A trend shows as points climbing or falling steadily. Random error shows as one point far out, or wider scatter. Below is a glucose level 1 control with an established mean of 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) and an SD of 2.0 mg/dL (0.11 mmol/L), so CV = 2.0%. Each call is the one the Westgard Rules Check calculator makes with this level alone.

z = (result − 100) ÷ 2.0. No single result on days 10 to 13 is even a 1-2s warning: only the multirule catches this small shift.

Exam tip Classic vs modern multirule: in the original scheme a 1-2s warning starts the check and the other rules are looked at only then. In current use (and in this app) every rejection rule is checked on every run, so a 4-1s pattern like days 10-13 is rejected even though no point passed 2 SD. If a question states its laboratory's approach, follow the question.

Why 1-2s is not a rejection rule: with one control level it rejects about 4.6% of good runs, 8.9% with two levels and 13.0% with three. A laboratory running two levels on 20 analytes will see a false 1-2s somewhere in almost every QC event. Some sources define R-4s as a range of more than 4 SD between the two levels in a run; Westgard and the calculator require one level beyond +2 SD and the other beyond −2 SD, which is the form used here.

Setting up QC and troubleshooting

  • New control lot: run it alongside the current lot. About 10 results on different days give a first mean; update it as more data come in (a floating mean) and set the SD from longer-term data that include day-to-day sources of variation.
  • Assayed controls: the manufacturer's value may be used only for your method and instrument and only after you verify it. Unassayed controls: the laboratory sets the mean and SD by running them alongside controls of known value.
  • Run controls the same way as patient specimens and rotate QC among all operators. If calibrator material is used as a control, it must come from a different lot from the one used to calibrate.
  • Run controls before resuming patient testing after a complete reagent change, major maintenance or replacement of a critical part.
  • A new reagent or control lot may shift the mean while the SD stays about the same; CLIA requires a mean and SD for each lot of control material.
  • Transient problems (inadequate cleaning, electronic noise, a clot or debris in the pipette) affect a few samples and may be gone by the next QC event. Persistent problems (calibration, reagent deterioration, wrong fluid levels, wrong temperature) stay until corrected and change either the bias or the imprecision.

Total allowable error and sigma

Total allowable error (TEa) is the largest error a result can carry without changing a medical decision. There is no single accepted set of TEa values; the laboratory director chooses one with clinicians, in order of preference from outcome studies, biological variation, then the state of the art (what current methods and peer groups achieve). CLIA's PT acceptance limits are also often used as TEa (a convention, not a CLIA rule; this page uses them for its worked example): glucose ±8% or ±6 mg/dL (whichever is greater), potassium ±0.3 mmol/L, sodium ±4 mmol/L, creatinine ±10% or ±0.2 mg/dL, total calcium ±1.0 mg/dL, total cholesterol ±10%.

Grades from Panda 2023. The 'Meaning for QC' column is a general reading of the published advice that higher sigma allows simpler QC; no source sets fixed QC rules for each band.
SigmaGradeMeaning for QC
6 or moreWorld classLow error rate; a less stringent QC strategy can be used
5 to below 6ExcellentFew errors
4 to below 5GoodAcceptable

Exam tip Worked: glucose at 100 mg/dL, TEa taken from the CLIA PT limit by choice (8% here, because 8% of 100 is 8 mg/dL, more than 6), bias 1.0%, CV 2.0%. Sigma = (8 − 1) ÷ 2 = 3.5: marginal. Total error = 1.0 + 1.96 × 2.0 = 4.9%, inside the 8% allowance, but with little margin.

Verification in practice

CharacteristicHow it is commonly shown
AccuracyTest materials of known value (calibrators, assayed controls, PT samples, previously tested patient specimens) or compare split patient samples with a method already shown to be accurate; results within the manufacturer's limits
PrecisionRepeat the same samples within a run and across days, with several operators (one may be enough on fully automated systems)
Reportable rangeSamples of known value at the lowest and highest levels the manufacturer claims (linearity materials or dilutions); report only results inside the verified range; the director decides how results above or below it are handled (for example a validated dilution)
Reference intervalShow the manufacturer's or a published interval fits the local population (the 20-subject transference check)
Analytical sensitivityLowest concentration the method can reliably detect (establishment only)
Analytical specificityMeasures only the analyte: interference (hemolysis, icterus, lipemia, drugs) and cross-reactivity (establishment only)
  • The same samples can serve accuracy, precision and reportable range at once. The technical consultant (moderate) or technical supervisor (high complexity) is responsible; the laboratory director approves the study before patient testing.
  • Tests in use before April 24, 2003 do not need verification under 493.1253.
  • A laboratory that runs one test on more than one instrument or site must compare their results twice a year.
  • Using a waived device off label (for example an arterial sample on a meter cleared only for capillary and venous blood) modifies the test: CMS treats a modified waived or moderate complexity test as uncategorized, so it becomes high complexity, and the laboratory must establish its own performance specifications and meet every high-complexity requirement, personnel included.

Regulation details

  • PT: enroll in a CMS-approved program for each regulated analyte; stay with a program at least a year; most programs send 5 samples per event, 3 events a year. The target value is usually the peer-group mean after removing results more than 3 SD out; the acceptance limit is a symmetrical ± around it.
  • After an unsatisfactory PT result the laboratory must take remedial action (training, technical assistance) and document it; a first unsuccessful performance can be met with required training or technical assistance instead of sanctions, unless patients are in immediate jeopardy.
  • PT only covers the primary method used for patients during the event; keep PT records, including the signed attestation, at least 2 years.
  • Competency is assessed by the technical consultant (moderate complexity) or technical supervisor (high complexity; may be delegated in writing to a qualified general supervisor). Supervisors and consultants also need competency assessed against their own regulatory duties.
  • Accreditation organizations must hold the laboratory to standards equal to or stricter than CLIA. Accredited laboratories still need a CLIA certificate (of accreditation), pay CLIA fees and do PT.
  • Quality assessment: CLIA requires ongoing review of preanalytic, analytic and postanalytic systems, checking whether corrective actions worked, revising procedures and discussing reviews with staff, all documented.
  • Specimen rules: written procedures for patient preparation, collection, labeling, storage, transport, processing, acceptance and rejection, and referral only to CLIA-certified laboratories; record the date and time each specimen arrives.

  • Plan-do-check-act (Deming): plan (find the problem and its causes, plan the improvement), do (put the plan into action), check (review whether it worked and revise the plan), act (take the corrective action, recheck, and start the cycle again).
  • Quality assessment draws on QC reviews, PT performance reviews, chart reviews, specimen rejection logs, turnaround time reports and complaint reports.
  • Turnaround time can be measured order to report, collection to report or receipt to report (in-lab); say which. In-lab TAT underestimates what the clinician experiences.
  • Teaching: higher Bloom's levels (apply, analyze, evaluate) need case or problem questions, not recall; an objective's verb tells you how to test it. Bench skills (psychomotor) are assessed by observation, as in competency element 1.
