Mycobacteria, Fungi, Parasites and Viruses

AFB stains and processing, Runyon groups, Nocardia, yeast and mold identification, dimorphic fungi, the O&P exam, blood parasites, viral specimens and tests, and the biosafety level for each

Checked against current sources November 2026

Biosafety at a glance (CDC BMBL 6th edition)

WorkLevel

Safety Never make a slide culture or open a fuzzy white (hyaline) mold on the open bench: it may be Coccidioides, whose airborne arthroconidia make laboratory-acquired infection a documented hazard. Most exposures happen when a culture of unknown status is handled on the open bench: seal the plate and work in a class II BSC.

Mycobacteria: stains, specimens and processing

StainHow it worksPositive looks like
Ziehl-Neelsen (hot)Carbol fuchsin driven in with heat, acid-alcohol decolorizer, methylene blue counterstainThin red bacilli on a blue background; read with oil immersion
Kinyoun (cold)Carbol fuchsin without heat; same decolorizer and counterstainAs Ziehl-Neelsen
Auramine-rhodamine (fluorochrome)Fluorescent dyes bind mycolic acids; read at low powerYellow-orange glowing bacilli on a dark field; more sensitive and faster to screen
Modified acid-fast (Kinyoun with 1-2% sulfuric acid)Weaker decolorizer for partly acid-fast organismsNocardia: beaded, branching filaments that are weakly acid-fast
  • Sputum for TB: at least 3 specimens 8-24 hours apart, at least one early morning. Smear result within 24 hours, graded 1+ to 4+.
  • A smear needs about 10,000 bacilli/mL to turn positive; culture detects about 10/mL. A negative smear does not exclude TB, and every specimen is cultured whatever the smear shows.
  • NAAT (for example Xpert MTB/RIF, which also detects rifampin-resistance mutations) on at least one respiratory specimen when TB is suspected; it does not replace smear, culture or growth-based susceptibility testing.
  • Digestion-decontamination of sputum: NALC (mucolytic) with NaOH (kills normal flora), about 15 minutes, neutralize with phosphate buffer pH 6.8, centrifuge (3000 × g for 15 minutes), smear and culture the sediment. Sterile tissue and fluids are not decontaminated.
  • Culture on solid (Lowenstein-Jensen, Middlebrook 7H10/7H11) and liquid media (MGIT); liquid is faster and more sensitive (results often in 2-3 weeks). Solid media are commonly read weekly for up to 8 weeks before a culture is called negative, and final confirmation can take longer.
Exam tip Contamination rate is the processing QC indicator: about 3-5% on solid media and up to about 7-8% in liquid (MGIT) is the usual target. A rate below about 3% with poor recovery and slower detection means the NaOH step is too harsh; a high rate means under-decontamination.

Mycobacteria: growth and identification

GroupDefining featureExamples
M. tuberculosis complexSlow (about 2 weeks and more); rough, dry, buff, cauliflower-like colonies; serpentine cords in smears; inhibited by PNB. M. tuberculosis is niacin positive, nitrate positive and heat-stable (68 °C) catalase negative; M. bovis is niacin and nitrate negativeM. tuberculosis, M. bovis (including BCG), M. africanum
Runyon I, photochromogensSlow; buff in the dark, turn yellow after light exposureM. kansasii (pulmonary), M. marinum (fish tank granuloma, grows best at 30-32 °C)
Runyon II, scotochromogensSlow; yellow-orange even in the darkM. scrofulaceum (cervical lymphadenitis in children), M. gordonae
Runyon III, nonchromogensSlow; no pigmentM. avium complex (lung disease; disseminated in advanced HIV), M. xenopi, M. ulcerans (Buruli ulcer, 30-33 °C, PCR preferred), M. haemophilum (needs hemin, 30-32 °C)
Runyon IV, rapid growersVisible colonies in under 7 daysM. abscessus complex, M. fortuitum group, M. chelonae: wounds, devices, post-procedure infections, cystic fibrosis lungs
  • Confirm MTB complex on growth by NAAT, DNA probe or MPT64 antigen; identify NTM by MALDI-TOF MS (extract and inactivate in the BSC, never spot live MTB colonies in the open), line probe assays or sequencing (16S rRNA, hsp65, rpoB).
  • Susceptibility testing on the first MTB isolate from every patient; molecular resistance testing (for example CDC's MDDR service) for smear-positive or NAAT-positive specimens with drug-resistance risk.
  • An NTM in one sputum may be colonization or contamination; MTB complex in any specimen is significant and is reported to public health.
Safety Call a new positive AFB smear, a positive MTB NAAT or an MTB complex culture to the care team and infection prevention the same day; TB is reportable to the health department.

Parasites: stool exam

  1. Collect three stools 2-3 days apart, before barium (wait 7-10 days), antibiotics (2-3 weeks) or antacids and mineral oil. One part stool to three parts preservative.
  2. Examine fresh liquid stool within 30 minutes of passage and soft stool within 1 hour, or preserve it, or trophozoites disintegrate.
  3. Concentrate a formalin portion by formalin-ethyl acetate sedimentation (500 × g for 10 minutes): best for eggs, larvae and cysts on a saline and iodine wet mount.
  4. Make a permanent stained smear (trichrome) from PVA, SAF or a one-vial fixative: best for protozoan trophozoites and cysts and the only reliable way to see Dientamoeba.
  5. Add special stains on request: modified acid-fast for Cryptosporidium, Cyclospora and Cystoisospora; chromotrope (modified trichrome) for microsporidia.

Babesia (B. microti in the US Northeast and Midwest)Normal sizeVaried, sometimes vacuolated ring forms; no pigment, no gametocytes or schizonts; dividing tetrads ('Maltese cross') and clumps outside red cells point to Babesia; PCR names the species. Spread by Ixodes ticks and by transfusion
  • Thick smear (not fixed, red cells lysed) finds the parasite; thin smear (methanol-fixed) names the species and gives the parasitemia. Giemsa stain. At least 2 of each.
  • Make smears from EDTA blood promptly; delay distorts parasites. Repeat every 8-12 hours for 2-3 days before calling malaria negative. Only one rapid antigen test is licensed in the US, and a positive or negative RDT is followed by smears.
  • Microfilariae, all sheathed: Wuchereria bancrofti (nuclei stop short of the tail tip) and Brugia malayi (gap between the last two tail nuclei) draw blood at night; Loa loa (nuclei to the tail tip, eye worm, Calabar swellings) draw 10 AM to 2 PM. Concentrate with Knott's technique (2% formalin) or a membrane filter.
  • Trypanosomes: T. cruzi (Chagas) is a C-shaped trypomastigote with a large kinetoplast in acute blood, amastigotes in tissue, and is diagnosed by two serologic tests when chronic. T. brucei has a small kinetoplast and undulating membrane; look in blood, chancre fluid, lymph node aspirate or CSF, using buffy coat concentration.
Safety Malaria is an emergency: read smears as soon as they are made, report any Plasmodium with the species (or 'P. falciparum cannot be excluded') and the percent parasitemia to the provider at once, and send for PCR when the species is uncertain.

Virology

  • Respiratory swabs: synthetic fiber (flocked) on a plastic or wire shaft, in viral transport medium. Calcium alginate and wooden shafts can inactivate virus and inhibit PCR.
  • NAAT (PCR, including multiplex panels) is the method of choice for most viruses. Rapid antigen tests are fast and specific but miss 30-50% of influenza; a negative result in a sick patient is followed by a molecular test.
  • Viral culture is now kept for surveillance, typing and special cases; it is insensitive for CSF and healing herpes lesions.

Mycobacteria: processing problems and what they mean

FindingLikely causeFix

Rapid growers or M. marinum/M. haemophilum missedNaOH kills them too (NALC-NaOH reduced M. abscessus counts by about 87% in one study); wrong temperatureMinimal alkali for skin and tissue; add 30-32 °C incubation and hemin for skin, joint and lymph node specimens
AFB seen in a slide-warmer smearHeat-fixed smears can still hold live tubercle bacilliTreat slides as infectious; fix on a slide warmer, not a flame

Fluorochrome smears are screened at low power because the glowing bacilli stand out; a positive auramine smear can be overstained or confirmed with Ziehl-Neelsen or Kinyoun. No acid-fast stain separates M. tuberculosis from NTM; cording is suggestive only, because some NTM form cords too. The MTB complex is a slow grower that is niacin positive, reduces nitrate, loses catalase activity at 68 °C and fails to grow on medium containing p-nitrobenzoic acid (NTM grow). Today most laboratories confirm the complex with a probe, NAAT, MPT64 antigen or MALDI-TOF and send isolates to the public health laboratory for susceptibility testing and genotyping.

Nocardia sits between the two worlds: it grows on routine bacterial media in a few days to 2 weeks as dry, chalky white colonies, looks like beaded branching gram-positive filaments, and is only weakly acid-fast (positive on the modified Kinyoun stain, usually negative on the full-strength AFB stain). Patients at risk include transplant recipients, people on high-dose steroids and people with chronic lung disease; brain abscesses occur in roughly a third of cases, and up to 40% of patients have no known immune defect. Identification is by molecular methods such as sequencing; MALDI-TOF helps in some laboratories.

Reliable identification of C. auris needs MALDI-TOF MS with an FDA-cleared library that includes it (Bruker Biotyper CA System or bioMérieux VITEK MS IVD v3.2 or later), sequencing of the D1-D2 or ITS region, or a blood culture panel that targets it. On chromogenic agar its colonies are usually white to pink, so colour alone cannot identify it. A rise in unidentified yeasts or in Candida from urine on one unit is itself a clue to transmission.

The germ tube test puts a light inoculum in 0.5 mL of serum at 37 °C and reads it after 2-3 hours: a germ tube is a hyphal outgrowth from the yeast cell. It separates C. albicans and C. dubliniensis from other Candida, but false positives and negatives occur, so most laboratories now confirm by chromogenic agar, MALDI-TOF or an automated card. Yeast from sputum, urine, skin or stool is often colonization; yeast in blood, CSF or another sterile site is always significant and is a critical result. Cryptococcus differs from Candida by its capsule, positive urease, melanin on niger seed agar and the capsular antigen; MALDI-TOF separates C. neoformans from C. gattii.

Molds: species detail and specimen handling

AspergillusColonyConidial headNote

A. flavusYellow-greenRadiate; uniseriate and biseriateSecond most common; sinusitis in warm, dry climates
A. nigerBlackRadiate; biseriateOtomycosis

  • Aspergillus in sputum may be colonization; it matters most in neutropenic or immunosuppressed patients or with compatible imaging. Galactomannan is more sensitive in BAL fluid than in serum; blood cultures are almost always negative.
  • Mucorales: send tissue fresh and promptly, do not refrigerate it, cut it into small pieces and lay them on the agar. Grinding, homogenizing and vortexing kill the fragile hyphae. Many labs incubate at 30 °C and 37 °C.
  • Dermatophytes: scrape the active edge of a skin lesion; pluck hairs. Anthropophilic species (T. rubrum, T. tonsurans, E. floccosum) are mostly endothrix in hair; zoophilic and geophilic ones are mostly ectothrix. T. mentagrophytes and T. interdigitale are urease positive and perforate hair; T. rubrum and the terbinafine-resistant T. indotineae are usually negative on both, and T. indotineae needs sequencing.
  • Dimorphic fungi grow slowly at 25-30 °C (Histoplasma can take 6-12 weeks, Coccidioides 2 days to several weeks). Yeast-like cells in tissue can be confused with each other: small intracellular Histoplasma with Talaromyces and Leishmania; Blastomyces with Cryptococcus; Coccidioides endospores with small yeasts.

A kit with only the LV-PVA vial filled cannot give a concentrate, a modified acid-fast stain or a Giardia/Cryptosporidium antigen test: ask for a formalin specimen. Cryptosporidium antigen kits work on fresh, frozen, formalin or SAF stool but not on concentrated or PVA stool, and immunofluorescence (DFA) is highly sensitive and specific. E. histolytica cannot be told from E. dispar by its shape, and ingested red cells are not a reliable sign; an E. histolytica-specific antigen test or PCR is needed. Several specimens are needed before Cryptosporidium or Cyclospora is called negative because shedding is intermittent.

Strongyloides can autoinfect, so a patient on high-dose corticosteroids can develop hyperinfection with larvae in sputum. Agar plate culture is the most sensitive stool method; serology (EIA) is useful but stays positive after past infection. Viral test interpretation

  • Rapid influenza antigen tests: sensitivity about 50-70% (higher with a reader), specificity 98% or more. Positive predictive value falls when influenza is rare (start and end of season, summer); negative predictive value falls when it is common. Hospitalized patients should have a molecular test.
  • Genital HSV: lesion NAAT is about 91-100% sensitive; culture loses sensitivity as lesions heal. Type-specific glycoprotein G serology helps when lesions are absent; IgM testing is not recommended, and low-index EIA positives need confirmation.
  • Viral meningitis: CSF shows lymphocytes, mildly raised protein and normal or slightly low glucose, a pattern that cannot by itself exclude bacterial meningitis. Multiplex CSF panels detect several viruses at once.
  • Measles: genotyping is the only way to tell wild-type infection from a rash after recent vaccination.
  • Biosafety: cytomegalovirus is a particular risk to pregnant staff. A suspected 'herpes simplex' lesion after a macaque bite or scratch may be B virus, and the laboratory must be told before testing.
