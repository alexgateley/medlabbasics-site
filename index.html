Laboratory Operations

The hematology laboratory prepares 0.9% (w/v) sodium chloride as a diluent for manual cell counts. Using a molar mass of 58.44 g/mol for NaCl, what is the molar concentration of this solution?

  1. 0.154 mol/L
  2. 1.54 mol/L
  3. 0.308 mol/L
  4. 0.0154 mol/L

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. 0.9% w/v is 0.9 g per 100 mL, or 9 g/L; 9 g/L / 58.44 g/mol = 0.154 mol/L (154 mmol/L).
  • B. This reads 0.9% as 90 g/L, a tenfold overestimate from shifting the decimal the wrong way.
  • C. Doubling for the two ions gives the ideal osmolarity, about 308 mOsm/L, not the molar concentration of NaCl.
  • D. This reads 0.9% as 0.9 g per liter instead of per 100 mL, which underestimates the concentration tenfold.

Percent weight per volume means grams of solute per 100 mL of solution, so 0.9% w/v NaCl holds 0.9 g in 100 mL, or 9 g in 1 L. Molarity is moles of solute per liter: 9 g/L divided by the molar mass of 58.44 g/mol gives 0.154 mol/L, which is 154 mmol/L of sodium and 154 mmol/L of chloride. Because each formula unit dissociates into two particles, the ideal osmolarity is about 308 mOsm/L, close to plasma, which is why 0.9% saline keeps red and white cells intact during manual counts and dilutions. Writing units at every step (g/100 mL, then g/L, then mol/L) prevents the tenfold slips.

Microbiology

A patient returns from travel with two weeks of watery diarrhea and fatigue. A modified acid-fast smear of formalin-preserved stool shows round oocysts measuring 8 to 10 µm by ocular micrometer. Some stain deep red, others pale pink, and a few are unstained with a wrinkled outline. On a wet mount under ultraviolet light, the same oocysts fluoresce blue. Which organism is MOST likely?

  1. Cystoisospora belli
  2. Cyclospora cayetanensis
  3. Cryptosporidium parvum
  4. Enterocytozoon bieneusi

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Cystoisospora oocysts are acid-fast but are elongated ellipsoids well over 20 µm long, not round 8 to 10 µm forms.
  • B. Correct. Round 7.5 to 10 µm oocysts with variable modified acid-fast staining, from deep red to unstained ghosts, and blue autofluorescence under ultraviolet light are typical of Cyclospora.
  • C. Cryptosporidium oocysts are also acid-fast, which makes this the near-miss, but they measure about 4 to 6 µm, half the size of these oocysts.
  • D. Microsporidial spores are only about 1 to 2 µm and are detected with modified trichrome (chromotrope) stains, not as red oocysts on an acid-fast smear.

The modified acid-fast stain uses a weaker decolorizer so that coccidian oocysts keep carbol fuchsin and stand out against a blue-green counterstain. Size is the key measurement: Cryptosporidium oocysts are about 4 to 6 µm, Cyclospora 7.5 to 10 µm, and Cystoisospora much larger and ellipsoidal. Cyclospora takes the stain unevenly, so some oocysts look like unstained ghosts, and its oocysts autofluoresce under ultraviolet microscopy, a quick confirming clue. Formalin- or SAF-preserved stool is suitable for this stain, whereas low-viscosity PVA is not.

Hematology

While reviewing a bone marrow aspirate on Wright stain, a laboratory scientist sees an erythroid precursor about 8 to 10 µm across. Its cytoplasm is pink-orange, close to the color of a mature red cell with a faint bluish tinge, and its nucleus is small, round, very dense and eccentric, with no visible chromatin pattern. Which stage is this cell?

  1. Reticulocyte
  2. Orthochromic normoblast
  3. Basophilic normoblast
  4. Polychromatic normoblast

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. On Wright stain a reticulocyte looks like a slightly large bluish-gray red cell (polychromasia), and it has no nucleus; this cell still carries a dense nucleus.
  • B. Correct. Near-mature hemoglobinized cytoplasm with a small pyknotic nucleus is the last nucleated stage; the cell no longer divides and next extrudes its nucleus to become a reticulocyte.
  • C. The basophilic normoblast has deep blue cytoplasm from abundant ribosomes and a larger nucleus with coarsening but still visible chromatin; it lacks the pink-orange color of a hemoglobin-filled cell.
  • D. Tempting because some bluish tinge remains, but the polychromatic stage has distinctly gray-blue to muddy cytoplasm and a larger nucleus with a checkerboard chromatin pattern, and it can still divide.

Erythroid precursors mature from pronormoblast to basophilic, polychromatic and orthochromic normoblasts, then reticulocyte and mature red cell. Along the way the cell shrinks, the nucleus condenses, and the cytoplasm moves from deep blue (ribosomal RNA) through gray-blue (RNA plus hemoglobin) to pink-orange (hemoglobin). Mitosis stops at about the polychromatic stage, so the orthochromic cell can only mature, not divide. The orthochromic normoblast is the last nucleated stage: its pyknotic nucleus is extruded and taken up by marrow macrophages, leaving a reticulocyte that still holds some RNA for one to two days.

Urinalysis and Body Fluids

On the night shift, an inpatient's routine urinalysis shows:
Specific gravity 1.012, pH 6.0
Reagent strip blood negative
Reagent strip leukocyte esterase negative
Reagent strip glucose negative
Microscopic: RBC 20-30/HPF, WBC 15-20/HPF
The same morning, serum glucose was 260 mg/dL (14.4 mmol/L). The medication list includes high-dose oral vitamin C. Which explanation BEST accounts for the discrepancies?

  1. Yeast cells counted as red cells on microscopy
  2. High specific gravity crenating cells and slowing the reactions
  3. Myoglobin rather than hemoglobin in the specimen
  4. High urinary ascorbic acid inhibiting the peroxidase pads

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Yeast can be mistaken for red cells, but that would not explain the negative leukocyte esterase with many white cells or the negative glucose with serum glucose of 260 mg/dL.
  • B. Concentrated urine can depress the blood, glucose and leukocyte esterase pads, which makes this the near-miss, but the specific gravity here is 1.012.
  • C. Myoglobin reacts on the blood pad just as hemoglobin does, so it would make the blood pad positive, not negative, and it does not explain the other two pads.
  • D. Correct. Ascorbic acid is a strong reducing agent that consumes the peroxide or oxidized chromogen in the blood and glucose pads and can suppress the leukocyte esterase reaction, giving falsely negative results while cells remain visible.

Several reagent strip pads end in an oxidation step: the blood pad uses the peroxidase-like activity of heme to oxidize a chromogen, the glucose pad uses glucose oxidase and peroxidase, and the leukocyte esterase and nitrite reactions are also vulnerable to reducing agents. Ascorbic acid in the urine reduces the oxidized chromogen or the peroxide, so the color does not develop. The tell is a negative pad alongside microscopic or serum evidence that the analyte is present. A serum glucose of 260 mg/dL exceeds the renal threshold of about 180 mg/dL, so glycosuria is expected. The laboratory reports with a comment or retests a specimen collected after vitamin C is stopped.
